Chapter 4 focus: Moral judgment is systematically asymmetric: people detect hypocrisy, selfishness, and aggression in others while rapidly producing justifications for themselves. Conflict becomes more tractable when attention is deliberately redirected toward one's own contribution.

Core claim: The most useful first move in moral conflict is to search for a specific fault in one's own conduct before prosecuting the other side.

Central concept (inner lawyer):
  Reasoning often behaves like an advocate hired after an intuitive verdict, gathering evidence for the preferred side and explaining away inconvenient facts.
  Why it matters: Recognizing advocacy as a default makes self-scrutiny an active practice instead of an assumption that good intentions guarantee accurate judgment.

Key claims:
- Moral hypocrisy seeks the appearance of fairness without accepting its personal cost.
- People demand stronger evidence for opposing conclusions than for conclusions they already favor.
- Intentions dominate self-judgment while visible consequences dominate judgments of others.
- Perpetrators and victims construct different narratives of the same harm, especially about provocation and motive.
- The myth of pure evil exaggerates villains' unity, cruelty, and lack of perspective, making real conflict harder to understand.
- Debiasing is more effective when a person is forced to examine personal behavior than when given a generic warning about bias.

Named examples:
- C. Daniel Batson / moral-hypocrisy task: Participants could assign a desirable task to themselves or another person and were offered a coin as a fair allocation device. Many who used the coin still ended with the desirable task while preserving a favorable view of their morality. Verified anchors: C. Daniel Batson; desirable and undesirable task assignment. (teaches: A fairness procedure can be recruited to protect self-image without producing fair behavior.)
- Deanna Kuhn and David Perkins / argument evaluation: Research on reasoning about public issues found that people readily generate supporting considerations for their own position and struggle to produce or evaluate counterevidence. Intelligence alone does not remove the directional pull of prior belief. Verified anchors: Deanna Kuhn; David Perkins. (teaches: Reasoning skill can strengthen a case without making the search for evidence evenhanded.)
- Roy Baumeister / myth of pure evil: Comparisons of victim and perpetrator accounts reveal recurring narrative differences. Victims emphasize innocent suffering and lasting damage; perpetrators minimize scope, cite provocation, and view the event as bounded rather than as proof of a wholly evil identity. Verified anchors: Roy F. Baumeister; Evil: Inside Human Violence and Cruelty published in 1997. (teaches: Understanding an offender's causal story is not exoneration; it is necessary for diagnosing real conflict.)
- David Burns / anger inventory: Cognitive-therapy exercises for anger ask a person to reconstruct a conflict, identify distorted appraisals, and name a concrete personal contribution. The emotional shift begins when the self-protective account is made specific enough to test. Verified anchors: David D. Burns; Feeling Good cognitive-therapy exercises. (teaches: Self-criticism becomes constructive when it identifies one revisable act rather than declaring the whole self guilty.)

Hard edge / typical misreading:
  Looking for one's own fault is not moral equivalence, surrender, or an instruction to ignore abuse. The move is epistemic: recover omitted information about personal causation so that boundaries and repair rest on a less distorted account.

Paraphrase notes:
Moral perception offers high-resolution images of other people's faults and flattering compression of one's own. C. Daniel Batson's allocation studies show the pattern under controlled conditions. Participants knew that a desirable and an undesirable task had to be divided and could use a coin to appear fair, yet a fairness device did not reliably make outcomes fair. The useful distinction is between wanting to be moral and wanting to appear moral to oneself. Deanna Kuhn and David Perkins reveal a parallel pattern in argument. People search readily for reasons supporting an existing view and apply more demanding standards to opposition; intellectual skill can produce a better advocate rather than a neutral judge. Roy Baumeister extends the asymmetry to stories of harm. Victims remember innocence, magnitude, and lasting consequences. Perpetrators highlight provocation, limited intent, and a brief episode. The familiar pure-evil narrative turns one side into a unified monster, which can support condemnation but often blocks causal understanding and conflict reduction. None of this requires treating both sides as equally responsible. The central intervention is narrower: before prosecuting the other person, locate one concrete way personal behavior was insensitive, hurtful, or inconsistent with a stated principle. David Burns's cognitive exercises make that search testable by writing down anger-producing interpretations and examining them. Expect immediate counterargument from the inner lawyer. The point is not global shame and not surrender of a legitimate boundary. It is to restore omitted causal information. A person who can see one personal contribution gains a new lever for apology, changed behavior, or a clearer request. Moral humility is therefore a method of better perception, not a declaration that wrongdoing is unreal.
