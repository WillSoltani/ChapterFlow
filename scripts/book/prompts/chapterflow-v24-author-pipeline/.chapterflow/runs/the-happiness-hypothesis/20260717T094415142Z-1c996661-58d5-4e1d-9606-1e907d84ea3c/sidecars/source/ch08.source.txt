Chapter 8 focus: Virtue traditions trained perception, emotion, and habit through models and repeated practice, whereas modern rule-centered ethics often asks reason to decide each act. Flourishing depends on developing reliable excellences while preserving critical scrutiny of inherited norms.

Core claim: Virtue becomes rewarding when communities and repeated practice train good action into character rather than leaving morality as abstract rule compliance.

Central concept (virtue as trained excellence):
  Virtue is a cultivated disposition to perceive, feel, and act well in recurring situations. It is broader than niceness and closer to excellence of character expressed in practice.
  Why it matters: Moral development can focus on exemplars, environments, and rehearsal that shape automatic response, while rules remain available to challenge prejudice and harmful tradition.

Key claims:
- The Greek arete concerns excellence in fulfilling a human capacity, not only compliance with prohibitions.
- Aristotelian virtue aims at a context-sensitive mean between excess and deficiency rather than bland moderation in everything.
- Stories, exemplars, maxims, and repeated practice train emotional perception more directly than abstract argument alone.
- Benjamin Franklin turned thirteen virtues into a weekly practice and daily error record.
- Kantian duty and Benthamite consequence calculation brought important impartiality while narrowing morality toward rules and decisions.
- The VIA classification offers a modern vocabulary of twenty-four strengths grouped under six broad virtues, but its grouping remains a model rather than a discovered natural law.

Named examples:
- Benjamin Franklin / thirteen-virtue chart: Franklin listed thirteen virtues, focused on one each week, and used a grid with days as columns to mark faults. Cycling through thirteen weeks allowed four complete rounds in a year, turning moral aspiration into repeated observation and practice. Verified anchors: thirteen named virtues; one virtue emphasized each week. (teaches: Character changes through tracked rehearsal and environmental structure, not admiration of a list alone.)
- Aristotle / virtue and the mean: Aristotle treated flourishing as activity that expresses cultivated excellence across a complete life. Courage, for example, responds appropriately to danger between rash excess and cowardly deficiency; the mean is relative to circumstance and practical judgment. Verified anchors: Nicomachean Ethics; eudaimonia as flourishing across a complete life. (teaches: Virtue is skilled calibration in action, not mechanical averaging or obedience to one formula.)
- Immanuel Kant and Jeremy Bentham / modern rule ethics: Kant centered duty and universalizable principle, while Bentham evaluated consequences through aggregate welfare. Both traditions supply powerful checks on favoritism, yet each can make moral life look primarily like solving explicit dilemmas rather than forming perception and character. Verified anchors: Kant's categorical imperative; Bentham's utilitarian calculus. (teaches: Rules and outcome tests are safeguards, but they do not replace the cultivation of automatic moral skill.)
- Christopher Peterson and Martin Seligman / VIA classification: The 2004 Character Strengths and Virtues project catalogued twenty-four measurable strengths under six broad virtue families after reviewing many cultural and historical traditions. It was designed as a positive counterpart to diagnostic classification. Verified anchors: Character Strengths and Virtues published in 2004; twenty-four character strengths. (teaches: A shared vocabulary can widen virtue beyond obedience while leaving room to debate how strengths cluster and conflict.)

Hard edge / typical misreading:
  Reviving virtue is not a license to restore every inherited norm or let communities suppress dissent. Rules and rights remain essential correctives; the target is integration, with principled criticism guiding the same habits and emotions that make good conduct reliable.

Paraphrase notes:
Virtue once meant trained excellence of the whole person, not merely avoiding prohibited acts. Aristotle's arete joins perception, emotion, choice, and practice. Courage sits between rashness and cowardice, but the mean is not an arithmetic midpoint; practical wisdom judges what response fits a particular danger. Stories, admired models, maxims, and repeated action teach the automatic system what to notice and desire. Benjamin Franklin made that training concrete. He listed thirteen virtues, emphasized one each week, and marked daily faults in a seven-column grid. A thirteen-week sequence could run four times per year. The chart did not produce perfection, but it converted aspiration into feedback and rehearsal. Modern ethical theory supplied indispensable checks while changing the center of gravity. Immanuel Kant asks whether a maxim can be universalized, restricting self-serving exceptions. Jeremy Bentham asks about aggregate consequences, forcing attention beyond custom and private loyalty. Both can protect outsiders, yet moral life becomes thin if it is imagined only as a series of explicit puzzles solved by the rider. Christopher Peterson and Martin Seligman's 2004 VIA classification tries to recover a richer vocabulary: twenty-four character strengths organized under wisdom, courage, humanity, justice, temperance, and transcendence. The taxonomy is debatable, not a periodic table of character, but it restores distinctions lost when virtue shrinks to being nice. The workable synthesis keeps rights and consequence tests as correctives while rebuilding the environments that train good automatic responses. Character is social infrastructure plus repeated practice, not a trait produced by reading rules once.
