Chapter 3 focus: Human cooperation is stabilized by a strong obligation to return benefits, punish exploitation, and track reputations through language. The same machinery that supports trust can be manipulated by gifts, concessions, and strategically created debts.

Core claim: Reciprocity works as a social accounting system whose positive and punitive sides make large-scale cooperation possible while creating predictable openings for influence.

Central concept (reciprocity ledger):
  People remember favors and harms, feel pressure to repay them, and share reputation information with others. The ledger is emotional and social rather than an exact calculation.
  Why it matters: A reader can build trust through genuine exchange, recognize manufactured obligation, and address conflicts before retaliation becomes a self-sustaining cycle.

Key claims:
- Reciprocity extends the gains of cooperation because help can be returned later rather than exchanged simultaneously.
- Punitive reciprocity deters cheating, but it can escalate when each side treats its own retaliation as justified repayment.
- Gossip distributes reputational information beyond direct partners and helps groups identify reliable cooperators.
- Unsolicited gifts can create a felt debt even when the receiver did not request or value them.
- A retreat from a large request to a smaller one can be experienced as a concession that calls for a reciprocal concession.
- Human ultrasociality depends on shared norms and reputation, not kindness alone.

Named examples:
- Dennis Regan / Coca-Cola reciprocity experiment: A confederate brought some participants an unsolicited Coca-Cola during an art-rating task and later sold raffle tickets. Participants who had received the drink bought more tickets, even when their reported liking for the confederate was low. Verified anchors: Dennis T. Regan's 1971 experiment; unsolicited bottle of Coca-Cola. (teaches: A small unasked-for benefit can create repayment pressure independent of affection.)
- Robert Cialdini / Hare Krishna flower tactic: Fundraisers placed a flower or other small gift into a passerby's hand before asking for a donation. Refusing or discarding the gift did not necessarily erase the social discomfort created by receiving first. Verified anchors: Hare Krishna airport fundraising; flower given before donation request. (teaches: Reciprocity can be triggered before consent, so the defensive move is to reclassify a manipulative gift rather than feel bound by it.)
- Robert Axelrod / iterated Prisoner's Dilemma tournaments: Computer strategies competed across repeated rounds of a cooperation dilemma. Anatol Rapoport's Tit for Tat began cooperatively and then copied the other strategy's preceding move, showing how conditional cooperation can be firm without initiating betrayal. Verified anchors: Robert Axelrod's iterated Prisoner's Dilemma tournaments; Anatol Rapoport's Tit for Tat strategy. (teaches: Stable exchange rewards a cooperative opening, quick response to defection, and readiness to resume cooperation.)
- Robin Dunbar / gossip as social grooming: Research on primate group size and human conversation connects language with the work of maintaining alliances and tracking reputations in larger networks. Talk about social conduct can spread information that direct observation alone cannot provide. Verified anchors: Robin Dunbar's social-brain research; primate grooming compared with human conversation. (teaches: Gossip is not only idle cruelty; it can enforce norms, though it also carries distortion and exclusion risks.)

Hard edge / typical misreading:
  The Golden Rule is not a claim that niceness alone sustains cooperation. Human exchange includes gratitude, suspicion, gossip, and punishment; ignoring the punitive side leaves both manipulation and escalating revenge unexplained.

Voice cues observed in this chapter:
- moves from a cross-cultural moral rule to experimental influence tactics
- pairs cooperative reciprocity with vengeance rather than treating them separately
- uses strategic games to expose the structure beneath everyday favors
- ends with practical defenses against manufactured obligation

Paraphrase notes:
Reciprocity converts a one-time benefit into the possibility of a continuing relationship. A favor can be returned later, so two people gain without settling every exchange immediately. The same memory system also tracks injury. Gratitude and vengeance are paired solutions to the problem of finding cooperators and discouraging exploitation. Dennis Regan's Coca-Cola experiment isolates the felt debt: a confederate supplied an unsolicited drink and later requested raffle-ticket purchases; recipients bought more even when liking did not account for the difference. Robert Cialdini's account of Hare Krishna airport fundraising shows the mechanism outside the laboratory. A flower placed in someone's hand before a donation request creates an exchange sequence before the target has agreed to participate. The defense is not blanket ingratitude but accurate classification: a sales device need not be entered in the same moral ledger as genuine generosity. Repeated games expose the strategic structure. Anatol Rapoport's Tit for Tat opened cooperatively, answered a defection on the next move, and resumed cooperation immediately after the counterpart did. It is firm without being first to betray and forgiving without ignoring exploitation. Real groups add gossip. Robin Dunbar's social-brain work treats conversation partly as a scalable way to maintain alliances and exchange reputation information when direct observation is impossible. Gossip can therefore police norms, although distortion and exclusion make it dangerous. These mechanisms explain both large-scale cooperation and feud. Each party in a conflict remembers the other's offense more vividly, discounts its own provocation, and experiences retaliation as restoration. The Golden Rule becomes psychologically serious only when joined to reputation, boundary setting, and a way to interrupt negative reciprocity.
