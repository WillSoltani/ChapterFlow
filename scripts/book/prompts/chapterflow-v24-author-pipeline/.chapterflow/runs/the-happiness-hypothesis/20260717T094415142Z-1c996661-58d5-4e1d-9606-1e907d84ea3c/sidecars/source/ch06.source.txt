Chapter 6 focus: Attachment is an evolved system for proximity, comfort, safety, and exploration that begins in caregiving relationships and can transfer to adult partners. Passionate attraction can initiate a bond, but companionate attachment and caregiving sustain it.

Core claim: Love becomes more intelligible when attraction, attachment, and caregiving are treated as interacting systems with different functions and time courses.

Central concept (attachment bond):
  An attachment figure is someone whose presence is sought, whose absence causes distress, who provides comfort under threat, and who supports confident exploration.
  Why it matters: Romantic intensity no longer has to carry the whole meaning of love; a reader can evaluate whether a relationship develops trust, secure reliance, and mutual care.

Key claims:
- Infant attachment cannot be reduced to food reinforcement because contact comfort has motivational value of its own.
- Bowlby's attachment theory framed proximity to a caregiver as an evolved survival system rather than infant dependency to be extinguished.
- Attachment figures provide proximity maintenance, separation distress, safe haven, and secure base.
- Peers gradually take over attachment functions from parents, with romantic partners able to become full attachment figures by late adolescence and adulthood.
- Passionate love and companionate love overlap but are not the same psychological state.
- Durable bonds impose constraints, yet those ties can create security, meaning, and a platform for exploration.

Named examples:
- John Bowlby / Maternal Care and Mental Health: Working for the World Health Organization, Bowlby reviewed the effects of prolonged caregiver separation and institutional care. His 1951 report helped shift attention from feeding schedules toward the developmental importance of a continuous, responsive relationship, though later attachment research refined the report's maternal focus. Verified anchors: John Bowlby; World Health Organization commission. (teaches: Attachment is a functional need for reliable care, not indulgence created by feeding.)
- Harry Harlow / cloth and wire surrogate mothers: Eight newborn rhesus monkeys received access to a cloth-covered surrogate and a wire surrogate, with milk location reversed across groups. They spent far more time clinging to the cloth surrogate even when the wire surrogate supplied milk and ran to the cloth figure when frightened. Verified anchors: eight newborn rhesus monkeys; cloth-covered surrogate mother. (teaches: Contact comfort and felt security cannot be explained as secondary by-products of feeding alone.)
- Cindy Hazan and Phillip Shaver / adult romantic attachment: Hazan and Shaver extended attachment concepts to adult romantic relationships, first through a newspaper questionnaire and later research. Secure, anxious, and avoidant patterns shaped expectations about trust, closeness, jealousy, and separation. Verified anchors: Cindy Hazan and Phillip Shaver; 1987 romantic-attachment study. (teaches: Adult romance recruits the same broad security system without making adult partners identical to parents.)
- Hazan and colleagues / transfer of attachment functions: A survey spanning ages six through eighty-two asked whom people preferred nearby, sought when upset, and used as a base for exploration. Peers increasingly gained attachment roles through childhood and adolescence, with romantic partners able to satisfy all four functions later. Verified anchors: participants ages six through eighty-two; four attachment functions. (teaches: Attachment reorganizes across development rather than simply disappearing after childhood.)

Hard edge / typical misreading:
  Attachment theory does not say one biological mother determines an unchangeable destiny, and Harlow's monkeys do not justify direct one-to-one claims about human romance. The evidence identifies a security system whose expression is shaped by multiple caregivers, development, and later relationships.

Voice cues observed in this chapter:
- begins with isolation and the need for human contact
- uses biographies of Bowlby and Harlow to overturn older theories
- moves from infant care to adult romance through one four-part definition
- contrasts passionate ignition with companionate durability

Forbidden leakage (concepts that belong to later chapters):

- Durkheimian social integration as the meaning of life

Paraphrase notes:
Love becomes less mysterious when attachment is separated from appetite and romantic intensity. John Bowlby's work for the World Health Organization treated prolonged caregiver separation and impersonal institutional care as developmental problems, leading to the 1951 report Maternal Care and Mental Health. The early maternal emphasis was later broadened, but the durable mechanism is reliable protection from a responsive caregiver. Harry Harlow's rhesus studies challenged feeding-based explanations directly. Eight newborn monkeys had access to cloth and wire surrogates, with the milk source reversed across groups. Contact followed the cloth figure far more than nourishment did, and frightened infants used the cloth surrogate as a haven. The experiment was ethically troubling and does not map mechanically onto people, yet it isolates contact comfort as a motive. Attachment has four observable functions: maintaining proximity, protesting separation, seeking a safe haven under distress, and using security as a base for exploration. Cindy Hazan and Phillip Shaver extended that functional pattern to adult romance in 1987. Later work across ages six through eighty-two traced a gradual transfer: peers first become preferred companions, then sources of comfort, and eventually romantic partners may fill all four roles. Passionate love is therefore not the whole bond. High arousal and idealization can initiate commitment, while companionate love grows through trust, knowledge, and reciprocal care. Constraints imposed by a close bond are not automatically a loss of freedom; a secure base can expand exploration. The main caution is against destiny stories. One caregiver, one early classification, or one intense romance does not fix a person forever. Attachment expectations can persist, but later reliable relationships also supply new learning.
