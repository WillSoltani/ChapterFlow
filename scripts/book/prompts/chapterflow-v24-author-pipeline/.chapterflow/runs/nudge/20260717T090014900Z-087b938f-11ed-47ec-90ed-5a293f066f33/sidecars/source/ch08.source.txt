Chapter 8 focus: Friction can prevent people from obtaining outcomes they want, whether by accident or deliberate exploitation. Sludge audits expose time, paperwork, confusion, and asymmetric processes as real costs.

Core claim: Administrative burden should be measured and justified because small frictions can operate like a wall.

Central concept (sludge):
  Friction in a choice environment that makes it harder for people to reach an outcome that would improve their welfare by their own standards.
  Why it matters: Time, confusion, and paperwork change take-up even when formal eligibility or available options remain unchanged.

Key claims:
- Sludge can be intentional or the residue of neglected design.
- Join-easy and leave-hard asymmetry traps subscribers.
- Rebate sellers profit from overoptimism about future paperwork.
- Shrouded attributes hide the full ownership cost.
- Administrative burden disproportionately excludes people with fewer resources.
- Government should count compliance time as a policy cost.
- A sludge audit separates necessary safeguards from needless friction.

Named examples:
- Robert Letzler and Joshua Tasoff / rebate redemption experiment: Participants expected to redeem mail-in forms far more often than they actually did. Expected redemption exceeded actual redemption by 49 percentage points, and simplifying the task reduced the gap. (teaches: Firms can profit when people underestimate the future cost of paperwork.)
- University of Michigan / HAIL Scholarship: The HAIL experiment sent high-achieving, low-income Michigan students an early, simple guarantee of four years of tuition and fees if admitted. The commitment reduced uncertainty and application burden before the normal aid process. (teaches: Removing procedural uncertainty can unlock an opportunity without changing academic eligibility.)
- US federal paperwork burden: Federal information-collection estimates placed the annual public paperwork burden near eleven billion hours. The total includes compliance by households, businesses, health workers, students, and benefit applicants. (teaches: Administrative time is a policy cost large enough to require explicit accounting.)

Hard edge / typical misreading:
  Not every friction is sludge. Identity checks, deliberation pauses, and safety reviews may prevent fraud or harm; the audit asks whether the burden is necessary, proportionate, and placed on the right party.

Paraphrase notes:
Sludge names friction that blocks an outcome people want by their own standards. It can arise from neglect, but its sharpest forms are asymmetric: a subscription takes one click to start and a phone call to cancel; a headline price is effortless to see while the cost of printer ink is buried. Robert Letzler and Joshua Tasoff quantified the rebate version. Participants' expected mail-in redemption exceeded actual completion by 49 percentage points, and simplification cut overoptimism roughly in half. Sellers can advertise the rebate to everyone while paying only those who survive the later task. Public sludge can be just as consequential. The University of Michigan's HAIL Scholarship sent high-achieving, low-income state residents an early four-year tuition-and-fee guarantee conditional on admission, reducing uncertainty and process burden before applications were due. Federal paperwork estimates near eleven billion hours per year show why a free form is not free: households, firms, clinicians, students, and benefit applicants all spend time satisfying it. A sludge audit therefore counts steps, waiting, search, documentation, confusion, and anxiety. It also asks who benefits from each burden. The goal is not frictionless administration at any cost. Verification can protect safety, privacy, and program integrity. The test is whether a burden is necessary and proportionate, whether information already held can be prefilled, and whether joining and leaving receive comparable treatment. Grounding anchors: expected redemption exceeded actual redemption by 49 percentage points; simplification reduced overoptimism by about half; targeted high-achieving low-income Michigan residents; approximately eleven billion hours per year; federal information-collection and paperwork requirements.
