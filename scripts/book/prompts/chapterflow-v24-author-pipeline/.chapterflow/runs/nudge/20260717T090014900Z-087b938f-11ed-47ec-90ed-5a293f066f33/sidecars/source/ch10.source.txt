Chapter 10 focus: Sweden's premium-pension system shows how an initial campaign for active choice can shape portfolios for years. Too many funds, promotional advertising, and inertia made early decisions persist after attention disappeared.

Core claim: A one-time nudge can have durable consequences when later review is rare and switching is not actively prompted.

Central concept (persistent choice architecture):
  An initial default or active-choice campaign continues to affect outcomes because people seldom revisit the decision.
  Why it matters: Architects must judge a design by long-run portfolio quality and review behavior, not only first-day engagement.

Key claims:
- Sweden launched its premium-pension fund market in 2000.
- A booklet presented 456 funds from 72 companies to about four million savers.
- Nearly two thirds initially chose actively after a major campaign.
- Fund advertising emphasized salience more than fees or diversification.
- Active portfolios were often riskier and more expensive than the default.
- New cohorts became far less likely to choose actively as promotion faded.
- Both active choices and delegation became sticky.

Named examples:
- Swedish Premium Pension Agency / 2000 fund launch: Around four million Swedes received a catalog covering 456 funds managed by 72 companies and could allocate savings among one to five funds. The government campaign encouraged active selection rather than passive use of the default. (teaches: A campaign can overpower a default by framing active choice as the responsible action.)
- Henrik Cronqvist and Richard Thaler / Swedish portfolio analysis: Cronqvist and Thaler compared actively selected portfolios with the government default. Active choosers tended toward higher fees, more concentrated risk, and stronger home bias, while advertising predicted fund flows. (teaches: More active choice does not guarantee a better portfolio when the menu and marketing are difficult.)
- Seventh AP Fund / Premium Savings Fund: Savers who made no selection entered the state-run Premium Savings Fund managed by AP7. The low-cost diversified default later evolved toward an age-adjusted AP7 Såfa structure. (teaches: A delegated default can outperform unaided active selection when it is designed as a real product rather than a penalty box.)

Hard edge / typical misreading:
  The lesson is not that active choice is always bad or that a government fund is always superior. It is that engagement counts are a poor success metric when the choice is complex, advertising is salient, and portfolios remain untouched for decades.

Paraphrase notes:
Sweden's premium-pension launch is a long experiment in competing nudges. In 2000, about four million people received material describing 456 funds from 72 companies and could allocate among one to five. A government campaign and roughly 65 million dollars in fund advertising encouraged active management, and almost two thirds selected portfolios rather than enter the Premium Savings Fund. Henrik Cronqvist and Richard Thaler's analysis found that engagement did not produce superior construction. Active portfolios tended to carry higher fees, more concentration, and stronger home bias than the low-cost diversified default managed by the Seventh AP Fund. Advertising and recent performance attracted flows, while relevant characteristics were harder to evaluate. The launch effect did not renew itself. Advertising fell below 10 million dollars by 2003, later cohorts overwhelmingly delegated, and people on both paths rarely revisited their status. A saver who actively chose once did not become a continuously attentive manager. The persistence cuts both ways: an effective default can protect inattentive savers, while a poor early portfolio can remain frozen long after its rationale disappears. AP7's later age-adjusted Såfa structure treats the default as a product that must evolve with the saver rather than a holding pen. The evaluation standard is therefore long-run fees, diversification, risk, and review—not the number of people induced to click in year one. Grounding anchors: 456 funds from 72 fund companies; catalog sent to about four million people in 2000; 2004 analysis of the Swedish premium-pension launch; active portfolios were costlier and less diversified than the default; default managed by Sweden's Seventh AP Fund; later AP7 Såfa allocation becomes more bond-heavy with age.
