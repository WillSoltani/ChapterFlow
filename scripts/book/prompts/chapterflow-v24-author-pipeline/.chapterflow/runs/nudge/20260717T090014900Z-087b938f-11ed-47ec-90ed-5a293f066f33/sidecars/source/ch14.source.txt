Chapter 14 focus: Climate change combines delayed, invisible, probabilistic harm with international free riding. Nudges can improve disclosure, defaults, and household feedback, but prices, caps, standards, and coordinated enforcement must carry the main load.

Core claim: Use every suitable tool: behavioral design can complement but cannot replace carbon pricing and collective policy.

Central concept (climate policy tool stack):
  A coordinated mix of prices, rules, disclosure, defaults, feedback, and international cooperation aimed at lowering emissions.
  Why it matters: The scale and externality of climate harm exceed what voluntary individual nudges can solve alone.

Key claims:
- Present bias makes current mitigation costs more salient than future climate harm.
- Greenhouse gases are less visible than local smoke or dirty water.
- No single villain concentrates responsibility.
- The atmosphere creates a tragedy-of-the-commons problem.
- Carbon taxes or tradable caps internalize part of the external cost.
- Emission disclosure can create reputational pressure.
- Green defaults and energy feedback reduce some consumption.
- International free riding requires coordination and consequences.

Named examples:
- Hunt Allcott / OPOWER Home Energy Reports: OPOWER mailed households reports comparing their electricity use with similar neighbors and offering conservation tips. Randomized programs covering about 600,000 households reduced average consumption by 2.0 percent. (teaches: Specific local feedback and social comparison can reduce household energy use at low cost.)
- US EPA / Toxics Release Inventory: Federal law required covered facilities to publish releases of listed toxic chemicals through the Toxics Release Inventory. Public rankings and scrutiny created reputational pressure even before a new emissions tax or ban. (teaches: Mandatory disclosure can change organizational behavior through visibility.)
- William Nordhaus / climate club proposal: Nordhaus proposed a coalition whose members adopt a common carbon price and impose trade penalties on nonparticipants. The penalty changes the incentive to free ride outside a cooperative agreement. (teaches: International cooperation needs an enforceable benefit of joining or cost of staying out.)

Hard edge / typical misreading:
  A two-percent household energy reduction is useful but not a climate strategy by itself. Low-cost nudges become dangerous when leaders use them to avoid pricing pollution, regulating major emitters, building clean infrastructure, or enforcing international agreements.

Paraphrase notes:
Climate change combines several barriers that magnify one another. The most serious harms feel distant, greenhouse gases are invisible, responsibility is dispersed, and immediate mitigation costs trigger loss aversion. At the international level, every country benefits when others reduce emissions and can therefore free ride. Prices and enforceable collective rules must lead the response. A carbon tax attaches a private cost to each unit of pollution; cap-and-trade fixes an aggregate limit and lets sources exchange permits; the social cost of carbon estimates marginal damage for policy analysis. William Nordhaus's climate-club proposal adds a trade penalty for nonparticipants so cooperation is not sustained by goodwill alone. Behavioral tools still contribute. The EPA's Toxics Release Inventory, created under the 1986 right-to-know law, makes facility releases visible and creates reputational pressure. Hunt Allcott's analysis of OPOWER Home Energy Reports covers about 600,000 households and finds a 2.0 percent average electricity reduction from neighbor comparisons and tailored tips. Green defaults and a Green More Tomorrow escalation path can similarly reduce friction and soften immediate loss. The quantitative boundary is crucial: two percent household savings is worthwhile, not sufficient. Nudges should supplement carbon prices, standards, infrastructure, innovation, and international enforcement. Treating them as a cheap substitute would mistake an assist for a climate policy. Grounding anchors: about 600,000 treatment and control households; average electricity reduction of 2.0 percent; created under the 1986 Emergency Planning and Community Right-to-Know Act; facility-level toxic release data made public; common carbon-price commitment among club members; trade penalties applied to nonparticipants.
