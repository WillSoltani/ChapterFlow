Chapter 13 focus: Registration defaults produce dramatic differences in recorded donor status, but presumed consent is a weak signal of an individual's wishes and still depends on family and clinical systems. Prompted choice creates clearer consent while addressing inattention.

Core claim: Organ-donation policy should prompt an explicit, easy decision rather than infer strong consent from silence.

Central concept (prompted organ-donor choice):
  People are directly asked to record yes or no at a natural administrative touchpoint, with registration made simple.
  Why it matters: An explicit signal helps families and clinicians honor a person's preference while overcoming procrastination.

Key claims:
- Opt-in and opt-out defaults create large gaps in recorded donor status.
- Recorded status is not the same as completed donation.
- Soft opt-out systems still consult families.
- Silence may reflect inertia rather than informed consent.
- Prompted choice produces a stronger preference signal.
- Driver's-license transactions reach a large share of US adults.
- First-person authorization laws strengthen recorded consent.
- Clinical capacity and family communication determine whether organs are recovered.

Named examples:
- Eric Johnson and Daniel Goldstein / European donor defaults: Johnson and Goldstein compared recorded consent under different national defaults, including roughly 12 percent in opt-in Germany and 99.98 percent in opt-out Austria. Their experiments also showed that a default could shift hypothetical donor choices. (teaches: Nonresponse rules have enormous effects on recorded consent.)
- NHS England and Wales / soft opt-out: Wales adopted deemed consent in 2015 and England followed in May 2020. The systems remain soft: families are consulted, and if no relatives can be reached and no decision is recorded, donation does not proceed. (teaches: A legal default does not translate mechanically into organ recovery.)
- Donate Life America / motor-vehicle registration prompts: US states place donor questions in driver's-license and identification-card transactions and maintain registries covering roughly 170 million people. First-person authorization laws treat a recorded yes as legal permission rather than a tentative preference. (teaches: A recurring administrative touchpoint can create explicit, scalable consent.)

Hard edge / typical misreading:
  High opt-out registration rates do not prove high donation rates, informed consent, or family acceptance. The Final Edition rejects routine presumed consent as a complete solution and favors better prompting, registration, family discussion, and transplant capacity.

Voice cues observed in this chapter:

- uses an apparent default success as a counterexample
- maps four affected parties
- separates legal consent from clinical practice

Paraphrase notes:
The dramatic European default comparison is real but easy to misuse. Eric Johnson and Daniel Goldstein reported recorded donor status near 12 percent in opt-in Germany and 99.98 percent in opt-out Austria. Those figures show the power of nonresponse rules, not completed transplants or informed enthusiasm. Wales' 2015 and England's May 2020 deemed-consent laws make the operational distinction clear. Both are soft systems: families are consulted, and a missing relative or uncertain wish can stop donation. Silence remains ambiguous because it may represent agreement, inattention, ignorance, or procrastination. Prompted choice seeks a stronger signal. US motor-vehicle transactions ask for a yes or no while issuing a driver's license or state ID, helping registries reach roughly 170 million people. First-person authorization laws can give a recorded yes legal force. The prompt must still be easy, understandable, and connected to family communication. Recipients need organs, potential donors need autonomy, families face bereavement, and clinicians need timely lawful authority and capacity. Optimizing only the registry rate ignores those interacting constraints. The correct Final Edition position is therefore not presumed consent as a magic default. It is repeated, well-placed prompts, simple registration, explicit permission, conversation with relatives, and investment in the transplant system that turns a valid preference into a safe recovery. Grounding anchors: Germany recorded about 12 percent donor consent; Austria recorded about 99.98 percent under opt-out; Wales soft opt-out began in 2015; England deemed-consent law took effect in May 2020; roughly 170 million people in US donor registries; driver's-license or state-ID transactions prompt the decision.
