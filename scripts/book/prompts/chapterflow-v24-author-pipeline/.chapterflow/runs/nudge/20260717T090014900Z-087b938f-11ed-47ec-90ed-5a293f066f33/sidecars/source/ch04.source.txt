Chapter 4 focus: Nudges are most valuable for decisions that are rare, difficult, poorly understood, weakly connected to feedback, or easy to neglect. The intervention should be highly likely to help and unlikely to harm.

Core claim: The case for a nudge grows when ordinary learning cannot reliably correct mistakes.

Central concept (nudge need conditions):
  A decision is a strong nudge candidate when people lack practice, prompt feedback, clear preference mapping, or enough attention.
  Why it matters: Choice architects can prioritize scarce effort where small design changes have the largest expected benefit and the least downside.

Key claims:
- Rare decisions do not generate enough repetition for learning.
- Delayed or missing feedback lets errors persist.
- Complex mappings make it hard to connect features to lived outcomes.
- Forgetfulness calls for reminders rather than larger incentives.
- Checklists coordinate known steps under pressure.
- Competitive markets may not protect consumers when mistakes are profitable.

Named examples:
- Todd Rogers, David Nickerson, and voting-plan prompts: Before the 2008 US presidential election, eligible voters were prompted to specify when, where, and how they would vote. The planning questions raised turnout particularly among households with a single eligible voter. (teaches: A concrete plan can close the gap between intention and an easily forgotten action.)
- World Health Organization / Surgical Safety Checklist: A WHO pilot introduced a 19-item checklist at eight hospitals in eight cities. The before-and-after study reported lower major complications and inpatient deaths after implementation. (teaches: A short prompt can protect a complex procedure from omission when teams already know the required steps.)
- US extended-warranty market: Retailers sell protection on inexpensive electronics and appliances at prices far above expected repair cost. These rare, opaque failures are difficult for buyers to evaluate and highly profitable for sellers. (teaches: Markets can amplify mistakes when firms earn more from consumer confusion than from correcting it.)

Hard edge / typical misreading:
  Difficulty alone does not justify steering toward an architect's preferred outcome. The golden rule asks whether the intervention is likely to make choosers better off by their own standards and whether a mistake by the architect would be easy to escape.

Paraphrase notes:
A nudge earns its keep where ordinary learning breaks down. The strongest candidates are rare, difficult decisions with delayed feedback, unfamiliar mappings, or an easy path to forgetting. Todd Rogers and David Nickerson's 2008 voting study turns intention into action by asking eligible voters when, where, and how they will vote. The content is mundane; the mechanism is a concrete implementation plan that makes the future cue retrievable. The World Health Organization's 19-item Surgical Safety Checklist addresses a different failure. Eight hospitals in eight cities tested a standardized set of pauses and confirmations, with the published before-and-after comparison reporting lower major complications and deaths. Expertise was not replaced; known steps were protected from omission and team coordination failure. Extended warranties show why a market does not automatically solve every decision problem. Failures are rare, repair probabilities are opaque, and sellers can earn high margins precisely because buyers overreact to a salient breakdown scenario. These examples point to a diagnostic sequence. Ask how often the choice is made, whether feedback arrives quickly enough to teach, whether attributes map to outcomes the chooser values, and whether inattention rather than preference is the obstacle. Then apply the golden rule: favor interventions with a high chance of helping by the chooser's own lights and a low chance of harm. A prompt, checklist, or warning should be easy to ignore when the architect is mistaken. Grounding anchors: 2008 United States presidential election; questions specified when, where, and how to vote; 19-item surgical checklist; extended warranties on electronics and appliances; retailer margins exceed expected repair value.
