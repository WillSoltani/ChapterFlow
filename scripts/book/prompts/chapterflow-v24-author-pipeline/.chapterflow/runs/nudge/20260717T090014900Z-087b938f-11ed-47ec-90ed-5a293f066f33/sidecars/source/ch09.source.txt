Chapter 9 focus: Retirement design must help people enroll, raise contributions, and invest sensibly. Automatic enrollment and future-pay-raise escalation redirect inertia and loss aversion toward long-term saving.

Core claim: Saving rises when enrollment and escalation happen automatically while exit remains available.

Central concept (automatic retirement saving):
  A plan enrolls eligible workers by default and can schedule contribution increases unless they opt out.
  Why it matters: It converts procrastination from a barrier into continuity while avoiding the felt loss of an immediate cut in take-home pay.

Key claims:
- Defined-contribution systems shift enrollment and investment decisions to workers.
- Automatic enrollment sharply increases participation.
- The default contribution and fund also anchor behavior.
- Save More Tomorrow schedules increases with future raises.
- A cap and easy opt-out preserve control.
- Low-cost diversified defaults protect inattentive savers.
- Directional nudging is justified when many workers say they want to save more.

Named examples:
- Brigitte Madrian and Dennis Shea / automatic 401(k) enrollment: A large US firm changed from opt-in enrollment to automatic enrollment without changing the plan's economic terms. Participation after one year rose from 49 percent for the opt-in cohort to 86 percent for the automatically enrolled cohort. (teaches: The nonresponse rule can dominate participation even with unchanged incentives.)
- Richard Thaler and Shlomo Benartzi / Save More Tomorrow field trial: Workers were invited to commit future pay raises to automatic contribution increases. Among participants, average saving rose from 3.5 percent to 13.6 percent of pay over four raises and about 40 months. (teaches: Future escalation avoids an immediate nominal pay cut and lets inertia sustain the plan.)
- Vanguard / How America Saves 2018: Vanguard reported on 473 record-kept plans in 2018. Fifty-nine percent used automatic enrollment, and those plans averaged 93 percent participation. (teaches: Automatic enrollment scaled beyond one firm and became a mainstream plan design.)

Hard edge / typical misreading:
  Automatic enrollment solves joining, not adequacy. A very low contribution rate or costly default fund can trap people in a bad plan, so defaults must be reviewed for level, fees, diversification, and distributional effects.

Paraphrase notes:
Retirement saving is not one choice. Workers must enter a plan, contribute enough, and place the balance in a sensible portfolio. Brigitte Madrian and Dennis Shea's firm-level study isolates the first problem. Switching the nonresponse rule from opt-in to automatic enrollment raised one-year participation from 49 to 86 percent without changing economic terms. The same inertia can preserve a default contribution rate, so enrollment success can conceal inadequate saving. Richard Thaler and Shlomo Benartzi's Save More Tomorrow design addresses that second task. Workers commit in advance to contribution increases timed with future pay raises. Participants' average rate rose from 3.5 to 13.6 percent over four raises and about 40 months. Because take-home pay need not fall in nominal terms, the design reduces loss aversion; because increases continue automatically, inertia supports rather than blocks the plan. Vanguard's 2018 record-keeping data from 473 plans show the mechanism at scale: 59 percent used automatic enrollment, and those plans averaged 93 percent participation. Investment design completes the stack. A low-cost diversified lifecycle default gives inattentive participants a defensible portfolio and changes risk with age. Every default still requires scrutiny. A tiny contribution, a high-fee fund, or concentrated employer stock can turn stickiness into harm. The practical standard is automatic participation, gradual escalation, a sound fund, a transparent cap, and easy exit. Grounding anchors: one-year participation rose from 49 to 86 percent; automatic enrollment allowed employees to opt out; saving rate rose from 3.5 to 13.6 percent; four pay raises over about 40 months; 473 retirement plans in the 2018 report; 59 percent used automatic enrollment and averaged 93 percent participation.
