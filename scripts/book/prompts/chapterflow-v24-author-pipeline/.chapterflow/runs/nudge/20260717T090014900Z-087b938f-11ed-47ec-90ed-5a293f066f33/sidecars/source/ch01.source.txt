Chapter 1 focus: Fast judgment is indispensable but predictably distorted by anchors, memorable examples, stereotypes, loss sensitivity, inertia, and framing. Better design anticipates those recurring errors instead of assuming unlimited calculation.

Core claim: Human mistakes are patterned enough that a choice environment can be designed around them.

Central concept (predictable judgment error):
  Mental shortcuts simplify hard judgments by substituting an accessible cue for a full calculation, sometimes producing systematic error.
  Why it matters: Recognizing the mechanism identifies where estimates, risk perceptions, and defaults will pull decisions away from a person's considered interests.

Key claims:
- Automatic and reflective processing divide quick pattern recognition from effortful checking.
- Anchors bias estimates because later adjustment is usually incomplete.
- Availability turns ease of recall into an unreliable proxy for frequency or risk.
- Representativeness encourages stereotype matching while neglecting base rates.
- Loss aversion and status-quo bias make current holdings unusually sticky.
- Equivalent outcome descriptions can produce different choices through framing.

Named examples:
- Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky / anchoring wheel experiment: Kahneman and Tversky asked people to spin a wheel rigged to stop at 10 or 65, then estimate the percentage of African countries in the United Nations. Median estimates shifted from 25 after the low anchor to 45 after the high anchor. (teaches: Even an explicitly arbitrary starting value can pull a later numerical judgment.)
- Kahneman and Tversky / Linda conjunction problem: Participants read a profile of Linda as outspoken and concerned with social justice. Many rated bank teller plus feminist activist as more likely than bank teller alone, violating the rule that a conjunction cannot be more probable than one of its components. (teaches: A vivid stereotype can displace the arithmetic of probability.)
- Howard Kunreuther / flood and earthquake insurance behavior: Insurance research found that recent disasters sharply raise demand while coverage declines as memories fade. Floodplain residents who personally know a victim are more likely to buy coverage even when the underlying hazard has not changed. (teaches: Memorable events can dominate stable statistical risk through availability.)

Hard edge / typical misreading:
  The useful takeaway is not that intuition is foolish or always wrong. Shortcuts work often enough to be essential; the design problem appears where their predictable failure modes meet high stakes, weak feedback, or sticky defaults.

Paraphrase notes:
A visual illusion supplies the opening warning: certainty is not evidence of accuracy. The same split between quick perception and slower checking appears in judgment. Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky's rigged-wheel study gives anchoring a hard numerical spine: an irrelevant 10 or 65 pulled median estimates to 25 or 45 percent. Their Linda problem shows a different substitution. A socially engaged profile feels representative of a feminist activist, so many people choose a detailed conjunction that must be mathematically less likely than its broader component. Availability then moves the problem into risk. Howard Kunreuther's insurance work shows demand surging after a flood or earthquake and fading with memory; knowing a flood victim changes coverage behavior without changing the floodplain. These mechanisms are distinct: an anchor supplies a starting value, availability supplies retrievable examples, and representativeness supplies a stereotype. Loss aversion adds a reference point, making a setback loom larger than an equal gain, while status-quo bias turns the existing or preselected option into a powerful attractor. Framing demonstrates that even identical quantities can feel different when presented as survival rather than mortality. The operational lesson is diagnostic rather than cynical. Fast judgment remains useful, but confidence should trigger a reflective check when base rates are hidden, a recent event dominates memory, an arbitrary number is present, or the current option is sticky. The strongest intervention targets the specific error rather than treating every imperfect choice as irrational. Grounding anchors: wheel outcomes fixed at 10 or 65; median estimates of 25 and 45 percent; Linda described as 31 years old; bank teller versus bank teller and feminist activist; floodplain insurance decisions; coverage demand rises after earthquakes then declines.
