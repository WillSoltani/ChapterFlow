Chapter 3 focus: Other people's conduct supplies both information and pressure to conform. Social norms can improve behavior, backfire, or cascade, depending on which group and which signal are made salient.

Core claim: Behavior changes when people learn what relevant peers do or expect, even without a material incentive.

Central concept (social influence):
  Choices respond to observed peer behavior and perceived peer approval.
  Why it matters: Norm messages can become low-cost interventions, but a badly chosen norm may legitimize the very behavior it is meant to reduce.

Key claims:
- People conform because others may possess useful information and because disagreement carries social cost.
- Unanimous groups can overpower clear private perception.
- Randomly assigned peers affect effort and social choices.
- Local norms often outperform generic appeals.
- Descriptive norms can boomerang among people already doing better than average.
- A small early shift can produce an informational cascade.

Named examples:
- Solomon Asch / line-judgment experiments: Asch placed one participant among confederates who unanimously named an obviously wrong matching line. Across the critical trials, roughly three quarters of participants conformed at least once, while control errors were rare. (teaches: A unanimous group can change stated perception without changing the visual evidence.)
- Bruce Sacerdote / randomly assigned Dartmouth roommates: Dartmouth freshmen were assigned roommates within housing categories and then paired randomly. Sacerdote found peer effects on academic effort, GPA, and fraternity participation, but not across every life choice. (teaches: Social influence can be causally identified and remains domain-specific.)
- Noah Goldstein, Robert Cialdini, and Vladas Griskevicius / hotel towels: Hotel-room cards comparing guests with other guests increased towel reuse more than a generic environmental appeal. A norm tied to people who had stayed in the same room was especially effective. (teaches: A nearby and behaviorally relevant comparison group strengthens a descriptive norm.)

Hard edge / typical misreading:
  A social norm is not automatically prosocial. Announcing that many people litter, evade taxes, or waste energy can normalize the conduct; effective messages identify a desirable norm and a reference group the audience recognizes.

Voice cues observed in this chapter:

- repeats the need to test on the actual population

Paraphrase notes:
Social behavior is not noise around an individual decision; it changes what the decision means. Solomon Asch's line experiments make the pressure visible. One participant faced confederates who unanimously selected the wrong line, and about three quarters conformed at least once despite a clear stimulus. Bruce Sacerdote's Dartmouth evidence moves beyond the laboratory: 1997 and 1998 freshmen were randomly paired within 32 housing categories, revealing peer effects in effort, GPA, and fraternity participation but not in every domain. The boundary matters because herding is not a universal solvent. Hotel towel cards designed by Noah Goldstein, Robert Cialdini, and Vladas Griskevicius show how to convert the mechanism into design. Their original field experiment reported that a same-room norm beat a generic appeal, but a later hotel replication did not reproduce the provincial-norm advantage, making the result mixed rather than a settled design law. Social influence has two channels. Others may know something the chooser does not, or their approval may impose a social cost on dissent. The distinction explains both useful learning and blind cascades. It also explains boomerangs: telling efficient households that neighbors use more energy can grant permission to consume more. An approval cue can preserve the desirable behavior by adding an injunctive norm. The Don't Mess with Texas campaign similarly tied litter prevention to a valued in-group identity. A responsible choice architect therefore names a desirable behavior, chooses a credible comparison group, checks the baseline, and tests rather than assuming that any statistic about peers will help. Grounding anchors: one participant surrounded by unanimous confederates; about 75 percent conformed at least once; Dartmouth freshman cohorts from 1997 and 1998; roommates randomly paired within 32 housing categories; hotel bathroom towel-reuse cards; same-room guest norm outperformed a general environmental appeal.
