Chapter 12 focus: Insurance should transfer risks that could cause financial ruin, not routine losses a household can absorb. High deductibles can dominate low ones, but cost sharing must not discourage high-value medication and care.

Core claim: Protect catastrophic risk while self-insuring manageable losses and exempting high-value care from blunt cost sharing.

Central concept (deductible design):
  A deductible sets how much loss the insured pays before coverage begins, trading lower premiums against greater out-of-pocket exposure.
  Why it matters: Choosing the wrong deductible can guarantee higher expected cost, while poorly targeted cost sharing can suppress essential care.

Key claims:
- Insurance creates the most value for rare losses a person cannot absorb.
- Extended warranties insure small losses at high prices.
- Deductible aversion leads many consumers to overpay for low deductibles.
- Dominance comparisons can identify a plan that is never financially better.
- High-deductible choices saved more than 500 dollars annually across studied firms.
- Cost sharing can reduce both low-value and high-value use.
- Health savings can be mentally earmarked to make self-insurance credible.

Named examples:
- Saurabh Bhargava, George Loewenstein, and Justin Sydnor / employer health-plan choices: Researchers compared employees' plan selections across firms offering low- and high-deductible options. Many selected financially dominated low-deductible plans, while the high-deductible option produced expected savings above 500 dollars a year across a broad range of spending. (teaches: Deductible aversion can survive even when one plan is no better in any spending state.)
- Peter Huckfeldt and colleagues / pharmaceutical deductibles: Two large firms switched employees to consumer-directed health plans, but only one subjected prescriptions to the deductible. Drug use fell most where prescriptions faced the deductible, including medicines for chronic disease. (teaches: Patients do not reliably cut only low-value care when exposed to broad cost sharing.)
- Pitney Bowes / value-based insurance design: Pitney Bowes reduced copayments for medicines treating asthma, hypertension, and diabetes. Adherence increased, and higher drug spending was offset by lower condition-related medical spending. (teaches: Cost sharing should be lower for care with high clinical value.)

Hard edge / typical misreading:
  The largest deductible is not universally safest. Liquidity, plan networks, employer HSA contributions, maximum out-of-pocket exposure, chronic illness, and drug exemptions can reverse the comparison; use dominance and total-cost analysis rather than a slogan.

Voice cues observed in this chapter:
- opens with a blunt risk rule
- contrasts flood-sized loss with toaster-sized loss
- uses dominance rather than intuition to compare plans
- introduces behavioral hazard as a correction to standard moral-hazard analysis

Forbidden leakage (concepts that belong to later chapters):

- retirement lifecycle funds

Paraphrase notes:
Insurance should concentrate on losses a household cannot absorb. Covering a flood or catastrophic illness pools ruinous risk; covering a toaster or routine repair adds administration and profit to a manageable expense. Deductibles turn that principle into a plan comparison. Saurabh Bhargava, George Loewenstein, and Justin Sydnor found employees choosing low-deductible health plans that were financially dominated, while high-deductible options offered expected savings above 500 dollars per year across broad spending ranges. A dominance check is stronger than a prediction: one plan is no worse in any relevant state and better in at least one. Health care adds a crucial complication. Peter Huckfeldt and colleagues compared two firms that moved to consumer-directed plans; prescription use fell more where drugs were subject to the deductible, including chronic-disease medicines. Broad cost sharing creates behavioral hazard when people skip valuable care as well as waste. Pitney Bowes used the opposite structure, lowering copays for asthma, hypertension, and diabetes drugs; adherence rose and condition-related medical costs fell enough to offset higher drug spending. A high deductible therefore needs liquidity and value-sensitive design. An On My Own HSA can earmark premium savings and employer contributions for self-insured expenses, but networks, maximum exposure, drug rules, and chronic needs remain part of the comparison. The rule is catastrophic protection plus careful total-cost mapping, not reflexively buying either the lowest or highest deductible. Grounding anchors: employees chose financially dominated low-deductible plans; two large employers switched to consumer-directed plans; prescriptions faced the deductible at one firm but were exempt at the other; reduced copayments for asthma, hypertension, and diabetes drugs; higher adherence accompanied lower condition-related medical costs.
