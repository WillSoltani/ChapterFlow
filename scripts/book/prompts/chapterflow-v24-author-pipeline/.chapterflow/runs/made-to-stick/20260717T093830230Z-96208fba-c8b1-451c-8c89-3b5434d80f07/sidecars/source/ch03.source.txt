Chapter 3 focus: Concrete language gives abstract ideas sensory form, creates multiple retrieval cues, and lets groups coordinate around the same observable target. Cases, objects, simulations, and specific criteria make meaning easier to inspect and harder to interpret away.

Core claim: People understand, remember, and coordinate around ideas better when the intended meaning is tied to observable people, objects, actions, and outcomes.

Central concept (concrete hooks):
  Express meaning through things that can be pictured, handled, enacted, or checked instead of relying only on category labels and abstractions.
  Why it matters: Concrete hooks give memory more routes back to an idea and give collaborators a common object against which to test interpretations.

Key claims:
- Sensory nouns and observable actions constrain ambiguity more effectively than broad conceptual labels.
- A case can carry abstract instruction by placing it inside a sequence of decisions and consequences.
- Simulations make a social mechanism felt rather than merely named.
- Concrete design criteria coordinate specialists who otherwise attach different meanings to the same abstract goal.
- A physical demonstration can make a simple intervention credible and teachable across language and expertise barriers.

Named examples:
- Georgia State Safe Night Out accounting case: Georgia State University instructors taught accounting through the Safe Night Out case, following co-founders Kris and Sandy as they built a startup. Transactions and statements acquired concrete consequences inside an unfolding business story. (teaches: A continuing case supplies people, motives, and consequences that abstract accounting terms can attach to.)
- Jane Elliott's blue-eyes/brown-eyes exercise: On April 5, 1968, Iowa teacher Jane Elliott divided her third-grade class by eye color, privileging one group and later reversing the roles. The blue-eyes/brown-eyes exercise turned discrimination from a definition into an experienced system of arbitrary status. (teaches: An enacted mechanism can reveal social consequences that an abstract lecture leaves distant.)
- Boeing 727 / LaGuardia Runway 4-22: The Boeing 727 design target included taking off fully loaded and landing on LaGuardia Runway 4-22. A named aircraft, payload condition, airport, and runway gave multiple engineering teams one inspectable performance test. (teaches: A shared concrete criterion aligns specialists better than a phrase such as high-performance airliner.)
- James P. Grant / oral rehydration salts packet: UNICEF executive director James P. Grant used a packet of sugar and salt to make oral rehydration therapy visible to policymakers. Oral rehydration salts are dissolved in clean water so coupled glucose and sodium absorption can replace fluid lost through severe diarrhoea. (teaches: A small physical object can carry a life-saving mechanism more effectively than an abstract program description.)
- Aesop's Fox and the Grapes: The Fox and the Grapes gives sour grapes a fox, unreachable fruit, and a face-saving judgment. The fable packages an abstract pattern of devaluing what one cannot obtain inside concrete action. (teaches: A compact scene can preserve an abstract moral through recognizable objects and behavior.)

Hard edge / typical misreading:
  Concrete is not the same as adding arbitrary color, dates, or decoration. Detail earns its place when it clarifies the mechanism, creates a retrieval hook, or supplies a shared test; irrelevant specificity can distract as badly as abstraction.

Voice cues observed in this chapter:
- moves between fable, classroom simulation, engineering, and public health
- turns abstract labels into objects or actions the reader can picture
- uses the Velcro metaphor to explain multiple retrieval routes
- asks whether different specialists would interpret a goal the same way

Paraphrase notes:
Abstraction is efficient for experts who already share a vocabulary, but it leaves everyone else with few retrieval cues and plenty of interpretive freedom. Aesop's fox and unreachable grapes show the alternative: motive, failed action, and face-saving judgment preserve a subtle psychological pattern in a scene. Georgia State University's Safe Night Out case similarly attaches accounting to Kris and Sandy, so transactions affect named founders rather than floating as entries without consequences.

Experience makes the mechanism still more tangible. Jane Elliott's April 5, 1968 third-grade exercise assigned status by eye color and later reversed it. The value lies in enacted rules and felt consequences, not in treating the exercise as controlled proof that a short simulation solves systemic racism. Concrete targets also coordinate. The Boeing 727 requirement of a fully loaded takeoff and safe landing on LaGuardia Runway 4-22 gave engineers a shared test more useful than a broad demand for excellent performance.

Public health supplies the physical-object case. James P. Grant carried a packet of sugar and salt to make oral rehydration therapy visible. Oral rehydration salts dissolved in clean water exploit coupled glucose and sodium absorption to replace lost fluid. The packet turns global policy into ingredients, preparation, and a body mechanism that can be taught. These examples support a Velcro model of memory: people, objects, sensations, actions, and consequences create several hooks for retrieval. The hard edge is functional detail. A date or color that does not clarify mechanism, memory, or coordination is ornament; useful concreteness makes the intended meaning inspectable.
