Chapter 6 focus: Stories prepare action by simulating decisions and inspire action by showing challenge, connection, or creative problem solving. Useful stories can be spotted in real work and used as springboards without being reduced to lifeless morals.

Core claim: A well-chosen story gives listeners a mental rehearsal or a motivating model, making action easier to imagine and initiate.

Central concept (stories as simulation and inspiration):
  Narrative places a person inside goals, obstacles, choices, and consequences so listeners can rehearse what to do or feel that action is possible.
  Why it matters: Instructions become usable experience and abstract encouragement acquires a visible path.

Key claims:
- A story can encode diagnostic knowledge that a formal rule omits.
- Process simulation rehearses the steps of action, whereas outcome fantasy only pictures the finish.
- Inspirational stories often follow challenge, connection, or creativity plots.
- Organizations can spot effective stories already present in ordinary work rather than inventing polished parables.
- A springboard story helps listeners imagine a comparable future in their own setting.
- Extracting only the moral can remove the causal sequence that made the lesson usable.

Named examples:
- Julian Orr / Xerox copier technicians: Julian Orr's mid-1980s fieldwork followed U.S. Xerox copier technicians as they diagnosed machines and shared repair stories. Their oral narratives circulated unusual symptoms, interpretations, and fixes that formal documentation did not fully capture. (teaches: Work stories can function as distributed technical training rather than mere entertainment.)
- Pham and Taylor mental-simulation experiment: Lien Pham and Shelley Taylor asked college freshmen to simulate either study processes, desired exam outcomes, both, or self-monitoring for 5 to 7 days before a midterm. Process simulation improved studying and grades through better planning and lower anxiety. (teaches: Rehearsing a causal process prepares behavior better than admiring an imagined result.)
- Jared Fogle / Subway campaign: A 1999 Indiana Daily Student story reported that Jared Fogle had lost 245 pounds through changed eating and exercise, and Subway made the transformation a national advertising story in 2000. The historical campaign preceded Fogle's later criminal conviction and must not be treated as a moral endorsement or current model spokesperson. (teaches: A discovered challenge narrative can outperform a menu claim because it supplies protagonist, obstacle, and visible change.)
- Stephen Denning / World Bank Zambia story: Stephen Denning used a June 1995 account of a health worker in Kamana, Zambia, who found malaria-treatment information online to explain knowledge sharing at the World Bank. The small existing case let listeners imagine a larger institutional future. (teaches: A springboard story shows one successful instance so the audience can project analogous change.)

Hard edge / typical misreading:
  Story is not decoration, proof by anecdote, or permission to dramatize a real person's life. The narrative must preserve sourced facts, reveal a usable causal sequence, and remain proportionate to what one case can establish.

Voice cues observed in this chapter:
- moves from workplace diagnosis to controlled mental-simulation research
- separates simulation from inspiration before naming three plot families
- treats story spotting as a practical skill
- ends with a small springboard case that lets listeners imagine scale

Forbidden leakage (concepts that belong to later chapters):
- Do not import unsourced scenes, quotations, or motives into any real person's case.
- Do not omit Jared Fogle's later-disgraced status when deciding whether to reuse the historical campaign; avoid celebratory moral framing.
- Do not retell Floyd Lee as the primary challenge plot; his sourced role is identity and morale.

Paraphrase notes:
Stories do cognitive work when they preserve a sequence someone can mentally enter. Julian Orr's mid-1980s fieldwork with U.S. Xerox copier technicians found that repair narratives circulated unusual symptoms, interpretations, and fixes. The talk was part of diagnosis and professional memory, not downtime entertainment. Pham and Taylor tested the action link with college freshmen for 5 to 7 days before a midterm. Rehearsing the study process improved planning, reduced anxiety, increased studying, and improved grades; picturing the desired outcome did not supply the same behavioral route.

Inspiration uses recognizable plot movement. Challenge shows an obstacle overcome, Connection crosses a social boundary, and Creativity reveals a resourceful solution. The historical Subway case arrived through spotting: a 1999 Indiana Daily Student account reported that Jared Fogle lost 245 pounds, and the national advertising story followed in 2000. Current use needs an ethical boundary. His later conviction for crimes involving minors makes celebratory treatment misleading; the sourced communication observation concerns discovery and plot, not endorsement of the person or diet.

Stephen Denning's June 1995 Kamana, Zambia case demonstrates the springboard form. A health worker found malaria information online, and that compact real instance helped World Bank listeners imagine broader knowledge sharing. The story works by launching a future scenario in the audience's mind, not by demanding literal copying. Across the cases, the moral cannot substitute for goal, obstacle, choice, action, and consequence. Those links are the simulation. Fidelity also matters: narrative power never licenses invented dialogue, compressed causality, or proof by one anecdote.
