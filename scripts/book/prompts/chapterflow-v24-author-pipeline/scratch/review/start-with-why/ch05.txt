# Clarity, Discipline and Consistency

## Hook
Why does project lead Neil's cheaper service still feel unclear after the price is set?

## Fast read
Why does project lead Neil's cheaper service still feel unclear after the price is set? Southwest Airlines (the airline that began service in 1971 from Dallas and became a low-fare carrier) gives him a way to answer. Price alone is not a promise. It is only the part outsiders can see.

The loop closes in order. Clarity of WHY names the reason. Discipline of HOW names the conduct that must answer it. Consistency of WHAT lets someone see the fit again. If Neil starts with the cheap tier, the tier has to explain too much. If he starts with why low cost matters, the tier can be judged. The fare did not prove the belief; the belief made the fare readable.

## Deep read
The reason gives each action a bill to pay. When the action comes first, it can look tidy, but it cannot tell a stranger why the choice deserves trust. Sequence matters.

On a family video call, Neil tries the easy answer: cheap service proves the promise. His sister hears a sale. So he reruns the thought through Southwest Airlines. The 1971 Dallas airline did not make low fare credible by saying the words. It had to keep choices lined up with the low-fare identity.

Herb Kelleher (a Southwest Airlines cofounder) moves the lesson away from price alone. In that 1971 airline identity, conduct, humor, and hiring had to carry the same direction. Humor is not the proof. Hiring is not the proof by itself. Each owes the reason.

Apple design consistency (the Cupertino design thread, shown here by the 1998 iMac) changes the field again. The useful lesson is not that one attractive product wins. It is that recognizable choices can repeat the same posture across more than one WHAT. What would a stranger see before the promise spoke?

That question keeps the advice from turning broad. You do not make a claim stronger by adding a nicer sign at the end. You make the sign easier to judge by naming the reason first, then asking what conduct must pay it back.

## Full read
Neil's mistake is common because visible things feel easier to manage than reasons. A lower price, a hiring note, or a product choice can be changed before anyone asks what it is meant to prove. That is how tidy acts start carrying the wrong load.

Use the rule as a pre-send pause. Say the reason in plain words. Then ask who owes whom what by when. Make the act owe the reason. If no person owes a visible act, the claim is still air.

Southwest Airlines keeps this practical. The Dallas detail belongs there because it is part of the airline's start in 1971. Move Dallas to the Cupertino design example and the sentence still sounds firm, but it grounds the wrong thing. True facts can lie when they travel to the wrong place.

Kelleher helps in another way. Cofounder is not the same kind of token as 1971 or Dallas; it points to a person whose choices, humor, and hiring reinforced the airline identity. That contrast matters because one example can charm you into treating luck as law. A second anchor asks whether the conduct still fits when the venue changes.

Apple design consistency gives the edge. The 1998 iMac helps show where alignment discipline works: a recognizable challenger-and-design posture shows up in what buyers can see. It also tells you where to stop. A product detail from Cupertino does not prove every other organization with a clean object is aligned.

This is useful even if you have no title. If you write the support reply, choose a feature, or send a hiring note, you can run the check before the miss. The loop that closes is small: reason first, owed conduct second, visible result last.

Neil tries it on the cheaper service. He stops calling the price the purpose and asks what customers would notice if low cost were real. The custom favors no longer fit. Cutting them hurts the offer's shine, but it makes the promise easier to inspect. The change is not louder. It is cleaner.

There is a cost. This discipline slows the fast sentence you want to send. It may kill a neat tactic you liked. It can make a warm Kelleher story less useful if the hiring rule still rewards charm alone. That discomfort helps because it fires before the miss.

Use this with care. It cannot rescue a false WHY, and it cannot turn thin evidence into strong proof. If the setting changes, ask for fresh conduct. If you are still learning, treat the visible act as a probe, not a verdict. When no act appears, say less.

## Key takeaway
Clear purpose becomes credible when conduct pays it back and visible choices repeat the fit. Southwest Airlines, Herb Kelleher, and Apple design consistency show the loop that closes.

## Try this now
Watch one outgoing promise for 5 minutes during your first inbox scan. Say the reason in one line; write down the visible act.

## Examples
### Example 1: 1971 Does Not Fit
1971 is the number that will not add up for project lead Neil. His cheaper service tier still includes custom favors, so the offer is headed for a miss: the price says low cost while the conduct says special handling. He traces the number back to Southwest Airlines, the 1971 Dallas low-fare carrier, and cuts the favors before launch. The offer changes from mixed promise to plain low-cost service.

What to do: Skip this and the number becomes decoration. Say why the promise should exist, then remove the move that makes the visible offer behave against it.

Why it matters: Let the early mismatch pass and a low price has to carry the whole belief. Southwest works here because conduct lets the reader inspect the claim.

### Example 2: Charm Still Scores
Training director Nancy states the demand before the hiring screen opens: Kelleher's humor counts only if it reinforces Southwest Airlines' 1971 identity through choices and hiring. A blue badge sits between her and Neil. "So charm is enough?" he asks. "No. Fit is the bar." The move helps, but the old score still rewards charm by itself, so the standard only half-works.

What to do: Ignore the leftover score and the story gets softer than the standard. Ask what the humor owed the airline identity, then remove charm that pays no debt.

Why it matters: Keep the extra reward and hiring only half-changes. The Kelleher anchor matters because cofounder gives contrast, not a blank pass for personality.

### Example 3: The Add-On Leaves
Product lead Holly is already cutting the attractive add-on. The demand was set by Apple design consistency: the 1998 iMac from the Cupertino thread helps mark a recognizable challenger-and-design posture. The add-on would point elsewhere. She drops it, loses the easy pitch, and the product choice leaves with one clearer signal.

What to do: Keep it and novelty splits the signal. Cut the extra that makes the visible WHAT point away from the reason.

Why it matters: Delay and the offer pays for flash with trust. Consistency has a cost: the tempting piece leaves.

### Example 4: Dallas Goes Home
At the return handoff, Neil catches the miss as the cursor blinks: Dallas has drifted into a Cupertino design paragraph. The city belongs with Southwest Airlines, which began service from Dallas in 1971. The email is nearly gone. He stops it, moves Dallas back to the airline section, and the client receives a narrower, truer claim.

What to do: Leave it and a true detail props up the wrong setting. Stop the send. Return Dallas to Southwest. Let Cupertino stand on its own facts.

Why it matters: Let it travel and the reader trusts a false link. Keep each hard detail with the place that earned it.

### Example 5: The Claim Pauses
After the staff update falls flat, strategist Preston traces the miss backward from the last claim. A birthday-card envelope sits beside him. The talk used purpose words, but no behavior tied them to Southwest Airlines' 1971 low-fare carrier setting, Herb Kelleher's cofounder role, or Apple Inc. in Cupertino around the 1998 iMac. He pauses the wider rollout and sets the condition: one visible act before the claim comes back.

What to do: Skip the trace and purpose words do all the lifting. Shrink the claim to the one behavior Preston can show, then wait.

Why it matters: Rush the wider claim and people have nothing to inspect. The pause changes the outcome: less reach now, a clearer debt later.

## Implementation plan
Title: Make The Promise Owe Conduct
Core skill: Use a promise as a debt, not a decoration. Say the reason first, name the conduct that owes it, and pick the visible result another person could see. If no act can be named, make the promise smaller.
If-then 1: [An outgoing message promises a benefit.] If an outgoing message promises a benefit, then count 1 visible act the recipient could see by the end of the day.
If-then 2: [A tactic feels neat before the reason is clear.] If a tactic feels neat before the reason is clear, then count 1 reason it must serve before you keep it.
If-then 3: [A true detail sounds useful in another setting.] If a true detail sounds useful in another setting, then count 1 source it belongs to before you move it.
24-hour challenge: Bolt this onto brushing your teeth tomorrow morning: say the reason behind 1 promise you will make, then say the 1 act another person could see.
Weekly practice: Every Friday before shutdown, say aloud: "The reason was __. The owed act was __. The visible result was __." Then pick one blank to fix before Monday.
