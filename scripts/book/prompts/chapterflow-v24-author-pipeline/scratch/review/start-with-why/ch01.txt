# Assume You Know

## Hook
Operations lead Gregoire pauses at the door gap and hears the repair praised.

## Fast read
Operations lead Gregoire pauses at the door gap and hears the repair praised. The fit improved, yet the praise may be aimed at the wrong thing. A clean repair can be the wrong win.

Assumption risk is the risk that a smart action starts from a weak guess. The order is simple: Known data, Assumptions, Decisions, Results. What can be shown comes first. What you think it means comes second. Only then do you spend effort and see what changed.

Japanese car-door assembly gives the first factory contrast. Japanese factories and the Toyota Production System show fit being designed into the process, not saved for the end. Andon helps make the behavior visible; jidoka stays with this factory example, because a hard detail can mislead when it travels.

Detroit carmakers add a second car setting. General Motors in Detroit puts the late-fix habit inside US car making. If the same risk shows up after the setting changes, it is harder to wave off as one odd story.

American Airlines adds airline work. It began in 1926 and is based near Fort Worth. Its boarding guesses show how a routine can feel clear until a new operating guess exposes drag.

## Deep read
The sequence works because the first line frames the guess. If known data leads, the assumption has to answer to something visible. If the assumption leads, the visible fix gets asked to explain a problem it may have helped create.

Gregoire is not trying to slow every choice. He is stopping the repair from taking credit before the premise has been read. That sounds cautious. It is more direct than arguing over price, effort, or style while the first guess keeps steering.

A bad guess charges twice. It charges once when it points capable action at the wrong problem. It charges again when the later correction looks like skill, so the same bad premise gets to travel further.

The Japanese car-door assembly contrast makes this concrete. The Toyota Production System is not background color here; it gives the reader behavior to inspect. Andon belongs in that factory setting because it helps show process, not praise, carrying the lesson.

Jidoka has the same limit. Use the term with Japanese car-door assembly, where the facts place it. In airline operations, a desk choice, or a family chore, carry the plain order instead: what is known, what is assumed, what gets done, what changes.

Detroit matters because one vivid example can fool the eye. General Motors in Detroit gives the car comparison a United States setting, and that difference lets you count what repeated after the place changed. One example can illustrate; two let you compare.

What did you know before the guess started moving? Ask that before the tactic gets a name, a budget, or a team around it. The promise comes back on a number, or it drifts.

At a desk, this is small. Read the choice aloud. Put one known line above the guess. Then make a decision small enough that the result can teach you instead of merely defend you.

## Full read
American Airlines sets the limit on the move. It is an airline operations example, not a factory example in a new coat. Founded in 1926 and based near Fort Worth, the company keeps the boarding routine tied to a real operating setting.

That boundary matters. A useful idea can still be stretched until it starts to lie. Fort Worth helps the reader see where the airline example stops, so a boarding routine does not become a claim about every old routine everywhere.

That is why American Airlines helps after the car settings, not as a louder version of them. Boarding can be defended as obvious. A different operating assumption can expose hidden friction, but only inside the lane where airline operations are doing the teaching. Keep the lane narrow.

Known data is the part you can point to before the guess starts spending. It is not perfect truth. It is the line you can defend before money, time, or attention begins to move.

The four jobs stay separate. Known data names what can be shown. Assumptions name the meaning placed on it. Decisions spend that reading. Results tell what changed. Blend the four into a slogan and the tool goes soft.

Detroit is the hinge. Japanese factories show one way to see the late-fix trap, while General Motors in Detroit gives the United States side its own ground in automobile manufacturing. The value is not brand weight. The value is contrast.

This is also where the move can fail. If you borrow a hard term because it sounds precise, you may make a weak application look grounded. Jidoka does not belong in an airline boarding paragraph. The sequence can travel; the factory word stays home.

Had a writer skipped that limit, the page would sound exact while the evidence drifted. That is the cost of false precision. A true detail can still mislead when it rides with the wrong setting.

You can use the same walk without a title. Read a choice aloud. Ask what fact led, what guess followed, what decision spent the guess, and what result would settle the account enough for the next step.

Sometimes you should do the opposite. If the task is openly a small experiment, move. Name the guess and let the result teach you. Do not wait for a second example when the cost is low and the learning is near.

Assume You Know is not a brake on action. It is a guard against giving the fix more credit than the premise earned. Ask instead: what can be shown before the guess starts moving?

## Key takeaway
Before you argue over a fix, walk backward: put known data before assumptions, make the smallest useful decision, and compare another setting before you trust the pattern.

## Try this now
Read aloud one live decision. Count only the facts you can prove before the first assumption appears; write that number beside the decision.

## Examples
### Example 1: The Early Gap
A pale door gap shows before anyone calls it a failure. Imagine operations lead Gregoire using Japanese car-door assembly before the repair earns credit. In Japanese factories, the Toyota Production System and andon make the signal part of process. The pass slows, but the decision changes: the late fix no longer counts as the win.

What to do: Trace the earliest signal. Name the fit fact, then the guess that put repair at the end.

Why it matters: Count the paid cost. A slower pass is useful only if it stops a larger repair from looking like success.

### Example 2: The Late Standard
The demand is already stated in Detroit automobile manufacturing: reduce visible defects. Suppose a quality analyst is ready to treat late repair as proof that the method works. Detroit automakers catch the miss late. General Motors in Detroit moves the comparison into the United States, and the analyst stops giving full credit to repair alone.

What to do: Compare before you conclude. Use Detroit to ask what repeated after the setting changed, then stop rewarding only the visible fix.

Why it matters: Refuse one-story proof. One vivid example can train your eye, but the second car setting makes the repeated risk harder to dismiss.

### Example 3: The Open Boarding Count
Boarding is already being changed; the demand for smoother movement came earlier. Imagine operations lead Gregoire at an American Airlines gate when his phone buzzes once and the count is still open. The company, founded in 1926 and based near Fort Worth, keeps the work in airline operations. Had he not named the count first, familiar would have passed as new.

What to do: Mark the cost of skipping the count: the old assumption can borrow the new order's shine. Name the count before the order changes.

Why it matters: Treat the result as unsettled. The changed order may expose hidden friction, but it has not earned full credit until something visible changes.

### Example 4: The Half Answer
At check-in, suppose an operations lead in American Airlines airline operations has a half answer. Fort Worth has done its job: the example no longer stands for every organization. What stays broken is the replacement guess. It has not yet shown which hidden friction it removed, so Results remain thin.

What to do: Show why it worked: the old routine had to answer to evidence. Then move Known data before the replacement guess hardens.

Why it matters: Name why it is partial. A better order can retire one bad premise while leaving the new premise underproved.

### Example 5: The Borrowed Word

### Example 6: The Outside Effect

## Review cards
Card 1 — Front: What comes before an assumption?
          Back: Known data. Put the part you can show before the meaning you place on it, then decide and look at what changed.

Card 2 — Front: Why is order not just style?
          Back: Order changes diagnosis. If the guess leads, the fix has to carry a job it cannot carry alone, even when it looks polished.

Card 3 — Front: What does a second setting add?
          Back: It lets you compare what repeated after the setting changed. One example may illustrate; a second one makes the one-off reading weaker.

Card 4 — Front: What does a boundary detail prevent?
          Back: It stops a useful idea from spreading past its evidence. The detail marks where the claim is strong and where you should slow down.

Card 5 — Front: What do you do with a hard detail from one setting?
          Back: Leave the hard detail with the setting that earned it. Carry the plain order of thought into the new setting instead.

Card 6 — Front: How do you keep the lesson from becoming purpose talk?
          Back: Tie the claim to named examples and hard specifics. Purpose language can sound clear while giving the reader nothing to inspect.

## Implementation plan
Title: Put Facts Before Guesses
Core skill: Before a fix gets credit, read the choice aloud and put the known line above the guess. Then make a small decision and say what result would change your mind. Use a second setting when you need to know whether a pattern repeated or merely looked vivid.
If-then 1: [First read the choice aloud; then mark the first unsupported word.] If the unsupported word is doing the steering, then write one known-data line before you act.
If-then 2: [First name the setting you learned from; then name the setting you are applying it to.] If the setting changes, then carry the sequence and leave hard terms where they were earned.
If-then 3: [First list the visible fix; then ask what earlier guess it hides.] If the same fix appears again, then compare a second setting before approving more effort.
24-hour challenge: Spend 20 minutes and read aloud one live decision. Write one known fact, one assumption, one decision, and the result you will count.
Weekly practice: During any week, if a familiar fix appears twice and the second instance looks like success, then run the sequence before approving more effort.
