# Split Happens

## Hook
A founder's return proves nothing until behavior can be traced.

## Fast read
A founder's return proves nothing by itself. Apple, the company Steve Jobs left in 1985 and returned to in 1997, makes that claim useful because you can ask for behavior, not a slogan. The split appears when WHY, the reason being carried, parts from WHAT, the visible output that still moves.

Walmart, the company Sam Walton built from Bentonville before his 1992 death, gives the contrast: no return, just succession. Hold the two together and the lesson changes. The question is not whether the founder is present. The question is who holds WHY, who runs HOW, and what comes back as proof.

## Deep read
Steve Jobs gives you a visible setting because Apple has a before and a return. He left in 1985. He returned in 1997. Those facts do not prove the WHY survived. They make the claim face a hard question: who held the reason while WHAT kept moving?

Use this move when output is being asked to prove more than output can carry. Do not use it to freeze a company around one person, excuse weak HOW, or make every founder story sacred. The move costs time because it slows the easy ending. Sometimes you should run the small tactic and refuse to call it proof yet.

Walmart keeps the claim honest by changing the shape of the problem. Sam Walton died in 1992 after building Walmart from Bentonville. There is no 1997-style return to lean on. You have to ask how scale and founder memory stayed joined, or split, after succession.

The order matters because the first job frames the second. WHY holder names where the reason lives. HOW operators name who turns it into acts. WHAT scale names the visible output as it grows. Succession names the handoff that can separate them.

Every unowned promise costs trust before output can prove anything. If HOW goes first, a tactic is forced to explain a promise it did not make. If WHAT goes first, size starts to look like evidence. Who carries WHY when scale keeps moving?

## Full read
Apple and Walmart teach by pulling against each other. Apple after Steve Jobs's 1985 exit gives the return question. Walmart after Sam Walton gives the no-return question. One makes the reader ask what comes back. The other asks what remains when the founder does not.

Start with Apple because the 1985 exit and 1997 return give the split a place to stand. Treat Apple as color and you lose the evidence. The real use is not to admire the name; it is to ask what behavior could show that WHY, HOW, and WHAT still line up.

Then move to Walmart. Sam Walton, 1992, Walmart, and Bentonville belong together. If those details are flattened into generic succession talk, comparison loses force. Apple becomes the whole proof, which is too much weight for one story.

The useful picture is a scale with Apple on one side and Walmart on the other. Put too much weight on Apple and the return looks like rescue. Put too much on Walmart and succession sounds like a bland handoff. The two together force the missing question: what proof comes back when the founder is no longer the easy answer?

Ford Motor Company, founded by Henry Ford in 1903, belongs here only as the stopping mark. The Model T and Dearborn legacy help mark where the idea stops. They do not make the contrast carry a new main story. They warn you not to make one vivid product carry every founder lesson.

That is the honest limit. The move does not tell you that every old habit should stay, that every founder memory is wise, or that every large output has drifted. It asks for fit. If a detail belongs to Apple, keep it with Jobs, 1985, and the 1997 return. If it belongs to Walmart, keep it with Walton, Bentonville, and 1992.

You can use the same question without title power. A project sponsor leaves. A family routine outgrows the person who began it. A shared task keeps running, but no one can say what it was meant to prove. Cross-out the WHAT for a moment and ask where the WHY now lives.

Had Apple been treated as decoration, the reader would get a famous name instead of behavior. Had Walmart been skipped, the pattern would rest on one attractive example. Had Ford been made to repeat Apple, the stopping mark would vanish. The contrast does the work.

Name the return before you trust the return. First ask who holds WHY. Then ask who carries HOW. Then wait for WHAT to come back where someone can judge it. A return is not proof. It is where proof is owed.

## Key takeaway
When WHAT keeps moving after WHY leaves its holder, do not crown the output; compare return with succession until proof has somewhere to land.

## Try this now
For 5 minutes, cross-out one WHAT claim on your list and say aloud: "Before I defend the output, I will name who holds WHY and who carries HOW."

## Examples
### Example 1: Return Without Verdict
At Apple in 1997, founder Steve Jobs returns, and the easy story wants to close at once. The 1985 exit sits behind it, but the return does not answer the split by itself. It creates the live question: who held WHY after 1985, who carried HOW, and what visible WHAT must now prove? The return point is set; the proof is still open.

What to do: What does skipping cost? It lets 1997 act like an answer. Keep the return unresolved until WHY holder, HOW carriers, and WHAT proof are named.

Why it matters: Where does the bill land? On trust. A famous return can feel like proof before anyone has traced the behavior the claim depends on.

### Example 2: Sloane Pays For Order
At her desk during a hypothetical source pass, writer Sloane looks back after the Apple line finally works. The first pass used the 1997 return as closure; the repaired pass paid a cost by cutting that neat ending. Sloane restores the order: 1985 exit, WHY holder, HOW operators, WHAT scale. Had she not restored that sequence, the return would have done proof's job.

What to do: Why does it work? Sloane pays for clarity by giving up the neat ending, then makes the sequence carry the claim instead of the name.

Why it matters: What was paid? Speed. The gain is a claim the reader can trace through Apple behavior instead of a slogan laid over a famous return.

### Example 3: Chantal Feels Flattened Proof
Chantal reads a hypothetical Walmart paragraph after the comparison has already been approved. Chantal was not part of the choice, but the miss reaches her anyway: Sam Walton, 1992, and Bentonville have been shaved down to another founder story. Why does the lesson feel thin? Because the second anchor lost its own facts, so contrast died.

What to do: Why did it miss? Chantal points the question at the lost facts, not the tone. Keep Walton, Walmart, Bentonville, and 1992 together.

Why it matters: Who feels the cost? The outside reader. Without Walmart's own marker, Apple stops being compared and starts being copied.

### Example 4: Harper Hears Bentonville Drop
Harper hears a hypothetical phone call home with the early signal: Walmart gets described as Apple after another founder. Harper pauses because Sam Walton died in 1992 after building it from Bentonville; that phrase has already dropped the place. The phone buzz cuts through the line. If the place falls out, the contrast falls out. The return point is set; proof has not returned.

What to do: What is the signal? Restore Bentonville before the Walmart line gets compared with Apple, then leave proof open.

Why it matters: What breaks? Contrast.

## Implementation plan
Title: Name the Return Proof
Core skill: Use Name the Return Proof when a visible WHAT is asked to prove a WHY. Name who carries the reason, who makes it real, and what evidence must come back later. Keep hard details with their own source before you compare.
If-then 1: [If output is being used as proof before WHY is named.] If output is being used as proof before WHY is named, then cross-out the output claim and write the WHY holder first.
If-then 2: [If a founder, sponsor, or habit leaves the center.] If a founder, sponsor, or habit leaves the center, then name who carries HOW and what proof must return.
If-then 3: [If a true detail starts traveling into another story.] If a true detail starts traveling into another story, then return it to its own source before you compare.
24-hour challenge: Draft this exact line before your next handoff: "I will cross-out the WHAT until we name who holds WHY, who carries HOW, and what proof returns."
Weekly practice: Cross-out one floating WHAT every Friday and rewrite it as a return proof.
