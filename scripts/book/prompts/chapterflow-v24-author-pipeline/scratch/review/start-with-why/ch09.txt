# Know WHY. Know HOW. Then WHAT?

## Hook
A product could prove the belief, or borrow it. Everyone agreed; no one could name the proof.

## Fast read
A product could prove the belief, or borrow it. Everyone agreed; no one could name the proof. Product lead Vincent hears that strain when a team starts with the shiny offer. The order has to do the work. WHY names the promise. HOW names the conduct. WHAT is the thing people can judge. The iPod, the 2001 digital music player from Apple Inc, worked as proof because the WHY and HOW were already clear enough for the product to show. Dell DJ, Dell's 2003 digital audio player, gives the contrast: plausible WHAT, weaker belief signal. That gap teaches. A product is a receipt, not a reason.

## Deep read
Vincent has to split the jobs before the offer gets praise. WHY says what promise is on the hook. HOW says which conduct will carry it. WHAT is the visible receipt. When those jobs blur, a useful product must carry a load it cannot carry alone.

Here is the honest stop. Do not use this move for a cheap trial, a rough draft, or a choice where the promise is still being learned. Volkswagen Phaeton, Volkswagen's 2002 luxury car built in Dresden, shows why. A luxury car can be impressive and still bend what a name already means. If meaning is still unsettled, shrink the claim before you call the offer proof.

The iPod and Dell DJ contrast does the main teaching. The iPod lets you judge behavior against a promise. Dell DJ keeps you from turning one success into a law. Same broad product space. Different signal. Once Vincent sees that split, he stops asking whether the object was useful and asks what belief it made visible.

The price of leading with WHAT is later pretending WHY was clear. You have paid that price when a launch sounds strong, then no one can say what conduct was supposed to come back. Every unowned promise costs a week of pretending. The fix is plain: write the promise, write the conduct, then let the offer answer.

This works even with no title. When a teammate offers a feature, a service change, or a small deliverable as proof of care, ask for the return path. Who brings the proof back, and when? If no one can say, do not kill the idea. Just stop calling it proof yet.

## Full read
At the return point, Vincent asks a blunt question: why did iPod work as proof when Dell DJ did not? The answer cannot be fame. It has to be order.

Apple iPod gives the reader a visible setting. The offer arrives after a promise and a way to act, so WHAT is not asked to invent the belief. It can show it. If you start there, the product does a modest job well. It lets behavior be judged.

Dell DJ is the cost of skipping the contrast. Dell released Dell DJ in 2003, and the digital audio player was technically plausible. Plausible is not enough. Without the same belief signal, the WHAT can look like a match while returning less meaning.

That is the two-way contrast. The iPod side shows what proof looks like when WHY and HOW are already doing their jobs. The Dell DJ side shows how close a product can come while still not carrying the same signal. You need both or the lesson tilts. One attractive success makes belief look easy. One plausible miss makes usefulness look useless. Held together, they ask the sharper question: what did the product make visible?

Hard details keep the answer honest. The company name belongs with iPod. The Dell name belongs with Dell DJ. A year or place cannot travel just because it sounds firm. When a true detail is moved to the wrong product, the sentence gets stronger while the claim gets weaker.

That discipline also keeps the contrast from becoming a brand contest. Vincent is not asking which company is better. He is asking what each offer can prove without borrowing detail from the other.

Phaeton enters only as a warning against overreach. Volkswagen Phaeton, introduced in 2002 and built in Dresden, was a luxury car and impressive WHAT that could confuse existing meaning. Do not turn that into a third copy of the iPod lesson. Use it as the place where the move stops: impressive output may bend the belief rather than prove it.

You can use the same filter on your own promises. A fast reply, a new offer, or a neat service change may feel like evidence because it is visible. Slow down. First name the promise. Then name the conduct. Then ask whether the visible thing actually brings that conduct back.

Had the iPod been treated as color, the reader would get a slogan with a device beside it. Had Dell DJ been skipped, the rule would rest on one clean success. Had Phaeton repeated iPod, the warning would vanish. The proof comes from comparison, not from noise.

So the return question is small. Who returns with proof, and when? Ask it before you let WHAT do too much. A product may be the receipt. It is not the reason.

## Key takeaway
A product is proof only after WHY names the promise and HOW names the conduct; otherwise WHAT can look useful while bringing nothing clear back.

## Try this now
When a useful offer is asked to stand for belief, circle the proof it must bring back before you let it speak.

## Examples
### Example 1: Vincent Flags Plausible
Product lead Vincent sees the early signal nobody flagged: the blue circle around plausible. In the postmortem, Dell DJ, Dell's 2003 digital audio player, had already played out as a technically sound WHAT that did not carry the same belief signal. Vincent sets the return point for the next claim: name the WHY and HOW the offer must make visible. No one has met it yet.

What to do: Vincent starts by telling you what to do: circle the first useful-looking offer, then write the belief and conduct it must return.

Why it matters: Vincent leaves you the cost: demand the proof before the promise travels, or a plausible product will spend a week pretending for you.

### Example 2: Janet Narrows iPod
Launch lead Janet states the demand before praise starts: the WHAT must prove the WHY, not replace it. iPod, the 2001 digital music player, earns a narrower claim because it fits the challenger-design WHY and HOW. Janet drops the broad line that any player could have done the same job. The claim loses reach, and gains a firmer return.

What to do: Janet risks reach first. She narrows the claim around iPod, then names the conduct that lets the product stand as proof.

Why it matters: Janet pays in reach so the proof gets cleaner. Skipping that cost makes a visible tactic carry a job it cannot hold for you.

### Example 3: Sabrina Catches Luxury
Market analyst Sabrina is already cutting the Phaeton paragraph when the red cursor catches the miss. The demand came earlier: make the product prove the belief. Volkswagen Phaeton, Volkswagen's 2002 luxury car built in Dresden, looked impressive. She reasons out loud that, had they not named the meaning it confused, luxury would pass for proof. Barely caught.

What to do: Sabrina keeps Phaeton as the edge. She names the confused meaning first, then lets the luxury car show where the move can fail.

Why it matters: Sabrina's catch works because the product teaches cost, not success. Move too fast and impressive starts to impersonate proof.

### Example 4: Vincent Names Return
Product lead Vincent opens at the return moment with the question you would ask: is the 2001 iPod digital music player and the 2003 Dell DJ digital audio player contrast enough? It half works. The two products show why order matters, but the draft still has a broken handoff. Each hard detail is in place, yet no one has named who will bring the proof back.

What to do: Vincent asks the return question before approval. He keeps each hard detail with its product, then names the missing proof path.

Why it matters: Vincent's half-win matters because true facts can still do false work. Detail helps only after someone owns what must return.

## Review cards
Card 1 — Front: What are the three jobs before an offer stands as proof?
          Back: WHY names the promise, HOW names the conduct, and WHAT is the visible receipt. Keep them split so the offer answers only the job it can answer.

Card 2 — Front: Why does order matter before proof?
          Back: The promise frames the conduct. If the visible tactic comes first, it has to repair a premise that no one named, and the diagnosis bends.

Card 3 — Front: Why add Dell DJ after iPod?
          Back: One strong product can illustrate the move. Dell DJ adds comparison, so the reader can see what travels and what was only tied to the first success.

Card 4 — Front: What should happen to hard details?
          Back: Keep each name, year, and place with the product that earned it. A true detail in the wrong spot creates false support.

Card 5 — Front: When should an impressive output stop you?
          Back: Stop when the output bends the meaning it is supposed to prove. Impressive WHAT can confuse belief instead of making it visible.

## Implementation plan
Title: Circle the Returning Proof
Core skill: Use Circle the Returning Proof when an offer is being asked to prove a belief. Write WHY as the promise, HOW as the conduct, and WHAT as the visible receipt. Then circle the proof only if someone can say what comes back.
If-then 1: [Name the promise.] If a useful offer starts to lead, then circle the promise it must prove before you discuss the offer.
If-then 2: [Hold the detail.] If a true name, year, or place starts to travel, then circle it and move it back to its product.
If-then 3: [Set the return.] If no one can say what proof comes back, then circle the missing conduct and pause the verdict.
24-hour challenge: Spend 20 minutes with one current promise: circle the offer being used as proof and the conduct that must come back before you trust it.
Weekly practice: Each week, measure one number: how many offers had named conduct before they were treated as proof. Fix the lowest-stakes miss first.
