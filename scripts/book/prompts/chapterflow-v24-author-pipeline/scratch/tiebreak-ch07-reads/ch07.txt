# The Strategy Process: Making the Link with People and Operations

## Hook
1971: Southwest Airlines began service, and its low-cost model had to fit.

## Fast read
1971 is the number to put on the first page. Southwest Airlines started service, and its low-cost model could not live in one line on a slide. Route, fleet, turnaround, and culture had to fit. If one choice fought the others, the fare promise got weak.

That is what a strategy process is for. It does not reward the longest list. It tests a few choices until people and operations can carry them. A strong review asks who leads the work, what proof will come back, and when the room will see it. Some bets should stay small while the team learns. Discovery is honest only when it has a test.

## Deep read
One hard move turns strategy into execution: make each promise answer to a lead, a visible proof point, and a return date. Without that link, the room can agree and still leave the work untouched.

Blair sees the gap in a direct-to-customer plan. The launch note sounds clear, but the delivery page is thin. Dell's direct model in the 1980s and 1990s did not work because the phrase sounded neat. It needed supply-chain speed and working-capital discipline. Direct selling was a work system before it was a promise to buyers.

That is why the review has to ask for the system under the words. Who owns the order flow? What fact will show speed? What money or small test proves the plan can move? A better announcement may help later, but it cannot replace the operating design. Customers meet the work, not the review.

Southwest Airlines began service in 1971; the case adds the fit test. Henri can write low cost in large type, but the words do not hold the plan together. The route plan, fleet plan, turn speed, and culture have to reinforce one another. A route choice that slows turns, or a staffing choice that breaks the fare promise, is not a detail for later. It is the strategy showing a crack.

People belong in the same review. A market choice asks named leaders to do new work, cut other work, or learn fast. If no leader can carry the tradeoff, the plan is still not ready. The answer may be full funding. It may be a small discovery test. Either way, the promise needs a person, proof, and a next check.

## Full read
Some plans are still too early for a full budget, and that is not a failure. A team may need a small test before it knows enough to commit. The failure is calling a trial a firm priority, then acting surprised when no one staffs it. Make the status visible.

Too many priorities create a softer version of the same problem. Each item sounds useful. Each sponsor can defend a piece of the list. Then the operating system gives the real answer: people cannot carry every promise at once. A short list is not weak. It is a way to protect the work that must happen.

A budget line is one way the room tells the truth. If a priority needs money and gets none, the review has learned something. The room can fund it, cut it, or mark it as discovery with a small test. What it should not do is leave the green mark on the page while the budget stays blank.

Clara's Intel review shows the cost of stale facts. Intel's 1980s memory-to-microprocessor shift belongs in the room because market pressure changed what the old plan meant. More ambition would not fix the problem. A strategy review has to let changed facts challenge old commitments before resources keep flowing to yesterday's choice.

Bad news often feels personal. A weak assumption can sound like blame. Early market trouble can feel disloyal to the people who wrote the plan. Leaders have to make facts usable before pride makes them costly. Name the assumption. Show the evidence. Decide what changes. Hidden assumptions become missed numbers later.

Remi's problem starts with a polished market page. The outside case is strong, and the customer need sounds real. The inside page is the weak one. No leader is named. The work system is vague. The funding line is open. Customers do not buy that clean review. They buy what the company can deliver after the review ends.

This is where people, strategy, and operations meet. Strategy chooses the market bet. The people process asks whether the right leaders can carry it. Operations asks whether the system, money, and schedule can bear it. Follow-up keeps the promise from fading once the meeting breaks.

Use the review to end drift. Keep the priority, cut it, fund it, assign it, test it, or change it. More study is useful when the next step is learning. It is delay when the room already has enough to decide. A strategy review earns its time when intent turns into a commitment the next meeting can inspect.

## Key takeaway
A strategy is executable when the market bet, capable leaders, money, and review rhythm line up. If proof is missing, call the next step a test.

## Try this now
Open the most recent planning email for 10 minutes. Watch for the first promise with no named owner. Write exactly: "One promise had no person to test it."

## Examples
### Example 1: Henri Tests The Fit
Henri stands at a review table. Separate cards name route and fleet and turnaround and culture. The low fare line looks clear until one card pulls the others out of shape. He writes Southwest Airlines, 1971, in the corner because its low-cost model depended on choices that reinforced one another. The room has to fix the fit before it can approve the promise.

What to do: Henri should move the weak card out of the approved plan, name the lead who can repair it, and bring back the one fact that shows the choices fit.

Why it matters: Southwest makes low cost concrete. The fare promise works only when the operating choices help one another instead of fighting in separate lanes.

### Example 2: Blair Finds The Missing System
Blair reads the direct-to-customer launch draft beside a blank delivery page. Dell's direct model in the 1980s and 1990s makes the flaw easy to see. The promise needed supply-chain speed and working-capital discipline. A sharper message will not move the computers faster if the order path has no lead.

What to do: Blair should draw the order path, assign one lead to the speed problem, and set the first proof point for the next review.

Why it matters: Dell ties strategy to the system under the promise. A direct offer becomes executable only when the work path can be seen and checked.

### Example 3: Clara Lets New Facts In
Clara stops on a marked market page while the room keeps defending last year's resource call. Intel's 1980s memory-to-microprocessor shift is her pressure test. The old plan may still have pride behind it, but the facts have moved. Another study pass would only keep money attached to the stale bet.

What to do: Clara should put the new fact before the resource call, then ask what stops, changes, or returns as a small test before the team votes.

Why it matters: Intel keeps the review from protecting the old plan. Changed facts need to affect resources, not sit politely beside last year's promise.

### Example 4: Remi Checks The Leaders
Remi clips the customer page to the staffing page and sees the gap at once. The market case is neat, but no leader is named for the supply-chain speed the promise needs. Dell's direct-to-customer model in the 1980s and 1990s is the reminder: buyers do not receive intentions. They receive what the company can build, fund, and run.

What to do: Remi should hold approval until one capable leader is named and one internal proof point is due before anyone calls the page done.

Why it matters: Strategy cannot float above people. The market choice has to meet the leaders and operating system that will carry it after the meeting.

### Example 5: The Add-On Loses Its Green Mark
The finance lead turns the budget page toward the low-cost team. Southwest Airlines is already on the page because its low-cost model depended on fit. Before the turn, every add-on looked harmless. After the turn, one route choice has money, one turnaround change has a lead, and the unfunded add-on becomes a trial instead of a promise.

What to do: The finance lead should remove the green mark from the add-on and write the small test that must earn funding later, then keep it there.

Why it matters: Few priorities protect capacity. An unfunded item may deserve discovery, but it should not pretend to be a full commitment.

### Example 6: The Review Stops Hiding
The review chair leaves the last study memo faceup and circles the assumption no one named out loud. Intel's 1980s memory-to-microprocessor shift is the warning on the table. More analysis now feels careful, but it keeps the old commitment alive. The room has enough to choose the next move.

What to do: The review chair should record the decision, assign the lead, and state the next fact the team must inspect before more study starts.

Why it matters: A review that never decides only delays execution. Early bad news is useful when it changes the plan or the next test soon.

## Quiz
Q1. A hardware start-up promises direct sales, but no one can explain how orders will reach buyers fast. Which repair fits the Dell lesson?
   a) Send buyers a clearer launch email so the direct offer sounds simple, urgent, and easy to trust.
   b) Raise the quarterly target so the team feels stronger pressure to move the computers.
   c) Show how orders will flow, name the person in charge, and pick a speed signal.

Q2. A low-price gym plan has cheap memberships, but hours, equipment, and cleaning choices pull against one another. What should leaders do?
   a) Approve the low-price goal now and let staff settle hours, equipment, and cleaning after the meeting.
   b) Make the price depend on a few choices that support the same cost promise.
   c) Rewrite the slide so every club hears a tighter low-price story.

Q3. A software firm keeps backing an old product even after customer data points toward a new tool. Which review move fits the Intel case?
   a) Set a bigger revenue goal so the old product feels worth backing for one more cycle.
   b) Call it a morale issue and ask teams to push harder.
   c) Use the new customer data before the old product keeps its funding.

Q4. When a retailer approves a new market plan while the regional leads are still unnamed, the rollout date starts to slide. What is missing?
   a) Check whether the named leaders can carry the new work before the rollout is approved.
   b) Score each leader's personality first, then decide whether the market idea feels safe enough to announce.
   c) Keep staffing outside the review so the market case stays clear and upbeat.

Q5. When a cafe chain survey shows demand for faster pickup but the kitchen handoff is still unclear, which question belongs next?
   a) Treat the customer survey as proof once the room agrees the offer sounds attractive enough.
   b) Ask which inside skills and systems can deliver the offer that customers were shown.
   c) Order more demand charts so the kitchen limits stay off today's agenda.

Q6. A service company lists many growth projects, and each sponsor says the larger scale proves the firm can handle them. What should happen first?
   a) Keep the full list so each sponsor sees a favored project on the final page.
   b) Cut the list to the few efforts the teams can staff and review well.
   c) Use fast growth as evidence that the operation can absorb all the initiatives.

Q7. Your pricing team hides a supplier-cost assumption to keep a plan moving through approval, and the margin target now rests on that hidden guess. What is the better move?
   a) Put the cost assumption on the review page before it becomes a missed number.
   b) Change the reward wording so culture talk carries the behavior shift for now.
   c) Keep the assumption private until the sponsor has a smoother answer for the whole room.

Q8. When a priority gets a green mark in the plan but the budget line and owner box stay empty, what does the resource test require?
   a) Pair the priority with funding, one lead, and a review date, or label it a test.
   b) Treat the date as a calendar note while teams search for money after the approval meeting ends.
   c) Approve the priority now so broad support can attract resources after the meeting.

Q9. An early market fact makes a review tense, so the chair suggests another study instead of a decision. Which ending fits?
   a) Keep studying until the weak fact feels less harsh to the team that wrote the plan.
   b) Set the concern aside so the group can leave with one calm message.
   c) Close with a choice, one lead, and the next fact to check soon afterward.

## Implementation plan
Title: Run The Strategy Fit Test
Core skill: Use one plan page to test whether the market choice, leaders, money, and operating work fit. Do not ask whether the goal sounds good. Ask what must be true, who will prove it, and what the next review will inspect.
If-then 1: [When a promise sounds strong but the work behind it is loose] If the promise lacks a capable lead, then write the lead's name and the proof due at the next review.
If-then 2: [When a priority has no money behind it] If the budget line is blank, then mark the item as a test and write the one result that could earn funding.
If-then 3: [When new facts weaken the old plan] If the market fact has changed, then choose what stops, changes, or returns with proof.
24-hour challenge: Teach one person for 5 minutes in your own words. Start with this sentence: "A strategy is real when a tradeoff has an owner, a fact, and a return date."
Weekly practice: Every Friday for 15 minutes, read the newest plan page. Under one priority write exactly: "This is a commitment, a test, or a cut."
