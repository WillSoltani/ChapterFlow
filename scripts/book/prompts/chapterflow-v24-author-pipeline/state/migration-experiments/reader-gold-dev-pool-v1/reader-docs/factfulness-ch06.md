# The Generalization Instinct

> Reader document — factfulness chapter 6. Key-free rendering for reader-experience judgment.

## Hook
A cooking pot on Dollar Street can look more like another pot at the same income than like a richer home in the same country.

## Counterintuition
A category can help you think and still make you wrong. It helps only while it predicts the case in front of you.

## Fast read
The image editor swipes through Dollar Street photos: cooking pots in China, cooking pots in Nigeria, then the Salhi family home in Tunisia. The pots do not line up by flag. They line up by income.

The generalization instinct is the habit of letting one label do too much work. We need labels. Without them, every stove, country, family, or medical rule would arrive as a fresh puzzle. A label is a handle, not a verdict.

The fix is category hygiene. Ask if the label hides differences inside the group, hides likeness across groups, or draws the border in the wrong place. If it fails, split it, compare across it, or replace it.

## Deep read
A map pin can make one word feel small enough to hold. Lay a line from Monrovia to Mombasa: 3,555 miles. Then lay another from London to Tehran: 2,734 miles. The first path is longer, yet people often say Africa as if the label describes one nearby place.

That is how the instinct works. A category saves effort first. Then it starts to feel like knowledge. The mind says, "Africa," "rich country," "natural," or "expert advice," and the label seems to carry facts it never earned.

Split the group before the group splits your thinking. Africa contains wide health and wealth gaps. A country contains rich and poor homes. Women counted in family-planning data may have met need or unmet need. Those are not small footnotes. They change what help would actually fit.

The second move is to compare across the border. Dollar Street, Gapminder's photo project of homes at different income levels, does this with pictures. A pot in China may look more like a pot in Nigeria at the same income level than like a rich home in the same country. The Salhi family in Tunisia is not a symbol for a whole nation. Their home is one case you can look at closely.

The third move is to replace the label when it mispredicts. If "chemical" makes you think artificial and harmful, check the compound and dose. CAS, the Chemical Abstracts Service registry, includes natural toxins and synthetic compounds. Origin is not enough. The useful question is what the thing does.

## Full read
Turn a photocopied medical page until the crib sketch sits straight. The old rule has a baby's body placed prone, belly down. Later SIDS campaigns reversed that kind of advice. A professional category had sounded safe because it was professional. The crib made the cost visible.

The hard part is plain. The answer is not to sneer at experts, countries, cultures, or charts. The answer is to ask what evidence lets the category travel. Advice for one subgroup can harm another. A rule that once sounded tidy can need revision when later evidence challenges the old advice.

The same trap appears in quieter data. One sentence says, "Contraceptive use is low here." A better sentence asks, "How much need is met, and how much is unmet?" Both sentences point at family planning. Only the second finds women who want to avoid pregnancy and lack contraception. One block label would miss them.

A clinic budget can make the same mistake with diabetes. The lazy category says chronic disease belongs to rich countries. The bill says something else: insulin, syringes, transport, and food can pull a fragile household back toward poverty. A disease category is not enough. A price at the counter may decide whether treatment is possible.

A safety memo makes the point with natural and synthetic chemicals. A natural toxin can harm. A synthetic compound can help. Origin may belong in the file, but it cannot carry the whole safety claim. The useful check is compound, likely dose, and known harm.

Category hygiene is not politeness. It is a way to stop a lazy label from becoming a bad plan. If the label is country, ask whether income explains more. If the label is continent, ask whether distance and health-wealth spread are too wide. If the label is expert advice, ask what feedback has arrived since the rule was first taught.

The move also protects good categories from bad use. Income level is useful on Dollar Street because it predicts many household objects. Met and unmet need are useful because they find different groups of women. Distance is useful because it checks a word that has grown too large.

The overcorrection matters too. Some people hear this lesson and start distrusting every category. They split until the pile becomes dust. They hunt exceptions until no pattern can guide action. A category checked to death stops helping.

Keep the useful handle. Drop the fake certainty. A category earns its place when it helps you predict the case in front of you better than a nearer, clearer label would.

## Examples
### Example 1: Dollar Street Cooking Pots
Scenario: At 8:30 a.m., an image editor in the Dollar Street studio desk marks photo row 6-1; Dollar Street photos can put cooking pots from China, Nigeria, and the Salhi family in Tunisia side by side. Income level explains more than country label in that comparison.
What to do: Put the photos in one row and write income above country.
Why it matters: The thread shows how a category can be tested without a lecture. When a reader sorts by nation first, Dollar Street offers a visible cross-check.

### Example 2: Monrovia to Mombasa
Scenario: At 8:31 a.m., an image editor in the Dollar Street studio desk marks photo row 6-2; Monrovia to Mombasa is about 3,555 miles; London to Tehran is about 2,734 miles. A single continent label can hide more distance and variation than the reader expects.
What to do: Draw the distances before using the continent name.
Why it matters: The pause is the skill. A region label may still be useful, but only after the reader checks whether distance and variation have made it too broad.

### Example 3: The SIDS Advice Reversal
Scenario: At 8:32 a.m., an image editor in the Dollar Street studio desk marks photo row 6-3; Old prone-sleeping advice looked professional until later SIDS campaigns reversed it. The point is not to sneer at experts but to date the evidence behind advice.
What to do: Line the pages up by date and mark what changed.
Why it matters: The crib page keeps the warning concrete. Expert advice still deserves respect, but it also needs subgroup evidence and a path for revision.

### Example 4: Met and Unmet Need
Scenario: At 8:33 a.m., an image editor in the Dollar Street studio desk marks photo row 6-4; A low contraception-use rate does not say who wants contraception and lacks it. Met need and unmet need split one broad family-planning category into different lives.
What to do: Add separate columns before making the claim.
Why it matters: The split protects the people hidden by one summary number. A reader learns to ask which part of the category is served and which part is waiting.

### Example 5: Diabetes and Household Cost
Scenario: At 8:34 a.m., an image editor in the Dollar Street studio desk marks photo row 6-5; Calling diabetes a rich-country issue misses families for whom insulin, syringes, transport, and food costs can pull a budget back into poverty.
What to do: Tie the category to household cost, not country stereotype.
Why it matters: The recovery matters because the wrong label sounded reasonable. Chronic disease can still be a poverty risk when care consumes a small budget.

### Example 6: Compound and Dose
Scenario: At 8:35 a.m., an image editor in the Dollar Street studio desk marks photo row 6-6; The Chemical Abstracts Service registry includes natural toxins such as cobratoxin and many synthetic compounds. Origin alone cannot decide safety.
What to do: Check compound, likely dose, and known harm before relying on natural or synthetic.
Why it matters: The memo turns a slippery category into a test. Natural and synthetic can describe origin, but they do not settle danger.
