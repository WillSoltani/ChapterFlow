# Divinity with or without God

> Reader document — the-happiness-hypothesis chapter 9. Key-free rendering for reader-experience judgment.

## Hook
1 of 3 vertical emotions looks ugly; the other 2 explain why sacred life can lift.

## Counterintuition
What looks like an argument about God is actually a fight over moral height. You may reject a creed and still feel that some acts lift a room while others stain it.

## Fast read
A group chat can make the problem visible fast. Someone posts a sneer about religion, someone else fires back, and no one is only debating facts. They are fighting over what counts as high, low, clean, or degraded. Haidt's chapter gives that vertical pull a name: the divinity dimension. It is the felt sense that some acts, places, people, or moments stand above ordinary use. Height can lift a life or harden a wall. The move is to read the feeling before obeying it.

## Deep read
Disgust shows the lower end of the scale first. You can smell spoiled food and pull back before you have words. Paul Rozin, Jonathan Haidt, and Clark McCauley showed why that matters for moral life: the same body alarm can move from contamination to judgment. A person says a practice is filthy, and the body may already be acting as if a boundary has been crossed.

That alarm is useful around rot. It is risky around people. Disgust is a smoke alarm, not a moral judge. If you let it write the whole verdict, you may confuse danger with difference, or care with purity policing. The feeling deserves notice. It does not deserve automatic rule.

The upper end feels different. Moral elevation is the warm lift you feel when you see courage, kindness, or self-giving. Awe arrives when something vast makes your normal frame too small, such as a night sky, a cathedral, a mountain, or a crowd singing as one body. Awe makes the self smaller without making it worthless.

That is why William James matters here. He studied religious experience by its effects on conduct and meaning, not by first proving heaven. Abraham Maslow later gave secular words to peak experiences. The careful claim is wide enough for both: human beings can be pulled upward by moral beauty and vastness, with or without God language.

## Full read
Edwin Abbott's Flatland helps when the whole topic feels fake to you. In Flatland, beings in a two-dimensional world cannot easily picture a third dimension. They may be honest and still miss what another being means by above. Sacredness can work like that. One person hears a hymn and feels lifted. Another hears only sound, habit, or social pressure. Neither reaction proves the other person is stupid.

The hard part is that this dimension cuts both ways. Sacred values are not just strong likes. They mark what feels protected from trade, mockery, or casual use. That can make a life sturdier. A wedding vow, a grave, a flag, a child, a lab standard, or a promise may need a fence around it. If everything is for sale, some part of the person thins out.

Yet the same fence can become a weapon. Purity can support self-control, reverence, and service. It can also fuel stigma. A group can call outsiders dirty, call dissent pollution, and feel noble while it excludes. That is the danger in reducing morality to purity rules. The body may feel clean while the conduct grows cruel.

You can see the safer path in ritual. A ritual is not empty just because it repeats. When bodies move together, voices join, and attention centers on one meaning, private feeling becomes shared. A religious service can do this. So can a memorial, a graduation, a recovery meeting, or a quiet minute before hard work. The repeat action tells the body: this matters here.

The secular reader does not need to fake belief to learn from that. The religious reader does not need to flatten the experience into mere brain events. James gives both sides a fair test: what does the experience do to conduct? Does it make you kinder, steadier, braver, and less trapped in yourself? Or does it make contempt feel holy?

This is where the practice costs something. Nolan feels it when a volunteer team starts joking in the group chat about a neighbor's "gross" apartment. He wants to belong, and the joke would be easy. Instead he writes one plain line: "Let's keep the cleanup about safety, not about calling her dirty." The thread cools. A friend stops replying for the afternoon. Nolan loses the cheap warmth of shared disgust.

That price is real. Reading sacred feelings carefully means giving up the speed of instant contempt. It also means giving up the easy pose that all sacred feeling is superstition. You have to pause at both ends of the vertical scale: when you recoil and when you rise. The question is not whether the feeling is powerful. The question is what kind of person it is asking you to become.

## Examples
### Example 1: Flatland Reply Reset
Scenario: Juliette is halfway through a family group chat reply when her brother drops the word superstition under a funeral photo. Her thumb keeps moving until Edwin Abbott's Flatland catches up with her. A two-dimensional world cannot picture above. She deletes the jab, stops beside the kitchen counter, and starts again with the missing dimension.
What to do: Juliette rewrites the reply around a question: what did the funeral make each person feel was higher than ordinary preference?
Why it matters: The pause keeps disagreement from becoming contempt. Flatland lets you ask what someone may be sensing before you decide they are only wrong.

### Example 2: Coding The Flinch
Scenario: There is barely enough grant time left to finish the coding sheet in the psychology lab. Stanley, the coding assistant, works with one hand on the mouse and one on a cold coffee cup. Paul Rozin, Jonathan Haidt, and Clark McCauley are not abstract names now. The disgust row fills: spoiled food, bodily boundary, moral taint, the same small flinch moving columns.
What to do: Stanley slows on each disgust entry and marks whether the reaction began as contamination, bodily boundary, or moralized aversion.
Why it matters: The vignette makes purity feel bodily before it becomes a speech. That helps you notice when a flinch is evidence, and when it is only a flinch.

### Example 3: After The Hymn
Scenario: The rain smell hangs in the hospital hallway outside the chapel after the evening volunteer shift. Edith, still wearing her ward badge, says, "I do not know what I believe, but that hymn changed how I walked back." Emmanuel answers, "William James would start there." "With proof?" "With conduct. Varieties of Religious Experience asks what it does. Abraham Maslow gives another name for the lift."
What to do: Edith and Emmanuel keep the talk on the aftereffect: whether the lifted feeling makes the next visit gentler, braver, or more honest.
Why it matters: The exchange lets belief and psychology share the room. You can study a peak experience by its fruit without pretending the God question is settled.

### Example 4: Checkout Correction
Scenario: "That is disgusting," someone says behind him at the grocery checkout, and Loic almost nods while the cashier wipes a spill near the card reader. In the after-work line, he hears his own mind slide from sticky syrup to the tired customer who caused it. Paul Rozin, Jonathan Haidt, and Clark McCauley give him the warning word: disgust can travel.
What to do: Loic corrects the jump before speaking. He names the spill as the problem, thanks the cashier, and leaves the customer out of the contamination story.
Why it matters: The recovery happens at the moment of noticing. Purity language can protect hygiene, but it can also make exclusion feel like common sense.

### Example 5: Secular Ritual Memo
Scenario: "Keep it secular," the coordinator says, and Gracie opens a blank memo.

Subject: Before Saturday's cleanup

William James helps us ask what an experience does. Abraham Maslow gives a plain phrase for moments of unity and wonder. So we will begin with a short silence for the neighbor we serve, not a prayer everyone must share.
What to do: Gracie sends the memo with the exact opening words. She protects a shared minute while avoiding language that would force agreement about God.
Why it matters: The written artifact carries the scene. Ritual can coordinate attention and meaning without making every person use the same theology.

### Example 6: Bell And Boundary
Scenario: The brass bell rolls across the community hall floor before Saturday's cleanup starts. Gracie, the volunteer coordinator, sets it upright and uses William James's conduct test: will the pause change how they serve? Abraham Maslow's peak language fits the hush. Nolan smirks, skips the pause, and jokes about the mess. Her side works steadier. His corner gets careless.
What to do: Gracie treats the bell as a boundary for attention, while Nolan treats the same pause as empty theater and loses the room's care.
Why it matters: The contrast shows sacredness as a practice, not a mood. A protected object can gather conduct, or mockery can drain it.

## Review cards
### Card 1
Front: What is the divinity dimension in plain language?
Back: It is the felt vertical axis of moral life: some things seem higher, purer, or sacred, while others feel degraded. Disgust, elevation, and awe mark different points on that axis.

### Card 2
Front: Where does rider and elephant (Ch 1) flatten Flatland's lesson about sacred height?
Back: It can make sacredness look like another force for the rider to steer. The divinity dimension says people may sense a moral height the rider did not invent and should not dismiss too fast.

### Card 3
Front: Why does disgust matter for more than hygiene?
Back: Disgust can move from spoiled food and bodily boundaries into moral judgment. That shift can protect a value, but it can also make another person feel like pollution.

### Card 4
Front: How is elevation different from disgust?
Back: Elevation is the warm lift that follows moral beauty or virtue. It pulls toward imitation and help, while disgust pulls away from what feels contaminated.

### Card 5
Front: Why can William James study religion without settling theology first?
Back: James can ask what the experience does to conduct, meaning, and life. The psychological question is about lived effects, even when the theological claim remains disputed.

### Card 6
Front: What is the danger in purity feeling?
Back: The same feeling that lifts self-control or reverence can also stigmatize outsiders. The test is whether it makes conduct more humane or merely makes contempt feel clean.
