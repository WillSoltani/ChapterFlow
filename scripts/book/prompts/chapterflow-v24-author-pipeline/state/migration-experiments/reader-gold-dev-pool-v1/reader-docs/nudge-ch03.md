# Following the Herd

> Reader document — nudge chapter 3. Key-free rendering for reader-experience judgment.

## Hook
A towel drops toward a hotel floor, then stops when the card names the guests who reused theirs.

## Counterintuition
I used to hear following the herd as an insult. In fact, other people often carry real information, which is why a bad crowd signal can do so much harm.

## Fast read
A towel pauses above the hotel floor. The card by the sink does not scold. It says other guests reused their towels, and that small fact changes the room. You can feel the pull: if people like me do this, maybe it is normal.

Crowds are useful because they save work. When you do not know what is true, safe, or polite, you look around. The crowd is a clue, not a conscience. The danger starts when the clue is false, staged, or too loud.

Bad social cues can cost real things. A group chat says everyone is skipping the form, and the deadline slides by while you wait to seem normal. The move is to ask what signal the crowd is sending before you follow it.

## Deep read
Addison sees the mechanism in a line judgment, not in a slogan. One card on the table is plainly the same length as the sample. Then the confederates give the wrong answer in order, and the room makes error feel safe. You may still see the line, but saying so now has a social price.

Social influence works because other people often know things you do not. A long line outside a cafe may mean good coffee. A quiet hallway may mean the meeting moved. In those cases, copying is not weak. It is a fast way to borrow information.

The trouble is that visible behavior can lie. In Solomon Asch's conformity trials, public agreement pushed many people toward wrong line judgments. The private eye and the public mouth split. Once that happens, a crowd can look like proof while it is only pressure.

Silence has the same problem. If nobody objects in a meeting, each person may read the silence as approval. Yet several people may be uneasy at the same time. Silence can look like agreement. That is how a bad norm survives without many true fans.

You need a simple test. Ask what the crowd actually knows, who can see the same facts, and whether anyone has room to dissent. If the crowd has better information, learn from it. If the crowd is only making fear visible, slow down.

## Full read
Marlowe sits in the lab chair with the switch box close enough to touch. The person in charge says to continue, and the private discomfort does not vanish. It changes shape. The question shifts from what feels right to who seems responsible for the next step.

Stanley Milgram's obedience study at Yale is disturbing for that reason. Participants believed they were giving electric shocks, yet the authority cue kept many moving. Force was not the main tool. The setting made responsibility feel as if it had moved up the chain.

Milgram's lab gives the hard edge of social influence. It is not a story about foolish people. It is a story about normal people reading the room. A lab coat, a unanimous group, or a printed hotel card can all say, in different ways, this is what people do here.

A choice architect has to respect that power. If a sign says many visitors steal wood from a park, it may warn people and also tell them theft is common. If a bill says neighbors use less energy than you, it can make saving feel normal. The same lever can spread repair or spread the mess.

Two norm messages can point in opposite ways. A descriptive norm says what people do. An injunctive norm says what people approve. If the bad act is common, naming the count may help it spread. In that case, approval is the safer signal to show.

A good nudge shows the right crowd. The hotel towel study worked by making a nearby norm visible: guests like you reuse towels. The message did not need a fine or a lecture. It gave the guest a social map for one small act.

Relevance matters. A vague claim about people somewhere else has weak pull. A cue about guests in this room, neighbors on this block, or classmates in this program feels closer. Nearby peers make the signal easier to trust.

Bad cues also travel fast online. Addison sees a class thread fill with jokes about skipping the last upload. Nobody says the assignment is pointless; they only make nonwork look normal. By the time Addison checks the portal, the good version of the file is still on the desktop and the window has closed.

There is a cost. Using social proof means giving up the clean thrill of being independent on every small choice. Julia watches the group chat fill with thumbs-up for a shortcut, then writes one plain line: I am filing the form anyway. She loses a little ease. She gains a chance to keep the deadline from disappearing into the herd.

Julia's line shows the trade. You do not reject the crowd. You check whether it is showing knowledge, fear, approval, or mere repetition. Then you decide what should be made visible next.

## Examples
### Example 1: Towel Thread
Scenario: The laundry cart squeaks through the hotel hall, and operations lead Addison's phone keeps buzzing.
Addison: New towel card?
Isabella: Goldstein, Cialdini, and Griskevicius tested hotel towels.
Addison: Scold people less?
Isabella: Show that guests like them reuse towels.
Addison: So the card names the crowd, not the guilt.
Isabella: Yes. Make the good act visible.
What to do: Addison rewrites the card so it points to similar hotel guests who reused towels. The line tells guests what people like them already did.
Why it matters: Social proof can help when the cue is close and clear. The towel does not need a sermon; it needs a believable signal about nearby guests.

### Example 2: Line Reset
Scenario: Marlowe slides the answer card back an inch before speaking in Solomon Asch's line judgments. The confederates have all named the wrong line, and the table feels hotter than the page. Marlowe stops the routine, looks again at the sample line, and restarts with one quiet sentence: I am going with what I see.
What to do: Marlowe pauses before echoing the room, checks the line against the sample, and says the private judgment without making a speech.
Why it matters: The reset gives the eye a moment to outrank the crowd when the crowd has no better facts.

### Example 3: Switch Box
Scenario: The switch box sits in front of student Graham, cleaner than his thoughts. At Yale, in Stanley Milgram's electric shocks study, the room is neat, the instructions are calm, and the person in charge sounds certain. Graham's shoulder tightens before each press. The shock is not in his body, but the weight is.
What to do: Graham names the shift as it happens: the instruction feels official, but the press is still his action. He asks to stop before the next switch.
Why it matters: Authority does not need to shout. When responsibility feels borrowed from someone above you, even a wrong act can feel procedural.

### Example 4: Quiet Dissent
Scenario: Student Josephine is halfway through rubbing the pencil mark from her thumb when Isabella interrupts in the hallway.
Isabella says, "You saw the same line I did, right?"
Josephine says, "I thought I was the only one."
"Solomon Asch set it up with confederates," Isabella says.
"Then our silence helped the wrong line look true," Josephine says.
What to do: Josephine says the hidden part aloud. She separates what people saw from what they were willing to say in the line judgment room.
Why it matters: Pluralistic ignorance feeds on silence. One plain sentence can show that the supposed consensus was thinner than it looked.

### Example 5: Bad Sign Repair
Scenario: There is not enough time to reprint every card before the hotel check-in rush, and front desk lead Lacey can feel the bad line hardening into policy: Many guests waste towels. Halfway through the stack, she catches her own error. The 2008 Goldstein, Cialdini, and Griskevicius hotel towels study did not work by publicizing waste. It made reuse visible.
What to do: Lacey stops the print run, crosses out the waste line, and replaces it with a local reuse line before the cards reach guest rooms.
Why it matters: A warning can teach the wrong norm. Repair starts when you notice that the sign is making bad conduct look common.

### Example 6: Responsibility Memo
Scenario: Every headset script in the call center tells staff to continue, and nobody has written where judgment returns to the caller. Julia's memo begins: Stanley Milgram's Yale work on electric shocks shows how calm authority can move responsibility out of sight. Add a stop line to the script before compliance feels automatic.
What to do: Julia writes the stop line into the memo: when an instruction feels wrong, the caller must name the concern and ask a supervisor to own the next step.
Why it matters: A written stop point gives people language before pressure peaks. It keeps authority from hiding the human choice inside a script.

## Review cards
### Card 1
Front: What does social influence ask you to inspect before you copy a crowd?
Back: Ask what the crowd is actually showing: better information, real approval, fear of dissent, or simple repetition. The same visible cue can teach or mislead.

### Card 2
Front: How can silence keep an unwanted norm alive?
Back: Each person may dislike the norm but read everyone else's silence as support. The norm survives because private dissent never becomes visible.

### Card 3
Front: What is the obedience lesson from a calm authority cue?
Back: A calm instruction can make responsibility feel shifted upward. The person still acts, but the setting makes the next step feel owned by someone else.

### Card 4
Front: Why can a warning about common bad behavior backfire?
Back: It can turn the bad act into a descriptive norm. A sign that says many people do it may make the behavior seem ordinary instead of shameful.

### Card 5
Front: Why did the hotel towel message need a relevant comparison group?
Back: Guests care more about people who feel nearby and similar. A cue about guests in the same setting gives stronger social proof than a distant statistic.

### Card 6
Front: Recall self-control conflict (Ch 2): name one concrete moment here where it actually bites.
Back: Graham's hand near the Milgram switch box is the moment. He needs a prechosen stop line because pressure is high and private discomfort is easy to override.

## Key takeaway
Social influence is useful information under uncertainty, but it needs design: show the good behavior clearly and avoid making the bad behavior look common.
