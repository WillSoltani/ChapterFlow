# Save More Tomorrow

> Reader document — nudge chapter 6. Key-free rendering for reader-experience judgment.

## Hook
You open payroll to fix one box, and your future paycheck is already voting.

## Counterintuition
The freer plan can be the one with a preset path: a 401(k) form nudges you, even when every option stays yours.

## Fast read
You are staring at a payroll page, trying to answer a question that feels too large for a lunch break: why is it so hard to save for a future you still care about? Richard Thaler and Shlomo Benartzi saw the snag inside the 401(k) form. You may want more saving later, but a smaller paycheck today hurts now. Save More Tomorrow moves the yes to a future raise, so the first step does not feel like a cut. A default is a decision with a head start. The lesson is not that workers are foolish. It is that payroll already has a path for delay, and that path can either leak future money or help it grow.

## Deep read
Tomorrow is easier to promise than today. That is the engine under Save More Tomorrow: the plan asks for a future commitment, then lets payroll raise the saving rate when pay rises later. The worker still has a choice, but the painful part is moved away from the current paycheck. If you have ever kept a subscription because canceling took too many clicks, you know the shape of the problem. Friction can drain money quietly, even when nobody forced you to keep paying.

Brigitte Madrian and Dennis Shea show the companion move through automatic enrollment in a 401(k). When enrollment requires an extra step, delay picks an answer. When enrollment is preset and exit remains open, the easy path changes. The design levers here are defaults, automatic enrollment, future escalation, and opt-out freedom. Each one handles a different part of the same messy choice. Defaults set the starting line. Automatic enrollment keeps delay from choosing nonparticipation. Future escalation links more saving to later pay. Opt-out freedom keeps the whole thing from becoming a shove.

This is why more paper is not enough. A thicker benefits packet may disclose every rule and still leave Valentin unsure what a small payroll change means for his later life. Better mapping means the form makes the consequence easier to see: choose this path now, save more when pay rises later, and leave if it no longer fits. The nudge works when it helps your own future plan survive today's friction.

## Full read
Natalie reads the opt-out line twice because the sentence is short but the consequence is not. She can stay in automatic enrollment, step into Save More Tomorrow, or leave the 401(k) path alone for now. The form is doing what every form does: it chooses an order, a default, and a level of hassle. The only honest question is whether that design serves the chooser, not whether design can be avoided.

That is the hard edge. Save More Tomorrow is not a license for a planner to chase any average result that looks better on a chart. Automatic retirement escalation is justified only when it improves welfare by the chooser's own lights. If Natalie would want more retirement saving once the current squeeze passes, a future raise is a smart time to ask for the increase. If Griffin can exit with a clear click, a phone call, or a simple form, the nudge has kept its promise. An opt-out matters only when exit is real.

The live danger is hidden pride in good design. A benefits team can fall in love with higher participation and stop asking what refusal feels like. That is where Thaler and Sunstein's liberty claim earns its keep. The worker should not need expert help, social courage, or a lost afternoon to say no. If the default is easy to enter but hard to leave, the plan has changed character. It may still raise saving, but it no longer has the same defense.

The idea also respects why people delay. Retirement is a rare choice. Feedback comes years later. The menu can be full of fund names that mean little at the moment of decision. Earlier nudge conditions fit this case almost too well: hard choice, delayed feedback, weak mapping, and little practice. That does not make workers helpless. It means the setting is asking ordinary people to do a hard thing while they are busy, tired, and looking at take-home pay.

So the practical test is simple: who benefits from the path of least resistance, and can the chooser leave that path without a fight? Ask that before you praise a default. Ask it before you remove one. Save More Tomorrow earns its name only when it lets a person protect future income without feeling poorer today.

Here is the counter-case. If Natalie is short on rent and a late fee is due before payday, obeying the escalation path would be a mistake. The right nudge then is not more saving tomorrow. It is an easy exit today, followed by a clear way back when the squeeze has passed.

## Examples
### Example 1: Valentin's Payroll Memo
Scenario: The benefits lead is out, and Valentin, usually the payroll clerk, gets the draft memo. He writes: "Richard Thaler and Shlomo Benartzi call this Save More Tomorrow. The 401(k) increase starts with a future raise, not today's check. Say yes now, and payroll lifts saving later. Say no, and the form must make that plain."
What to do: Valentin rewrites the memo around the worker's felt problem. He names the future raise, the later payroll increase, and the plain no before sending it back.
Why it matters: The memo works because the action lives in payroll, not in a lecture. The worker sees how saving can rise without making today's check smaller.

### Example 2: Jules and Griffin Set Defaults
Scenario: Jules is keying the default into the benefits screen while the preview still spins. He leaves 401(k) enrollment unchecked, sure that active choice proves freedom. Across the table, Griffin follows Brigitte Madrian and Dennis Shea: automatic enrollment is preselected, and the exit link sits beside it. Jules gets clean consent on paper. Griffin changes what delay selects.
What to do: Griffin keeps refusal visible while making enrollment the starting point. Jules records a choice process that treats silence as if it had no effect.
Why it matters: The contrast shows the cost of a blank default. Delay is not neutral when one screen leaves workers out and the other starts them saving.

### Example 4: Skylar's Onboarding Question

What to do: Skylar repeats the rule in plain words: the preset path handles delay, but the exit must stay clear enough for a new worker to use.
Why it matters: The classroom confusion is useful. It separates steering from trapping, which is the line a savings nudge cannot blur.

### Example 5: Natalie's Raise Later
Scenario: Natalie sets the pen beside the raise letter and leaves the 401(k) form untouched. Months earlier, the same question meant less take-home pay right away, and she slid it into a drawer. This time the text thread from payroll says the increase can begin with the raise. She writes back, "Yes, if the opt-out stays easy."
What to do: Natalie accepts the future escalation because the timing changed. She also asks for the refusal step in writing before she treats the yes as settled.
Why it matters: The before and later view shows the mechanism. The same saver can act differently when the plan removes the visible pay cut.

### Example 6: Imogen Tests the Exit
Scenario: The opt-out was already buried in the benefits phone script by the time plan designer Imogen heard the complaint. "If Richard Thaler would call this a nudge, why hide the no?" she asks. Valentin answers, "Because enrollment rises when exit is quiet." Imogen says, "Then we are measuring the wrong thing. Put the no where a busy worker can find it."
What to do: Imogen makes the team test refusal as carefully as enrollment. Valentin moves the opt-out link beside the automatic 401(k) enrollment line.
Why it matters: The dialogue protects the hard edge. A nudge can favor saving, but it loses its defense when exit depends on patience or insider knowledge.

## Review cards
### Card 1
Front: Why does Save More Tomorrow ask for a future commitment instead of a bigger cut today?
Back: A current pay cut hurts now, even when future saving sounds wise. Save More Tomorrow pairs the increase with a later raise, so saving rises without making today's paycheck feel smaller.

### Card 2
Front: How does a default change behavior without arguing with the worker?
Back: It changes the easiest route. Under complexity, many people accept the path already set for them, so a preset 401(k) enrollment can matter even without a speech.

### Card 3
Front: What makes opt-out freedom real rather than decorative?
Back: The exit has to be practical. A worker should be able to refuse without expert help, social pressure, hidden links, or a process so annoying that delay traps them.

### Card 4
Front: Whose welfare is supposed to justify automatic retirement escalation?
Back: The chooser's welfare justifies the nudge, not the designer's favorite metric. Higher saving is defensible when it fits what the worker would want under clearer conditions.

### Card 5
Front: How would nudge conditions change the way you handle automatic retirement escalation?
Back: Nudge conditions say design matters most when choices are hard, rare, delayed, unfamiliar, or poorly mapped. Retirement saving fits that pattern, so payroll design deserves extra care.

### Card 6
Front: What are the four Save More Tomorrow design levers named for payroll saving?
Back: The four levers are defaults, automatic enrollment, future escalation, and opt-out freedom. Together they make saving easier while keeping refusal inside the design.

## Implementation plan
Core skill: Practice checking whether a helpful path is still easy to refuse. Use Test the Exit before you praise a default, copy a preset option, or ask someone to accept automatic escalation. The move is one question: can the person say no without a fight?
If-then (If a default looks harmless because everyone can technically change it): If the default looks harmless, then Test the Exit by walking the refusal path yourself before you defend the design.
If-then (If a choice depends on a future benefit that is hard to picture): If the future benefit feels fuzzy, then Test the Exit and rewrite the choice so today's action maps to the later consequence.
If-then (If someone praises a nudge only because the average result improved): If the result looks better, then Test the Exit by asking whether refusal stayed cheap, visible, and socially easy.
If-then (If your preferred outcome starts sounding like the chooser's best interest): If your own goal gets loud, then Test the Exit by asking what the chooser would want under clearer conditions.
24-hour challenge: Before one real response about money in the next day, run one rehearsal. Stand where you will answer, ask "Could I refuse this without hassle?" and change one word if the answer is no.
Weekly practice: At the end of this week, review one choice you made easier for someone else. Name what changed about your skill at testing the exit, and do not keep a daily log.
