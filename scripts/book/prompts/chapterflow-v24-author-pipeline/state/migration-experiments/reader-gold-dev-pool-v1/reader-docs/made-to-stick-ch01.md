# Simple

> Reader document — made-to-stick chapter 1. Key-free rendering for reader-experience judgment.

## Hook
Rainwater runs under the ER doors while one red tag decides who moves first.

## Counterintuition
A message can shrink and still become harder to use. The useful version is not the shortest one; it is the line that tells people what to protect when pressure forces a tradeoff.

## Fast read
Rainwater pools under the ambulance-bay doors while Megan stares at a triage board full of colors. Chest pain, fever, fall risk, language support, family calls, bed turnover. All of it matters. None of it can matter first. She wipes the board clean and leaves one red-tag rule: the patient whose delay changes the outcome moves first.

That is simplicity. Not fewer words. Not a nicer slogan. A core is simple when it tells people what wins when too many details ask for loyalty at once.

If a sentence cannot settle the next choice, it is still clutter.

## Deep read
Megan's board works because it ranks pressure before pressure arrives. The float nurse does not need Megan beside her for every case. She can look at the red tag, look at the waiting room, and know which tradeoff has already been chosen.

Commanders Intent solves the same problem in a rougher setting. When the field scrambles the plan, Commanders Intent is the sentence people can still carry. Details break. Weather changes. The map lies. A compact mission core lets people improvise without guessing what the mission was supposed to protect.

That is the difference between compact and shallow.

Hoover Adams shows the same discipline in a newsroom. The Daily Record could chase prestige, distant importance, or whatever looked big that morning. A local core told editors what deserved another phone call and what could wait. The rule did not make judgment smaller. It made judgment usable.

The limit is fidelity. A shortcut that drops the condition that makes the message true is not simple. It is a trap with clean wording. A proverb, a schema, or a familiar pitch frame can help memory hold the core, but only while the core still tells the truth.

Megan can still teach the remaining board later. The first job is to make the next urgent choice survivable.

## Full read
Mathieu unfolds a salt-streaked route map before the first snow. Contractors crowd around a folding table at 4:42 a.m., coffee cooling beside radio call signs. Every road has someone angry behind it. Side streets, storefronts, steep lanes, the mayor's block, the school loop, the clinic road. If Mathieu treats them as equal, the plows will spend the morning proving that equal importance is useless.

He circles the school and clinic routes in black. Then he says the sentence that matters: buses and dialysis pickups beat complaint volume. No one loves it. Everyone can use it.

A strong core does not make the world neat. It makes the next tradeoff less mysterious. That is why Southwest Airlines matters here. Southwest's low-fare priority works because it settles tradeoffs before the next local question arrives. A gate agent, a scheduler, or a manager still faces messy choices, but the low-fare core tilts the answer before the debate begins.

Ask a harder question about your own message. What should someone sacrifice first when not everything can survive? If you cannot answer that, you do not have a core yet. You have a pile of preferences waiting for the first stressful hour to expose them.

There is a danger in worshiping compact language. A short sentence can feel brave because it is easy to repeat. It can also cut away the very edge that kept the claim honest. Product schemas and movie-pitch frames help because they give a new thing a known shape. They fail when the known shape drags in the wrong promise. The mind likes handles, but the handle must still be attached to the right tool.

Ingrid learns that behind the bookstore register. The holiday order cart is full of maybe titles, all defensible, all costly. She can keep debating taste, prestige, margins, and staff favorites until the invoice stack decides for her. Instead she names the store's core for December: books that work as local gifts. The cart gets lighter. Payroll in January gets a chance.

Compression should make good action easier, not just make memory happier. That is the test. If the reduced version helps people choose when details collide, keep cutting. If it starts hiding the condition that matters, put words back.

The same standard applies in smaller rooms. Daniel can rehearse every blackout cue for the community theater cast, but darkness steals detail from nervous volunteers. His useful sentence is hands off the stairs until work lights are up. Once that core is clear, every actor and stagehand can protect the same thing without remembering the whole cue sheet.

The core is the part of the message that still knows what to do when the rest of the message falls away.

A short message earns its keep only when someone can use it without you standing nearby. That is when compression becomes service instead of decoration.

## Examples
### Example 1: Megan Leaves One Red Tag Rule
Scenario: Rainwater pools under the ambulance-bay doors at 11:40 p.m. while Megan, the charge nurse, studies the laminated triage board. Four color codes, red tag stickers, and three overflow rows are confusing the float nurse beside the waiting-room chairs. Megan has to choose before the next ambulance backs in: keep every priority visible, or erase the board and leave one red-tag rule for who moves first.
What to do: Erase the codes that compete for first place. Write the one red-tag rule that names the delay that changes outcomes, then use the stickers only to support that rule.
Why it matters: The float nurse needs a tradeoff winner, not more accurate clutter. One governing rule lets her act when Megan is across the room and the waiting area gets louder.

### Example 2: Mathieu Circles the School and Clinic Roads
Scenario: At 4:42 a.m. inside the public-works depot, Mathieu spreads a salt-streaked paper route map across a folding table. Black grease pencil rings the school loop and clinic road while radio call signs crackle from coat pockets. Contractors are already naming side streets that always complain. He must decide whether to let complaints steer the plows or make those two circles outrank every call. The school superintendent is already on the radio, and Mathieu keeps his hand on the map until the drivers repeat the priority back.
What to do: Name the route priority before the first angry call arrives. Circle the roads tied to buses and dialysis, and let that compact rule settle the morning order.
Why it matters: Every driver will face a local exception. A clear core keeps the routing from being rewritten by whoever complains loudest once the snow starts.

### Example 3: Ingrid Lightens the Holiday Book Cart
Scenario: The smell of cardboard and pine-scented candles hangs behind the register as Ingrid stacks invoice sheets beside a rolling book cart. By noon, the cart is full of maybe titles: prize novels, staff pets, clever imports. After checking January payroll, she pulls every book that does not fit the local-gift table and leaves the cart half full. A sales rep is waiting near the candle display, and Ingrid keeps the invoice stack visible so the choice stays tied to payroll.
What to do: Use the store identity as a buying filter. Keep the books that serve local gifts, and remove the defensible titles that do not settle the holiday job.
Why it matters: Shelf space and cash are tradeoffs. The core keeps taste, prestige, and staff enthusiasm from pretending they all deserve the same answer.

### Example 4: Daniel Calls the White Light Rule
Scenario: "Nobody moves on the stairs until I call white light," Daniel says into the empty house. Gaffer tape marks the painted steps. Two volunteers are waiting for headset cues, and the teenagers keep asking about their entrances. Daniel must choose during rehearsal: explain every blackout cue again, or make the single safety rule carry the scene. A ladder sits half-folded in the wing, and one volunteer is already reaching for the dark stair rail.
What to do: Stop the cue lecture. Repeat the white-light rule, mark the stairs, and make every other instruction secondary to keeping hands and feet off the steps in darkness.
Why it matters: A blackout breaks detailed memory. One safety core gives volunteers and actors the same decision rule when the headset chatter turns messy.

### Example 5: Catarina Finds the Cold Stock Priority
Scenario: It took Catarina three Saturdays to see why the checklist kept failing. Wax paper, sample knives, the digital thermometer, and the cold case drawer all mattered before the market hall filled. New staff tried to do the whole ritual at once. Catarina is about to choose what to teach first: the full opening dance, or the one rule that keeps cold stock cold. The cheese case alarm chirps once, which is exactly the kind of detail the old checklist buried.
What to do: Teach the cold-stock rule first. Let the thermometer and drawer routine carry the morning, then add the display details after the health risk is protected.
Why it matters: The staff cannot honor every step equally during a rush. A core priority keeps the most costly failure from being buried inside a long checklist.

### Example 6: Akira Cuts the Audio Tour to One Question
Scenario: What should a visitor remember after the headphones come off? Akira stands beside a bronze gallery label with an audio transcript full of dates, donors, wars, and techniques. The visitor flow counter shows people quitting after room two. He must choose before recording day: preserve the full tour, or give each gallery one question and cut every clip that wanders. The bronze gallery opens in two weeks, and the recording booth is already booked for Friday morning.
What to do: Choose the one question each room should answer. Keep clips that help answer it, and remove facts that are accurate but do not guide the visitor through the gallery.
Why it matters: Equal importance exhausts attention. A gallery question gives visitors a compact schema, so the facts attach to a shape they can carry out of the room.

## Review cards
### Card 1
Front: A message is short but no one knows what to do when priorities conflict. What is missing?
Back: A core priority. Simple means the message names what should win when tradeoffs appear, not merely that the wording is brief.

### Card 2
Front: You are cutting a policy for a busy team. What test tells you whether the cut helped?
Back: Ask whether the shorter version settles a real choice the team will face. If it only sounds cleaner, keep working.

### Card 3
Front: A familiar comparison makes your product easy to grasp but creates the wrong expectation. What do you do?
Back: Keep the schema only if you can add the boundary that keeps it true. A sticky frame that misleads is not simple. It is distortion.

### Card 4
Front: A supervisor wants every detail in the morning brief because all details matter. What should she choose first?
Back: Choose the detail that governs action when the other details collide. The point is not to deny the rest. It is to rank what must survive pressure.

### Card 5
Front: What does Commanders Intent teach about simplicity?
Back: The plan may break, but the mission core must still travel. A simple message is the part people can use after the details stop matching reality.
