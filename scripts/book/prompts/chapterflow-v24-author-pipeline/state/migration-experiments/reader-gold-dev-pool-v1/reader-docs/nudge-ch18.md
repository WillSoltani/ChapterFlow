# The Real Third Way

> Reader document — nudge chapter 18. Key-free rendering for reader-experience judgment.

## Hook
You are one blank checkbox from a default that can spend rent money before you notice.

## Counterintuition
Why does doing nothing still steer people? Because a default, a menu, or a buried exit can choose for you while claiming to stay neutral.

## Fast read
Why does Anna's blank form steer her before anyone signs? She sees three open boxes, but the bold path is already chosen. If she misses the small exit line, next month's money moves. A menu is never blank.

The real third way starts there. You do not have to pick between doing nothing and forcing everyone into one rule. The middle path asks a harder design question: since the setup already steers, does it steer toward the chooser's welfare, and can the chooser refuse without a trap?

A good nudge is not a shove with nicer words. It changes the easy path while keeping the exit real. Freedom needs a working exit.

## Deep read
Gordon finds the mechanism in the small parts of a form: the prefilled box, the order of choices, the warning that appears too late. These parts shape behavior before anyone gives a speech. People often follow the path that is easiest to see, easiest to finish, and least awkward to leave alone.

That is why libertarian paternalism matters. Libertarian means choice stays open. Paternalist means the design aims to help the chooser, by the chooser's own lights. Choice architecture means the layout of the decision: defaults, menus, warnings, order, and friction. Opt-out means the person can leave the path without a real penalty.

The 2003 American Economic Review article called this stance Libertarian Paternalism. The compact claim is not that designers know best. It is that designers cannot avoid arranging the choice. The cafeteria, website, school office, employer, and state agency all put something first. They can do that carelessly, or they can do it with a welfare test and an exit test.

Colette has felt the cost of a bad setup. A payment site leaves the cheap plan hidden under a longer menu, and the default fee hits before rent clears. Nobody yelled at her. Nobody banned the cheaper plan. The design still made one path easier than the rest.

A real third way refuses two lazy answers. The first says, "Just give options." That misses how delay and clutter pick for people. The second says, "Just mandate the best answer." That misses how personal welfare can differ. Nudging is stricter than both. It asks what the likely path does to a real person, then asks whether a real exit remains.

## Full read
Maureen can see why the phrase sounds odd. Libertarian paternalism puts two words together that seem to fight. One word protects choice. The other word admits that people can make choices they later regret. The University of Chicago Law Review title, Libertarian Paternalism Is Not an Oxymoron, presses on that clash instead of hiding it.

The title works only if both halves stay alive. If the design improves welfare but makes refusal costly, the libertarian half has been lost. If the design keeps every option open but leaves people lost in clutter, the paternalist half has been abandoned. The real third way is not a slogan. It is a test you can run on a form.

Start with the libertarian part. Can the person see the other route? Can they leave the default without a maze? Can they say no without a fee, delay, shame, or hidden penalty? A checkbox that technically exists but sits three screens deep is not much freedom. Exit is the proof of freedom.

Now test the paternalist part. Whose welfare is being served? A nudge is defended only when it serves the chooser, not the designer's favorite metric. A city may like clean enrollment numbers. A company may like fewer calls. A school may like a faster portal. Those gains do not settle the case. The design should help the person who has to live with the result.

This is why disclosure alone often fails. More pages can look respectful while making the choice harder. A credit form can list every fee and still hide the total cost at the moment of decision. A health plan can disclose every term and still leave a tired person unable to compare real drug costs. Useful disclosure puts the key fact where the choice is made.

Defaults have the same double edge. A default can rescue a person from delay when delay would quietly choose the old path. It can also become pressure if exit is slow or embarrassing. The default is not good because it improves a spreadsheet. It is good only when it helps likely choosers and stays easy to refuse.

The American Economic Review piece keeps the idea compact: a better path can preserve choice when the old path was already steering. The Law Review version keeps the objection in view: do not call it liberty if refusal works only for experts with time.

Use the opposite move when the stakes require a hard rule. If a factory is dumping poison, a clean label is too weak. If fraud strips people of real consent, a prettier menu is not enough. A nudge earns trust by staying easy to refuse. Some harms need a ban, a right, or direct enforcement instead.

## Examples
### Example 1: Anna's Rebuilt Form
Scenario: The committee chair pushes the draft enrollment form toward Anna after the usual reviewer loses network access mid-call. The old version copied the University of Chicago Law Review puzzle in Libertarian Paternalism Is Not an Oxymoron: choice stayed open, yet the opt-out line hid under dense text. Later, Anna moves the exit beside the default. The same box now helps without quietly spending next month's rent.
What to do: Anna keeps the helpful default but moves the refusal path into view. She changes one variable: the chooser can leave the path before money moves.
Why it matters: The contrast shows the real test. A default can guide, but its freedom claim depends on whether exit is visible and cheap at the moment of choice.

### Example 2: Gordon's Audit Find
Scenario: Gordon is deleting the last objection from the policy memo when the appendix catches him. The American Economic Review version of Libertarian Paternalism says the path may be guided, not trapped. In the records, the opt-out takes a mailed form while enrollment takes one click. That one find changes the draft from a nudge into pressure.
What to do: Gordon stops the deletion and marks the mismatch. He asks that refusal use the same channel as enrollment before the memo calls the design libertarian.
Why it matters: The audit turns a smooth claim into a testable one. The exit route is not a detail when it decides whether choice is real.

### Example 3: Colette's Stuck Tab

If the cheap plan hides under broken filters, freedom is thin.
What to do: Colette refuses to treat the open menu as useful until the costs compare on one screen. She names the broken filter as part of the choice.
Why it matters: Her inner check separates formal options from usable options. A person can have choices and still be steered by friction.

### Example 4: Maureen Budgets Attention
Scenario: The projector hum warms the community center hall before website planner Maureen budgets the page space for Saturday's launch. The 2003 American Economic Review frame gives her four claims to protect: libertarian, paternalist, choice architecture, opt-out. She has one top screen, three long disclosures, and no extra attention from users. The planning choice earns the first line.
What to do: Maureen assigns the top screen to the default, the plain welfare reason, and the exit route. The longer legal text moves below the live choice.

### Example 5: Gabrielle's Stamp
Scenario: Benefits reviewer Gabrielle sets the approval stamp down mid-page at the benefits approval desk and turns the file sideways. The default will help people who delay, but refusal requires a call during work hours. She weighs two bad options: approve a flawed help now, or hold it while delay keeps hurting others. She chooses repair because opt-out has to be practical before the stamp means freedom.
What to do: Gabrielle withholds approval until the refusal route works outside office hours. She protects the default by fixing the exit first.
Why it matters: The dilemma keeps both halves alive. Welfare gains matter, but they do not erase the need for realistic refusal at the point of use.

### Example 6: Joan And Margaux Check Welfare
Scenario: The enrollment email had already gone out by the time the complaint reached Joan.
Joan: "American Economic Review called it Libertarian Paternalism. Completion is up."
Margaux: "For whom?"
Joan: "The office has fewer calls."
Margaux: "That is our welfare. Does the chooser get a better path?"
Joan: "Only if the exit link sits in the same message."
What to do: Joan revises the next email so the benefit, default, and exit link sit together. Margaux keeps the test aimed at the chooser, not the office.
Why it matters: The exchange blocks a common drift. A nudge is not justified by a smoother back office if the person choosing loses a real way out.

## Review cards
### Card 1
Front: What makes libertarian paternalism a third way rather than a polite mandate?
Back: It guides the likely path toward the chooser's welfare while keeping other paths visible and practical. If refusal is costly, hidden, or shaming, the design has crossed into pressure.

### Card 2
Front: How do the four Real Third Way design levers fit together?
Back: Libertarian protects open choice, paternalist aims at chooser welfare, choice architecture names the setup, and opt-out checks whether refusal stays realistic.

### Card 3
Front: Which matters more in this situation, self-control conflict (Ch 2) or libertarian paternalism, and why?
Back: The exit test matters more here. Ch 2 explains why delay can beat intention, but this policy question asks who benefits and how easily a person can say no.

### Card 4
Front: Why can disclosure fail even when every fact appears somewhere?
Back: Disclosure fails when facts arrive away from the live choice. A fee hidden in extra pages may be true, but it does not help the person compare costs before clicking.

### Card 5
Front: What question separates chooser welfare from designer preference?
Back: Ask who benefits by their own lights. Fewer calls, cleaner reports, or faster forms may help the office, but the nudge needs to help the person living with the result.

### Card 6
Front: When should you use the opposite of a nudge?
Back: Use a hard rule when refusal, safety, or consent cannot be protected by a better menu. Fraud, poison, or direct harm may need a ban, right, or enforcement.

## Implementation plan
Core skill: Practice checking a choice setup before you praise it as free. Use Check Refusal Before Praise: name the easy path, name whose welfare it serves, then check whether refusal is visible and practical. If any part is missing, the design needs repair before it earns trust.
If-then (If a form has a prefilled box): If the box is already chosen, then use Check Refusal Before Praise. Name what happens if you do nothing and where the refusal path sits.
If-then (If a menu leaves you tired before you choose): If the menu drains your attention, then use Check Refusal Before Praise. Ask which fact must move beside the choice so comparison is possible.
If-then (If someone praises a default for better numbers): If the numbers sound good, then use Check Refusal Before Praise. Ask whether the chooser gains by their own lights and can still leave cheaply.
If-then (If disclosure arrives as a long separate file): If the key fact is off to the side, then use Check Refusal Before Praise. Move the cost, deadline, or exit instruction to the live decision.
24-hour challenge: The next time you open a form or menu, find the working exit before you continue. If exit takes a call, fee, mailed form, or hidden page, mark the setup as pressure, not free choice.
Weekly practice: This week, teach one person Check Refusal Before Praise in your own words. Use a real form or menu and explain easy path, chooser welfare, and opt-out.
