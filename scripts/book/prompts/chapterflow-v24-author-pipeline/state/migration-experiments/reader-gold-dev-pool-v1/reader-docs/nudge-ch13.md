# Improving School Choices

> Reader document — nudge chapter 13. Key-free rendering for reader-experience judgment.

## Hook
4 design levers still leave 1 hard truth: a school menu fails when families cannot use it.

## Counterintuition
Choice can grow and shrink at once: more schools can mean less choice when the path is hard to follow.

## Fast read
A parent can miss a school deadline while doing everything that looks responsible. The tab is open, the list is long, and the one upload quietly slides past. The cost is not laziness. It is clutter built into the choice. A menu is not a map.

School choice sounds like freedom because it offers families options. But if a family cannot compare school quality, ranked choices, lottery odds, transportation, and deadlines, the menu rewards the people with more time and better inside knowledge. The busy family pays for clutter.

Caroline Hoxby's school-choice work and New York City's high school admissions problem point to the same fix. Give families search support, clear data, ranked choice, and default assignment that can be left. The aim is not to pick for them. The aim is to make the real choice visible.

## Deep read
Travis watches the New York City high school form refuse one file upload, and the broken page teaches the mechanism. Complexity changes the path of least resistance. The family may still have many schools on paper, but the path in front of them can become delay, a weaker list, or the current assignment.

That is why structured school choice is a design problem. Search support means the system helps families find schools that fit. Clear data means the facts are plain enough to use before the deadline. Ranked choice means families can state more than one preference without guessing one perfect answer. Default assignment means the system has a backup, but the backup must not trap anyone.

Caroline Hoxby's work keeps the policy claim from floating away. School productivity and choice are not slogans in that frame. They are claims that need data and real families using real rules. If the rules are legible only to the most prepared parents, the system can widen the gap it meant to close.

New York City shows the pressure in everyday terms. A ranked high school list is not just a form. It asks a family to read quality, fit, distance, odds, and timing all at once. Clearer design does not remove judgment. It keeps judgment from being buried under paperwork.

A ranked list adds a practical guardrail. Families can name more than one school, but the tool can still punish them when odds, travel, and deadlines sit on separate pages. Evidence matters. But evidence does not license a designer to hide the exit.

Good choice design serves the chooser, not the designer. Freedom shrinks when exit is fake.

## Full read
Harriet folds the printed school list in half during a training session and asks which half a rushed family will actually read. The room says the top half. That answer is useful and dangerous. Useful, because salience matters. Dangerous, because whatever lands at the top now carries power.

Here is the counter-case. Better architecture can help families, but it can also become a quiet way for officials to pick winners while saying everyone chose. Judge a ranked list, search screen, or default by a hard test. Does it help families choose by their own lights, and can they refuse without paying a hidden price?

Start with search support. A family should not need a private guide to find which schools match a child's program needs, commute, and admissions rules. If the search page makes transportation hard to see, it can make a far school look better than it is. That is not better choice. It is a blind spot with a polished form.

Clear data has the same edge. More documents do not mean better disclosure. A family buried in separate pages for quality, deadlines, lottery rules, and travel time may have less usable information than a family looking at one plain comparison. Disclosure works only if it reaches the person while the decision is still live.

Ranked choice is helpful because it lets families express several preferences. It also asks them to think in sequence. When the rules stay opaque, parents may rank timidly, copy rumors, or leave a good option off the list because the odds feel unknowable. The system then looks fair on paper while rewarding families who can decode it.

Default assignment is the sharpest tool. It protects against a blank form or a missed deadline, which matters because delay is not neutral. But a default can drift into coercion when exit is hard, late, or costly. A family assigned to a school should be able to see the reason, the alternatives, and the next step for changing course.

The hard edge is not anti-choice. It is anti-fake-choice. A formal menu can still fail if the family needs expert time to understand it. Caroline Hoxby and New York City admissions keep the lesson grounded: policy works through actual rules, forms, odds, and deadlines, not through the word choice alone.

Now the limit. Suppose Sabine's child is thriving at a smaller school with a sibling, a known counselor, and a reliable bus, while a search screen ranks a higher-scoring school first. Obeying the screen would be a mistake if it treats test data as the whole welfare story. The right nudge makes the facts usable. It does not replace the family's reasons.

## Examples
### Example 1: Two Ranked Lists
Scenario: Travis is typing the last ranked choice into the New York City high school admissions portal while the upload wheel spins again. He sorts by commute first and sees the deadline banner in the same view. Helene keeps a stronger school on a paper list but searches three separate pages for lottery rules, travel, and deadlines. Travis submits a clear list; Helene finds the closed upload after dinner.
What to do: Travis follows the portal path that puts commute, ranked choices, and the deadline together. Helene's lost upload shows what happens when the same facts live apart.
Why it matters: The contrast makes the cost visible. Choice improves when the system lowers search friction before the deadline, not after a family has already missed it.

### Example 2: Harriet's Map Line
Scenario: The folder zipper sticks, and Harriet has to pull twice before the school choice packet opens. The parent on the phone keeps saying Caroline Hoxby proves choice works. Harriet stops him gently. "A choice list is not help until the family can read the map." She walks him from school productivity to fit, odds, travel, and the next real deadline.
What to do: Harriet turns the call from a debate about school choice into a map check. She asks for the facts the family can use before the next deadline.
Why it matters: The reframe keeps the policy honest. Evidence about schools matters, but it helps families only when it becomes a path they can follow.

### Example 3: Elijah's Admissions Lesson
Scenario: Elijah hears a dry marker squeak across the classroom board, and the phrase "New York City high school admissions" still feels too broad. "If families rank schools, why study the assignment rule?" he asks. The instructor circles ranked choices and deadlines. The lesson is that outcomes depend on rules, not just the brochure families see.
What to do: Elijah asks which rule would move a family from one listed school to another before he judges whether the menu helped them.
Why it matters: The class confusion is the lesson. Evidence can test school options, but it does not erase the need for usable admissions rules.

### Example 4: Sabine Adds Travel
Scenario: Parent Sabine slides the bus pass back into her wallet before she changes the ranked list. Earlier, her New York City high school admissions sheet showed program names and deadlines, so one school looked easy to rank high. Later, she adds travel beside each choice. The top line no longer looks harmless when the trip would eat the afternoon.
What to do: Sabine keeps the school on the list but moves it lower after adding travel time beside the deadline and program fit.
Why it matters: The same family makes a better call once the missing variable is visible. Clear data means the key fact sits where the ranking happens.

### Example 5: Joanne Checks Exit
Scenario: Director Joanne marks the default school line before the New York City neighborhood meeting starts.
Joanne: "The backup protects families who miss the deadline."
Lola: "Can they leave it without a maze?"
Joanne: "One form, visible date, same phone number."
Lola: "Then it is a nudge. Hide the exit, and it becomes pressure."
What to do: Joanne keeps the default assignment only after the exit path is visible on the same notice, with the date and contact route in plain view.
Why it matters: The exchange guards liberty. A default can help busy families, but refusal must stay practical for the design to count as a nudge.

### Example 6: Brielle Thinks Twice
Scenario: Parent Brielle has barely started the kitchen-counter reply when the New York City assignment email lands. Her first thought is relief: the default school has strong numbers. Then the other facts arrive in order. The late bus. The sibling at another school. The ranked choice she meant to defend. If the design helps by her family's lights, it should make those reasons easier to state, not harder.
What to do: Brielle pre-writes the reply around her family's reasons: ranked preference, travel, sibling fit, and the request for a practical exit from the default.
Why it matters: Her inner check separates welfare from the designer's favorite metric. A school nudge is justified by the chooser's reasons, not by a tidy average.

## Review cards
### Card 1
Front: What makes a school-choice menu usable rather than merely formal?
Back: The family can compare school quality, admissions odds, deadlines, transportation, and fit at the moment they rank or submit choices. A bare list is not enough.

### Card 2
Front: Why does search support count as a nudge in school choice?
Back: It rearranges the task so useful facts sit where families need them. Families still choose, but the portal or adviser keeps delay and rumor from doing the work.

### Card 3
Front: When does a default school assignment stop preserving freedom?
Back: It stops preserving freedom when leaving it is hidden, late, costly, or vague. A backup assignment helps only if families can see and use the exit.

### Card 4
Front: What does New York City high school admissions add to the school-choice lesson?
Back: It shows the design problem inside ranked choices, deadlines, travel, and assignment rules. Families need those facts together while the ranking is still live.

### Card 5
Front: Bounded rationality (Ch 1): what would it make you change on a school-choice form?
Back: It would make you expect missed facts under pressure. You would put deadlines, odds, travel, and exit steps in the same view before asking a family to make a careful ranking.

### Card 6
Front: Name the four Improving School Choices design levers and what they protect.
Back: The levers are search support, clear data, ranked choice, and default assignment. They protect usable choice by making the path clearer while keeping refusal practical.
