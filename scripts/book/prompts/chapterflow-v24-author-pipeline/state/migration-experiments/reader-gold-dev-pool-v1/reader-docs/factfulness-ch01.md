# The Gap Instinct

> Reader document — factfulness chapter 1. Key-free rendering for reader-experience judgment.

## Hook
Hans Rosling's bubble chart shows why the old rich/poor split missed the 68% in the middle.

## Counterintuition
It feels clear to split the world into rich and poor. Rosling's correction is that the split often hides the crowded middle, where most lives and many changes sit.

## Fast read
Rosling's old bubble chart begins with a real split: in 1965, many countries sat in a low-survival, high-fertility box. The picture looks like two worlds. Then the 2017 chart arrives, and the clean split breaks. Many bubbles have moved into the middle or upper range.

The gap instinct is the habit of cutting the world into two camps and picturing an empty space between them. It feels tidy. It is often false. The gap is often a bad map.

Use Find the Middle, a quick check that asks where the largest group sits before you name the whole world: ask where most people are before the loud extremes take over. Keep severe poverty in view, but do not let it stand for everyone outside rich countries.

## Deep read
A line on a chart can make a crowd disappear. When the mind sees two clumps, it wants two names. Developed and developing sound practical, but they train the eye to expect a gulf. In 1965, that picture had some visual support. By 2017, the bubbles no longer fit the old split.

Cover the labels on the 2017 health chart and the old words become harder to defend. The old words say two camps. The dots say movement. Some countries still face harsh child mortality and poverty. Many others sit between the poorest and the richest, with better survival, smaller families, and uneven but real gains.

That is why Rosling's four income levels matter. They are a rough ladder of daily income, not a rank of human worth. Level 1: less than $2 per day. Level 2: $2-$8 per day. Level 3: $8-$32 per day. Level 4: more than $32 per day. A family on Level 2 may have a bicycle but no steady water. A family on Level 3 may have a motorbike and a fridge. Most people live between the drama.

This does not make the world fine. It makes the map usable. If you start with the poorest and richest pictures, you may miss the billions whose lives are neither disaster nor comfort. Find the Middle asks for one extra step before judgment: locate the crowd, then look at the edges.

## Full read
Put Sweden 1869 beside Saudi Arabia 1960 and the old ranking starts to wobble. The numbers feel shocking: 249 and 242 child deaths per 1,000 births. If the comparison stops there, the story can sound like national character: one country ahead, the other behind.

The dates change the story. Sweden needed 77 years, from 1869 to 1946, to get below 35 child deaths per 1,000 births. Saudi Arabia made a similar move from 1960 to 1993, in 33 years. The old gap picture turns into a moving road. Later starters can move faster because knowledge, vaccines, sanitation, and basic health systems already exist.

This is the heart of the idea. A false gap freezes people in place. A moving scale lets you see progress and pain at the same time. Saudi Arabia's faster change does not erase grief in the years when children died. Sweden's earlier progress does not make it a different kind of human society. It means both places moved through time, with different tools available.

World Bank language shows the same trap in official clothes. In a filmed World Bank lecture in June 2015, the old phrase developing world came under pressure because the data no longer matched it well. A later World Bank blog announced plans to phase that wording out. Old labels can outlive old facts.

Four levels can also be misused. If you treat them as fixed castes, you have only traded two boxes for four. The stronger habit is to see a road. Households can move up or down. People inside one country can live on different levels. A national average can hide a city apartment, a rural farm, and a family one illness away from falling.

A short news brief shows where the ladder can still mislead. The headline says a country is rising, but the chart shows one city on Level 4 and many villages near Level 2. The average is true and still thin. It cannot tell whose water is safe, whose clinic has medicine, or whose daughter can reach school.

Here is where Find the Middle breaks down: it cannot tell you what a specific family can afford tonight. A mother deciding whether to buy medicine or food needs local facts, not a global ladder. The ladder helps when you are tempted to say the world is split in two. It fails when a real person needs a price, a clinic, a school, or a safe road.

So use the tool for the right job. When a headline, map, or speech divides people into two camps, pause before you nod. Ask where the bubbles cluster. Ask whether the gap still exists. Ask whether the words are older than the facts. A better map does not make hardship smaller. It keeps hardship from being placed in the wrong world.

## Examples
### Example 1: The Bubble Chart Split
Scenario: At 8:00 a.m., a teacher in the Gapminder training room marks bubble chart 1-1; Rosling's 1965 chart shows many countries in a low-survival, high-fertility box. The 2017 chart spreads the bubbles upward and inward, so the old two-world label no longer describes where most people live.
What to do: Show the two charts in order. Name the old split, then ask where the 2017 bubbles cluster before choosing a label.
Why it matters: The move keeps history and update together. Readers learn to ask whether a category still fits before they reuse it.

### Example 2: Sweden and Saudi Arabia by Time
Scenario: At 8:01 a.m., a teacher in the Gapminder training room marks bubble chart 1-2; Sweden moved from 249 child deaths per 1,000 births in 1869 to below 35 in 1946. Saudi Arabia moved from 242 in 1960 to a similar level in 1993. The useful comparison is not rank; it is timing, tools, and pace.
What to do: Keep the death rates and years on the same page so the comparison turns from national status into historical movement.
Why it matters: The exchange makes the gap instinct less automatic. A country can look far away when the real difference is timing, tools, and speed of change.

### Example 3: The World Bank Label Lag
Scenario: At 8:02 a.m., a teacher in the Gapminder training room marks bubble chart 1-3; In a June 2015 World Bank lecture, the phrase developing world no longer fit the data well. A later World Bank blog described phasing out that wording. The language trailed the facts.
What to do: Treat official wording as evidence to check, not proof that the old map is current.
Why it matters: Institutions can keep useful but stale words alive. Readers need to compare official terms with the distribution those terms claim to name.

### Example 4: The 1965 and 2017 Thread
Scenario: At 8:03 a.m., a teacher in the Gapminder training room marks bubble chart 1-4; A 1965 chart can still make two worlds feel obvious. Send the 2017 chart next and the question changes: where did the middle go? The answer is that it was there in the bubbles, not in the old label.
What to do: Use the second chart to locate the largest group before naming the world.
Why it matters: Fast messages reward simple labels. A second chart and one middle-seeking question can slow the reflex before it hardens.

### Example 5: The Child-Mortality Scale
Scenario: At 8:04 a.m., a teacher in the Gapminder training room marks bubble chart 1-5; A rank-order exercise breaks when Sweden 1869-1946 and Saudi Arabia 1960-1993 sit near the same child-mortality rates but move on different calendars.
What to do: Redraw the comparison as movement over time, with the years visible.
Why it matters: The pause is the method. Changing the layout helps a reader see a moving road instead of a fixed ladder of countries.

### Example 6: The Four-Level Napkin
Scenario: At 8:05 a.m., a teacher in the Gapminder training room marks bubble chart 1-6; The four income levels are a rough daily-income ladder: Level 1 below $2, Level 2 $2-$8, Level 3 $8-$32, Level 4 above $32. They improve on two boxes but still need local detail.
What to do: Explain each rung as a working map, then ask what a national average hides.
Why it matters: A small wording edit can change the reader's map. The levels show rough positions without pretending every country is one thing.

## Review cards
### Card 1
Front: What does the gap instinct make the world look like?
Back: It makes humanity look split into two camps with an empty gulf between them. That picture hides the large middle where many people live and move.

### Card 2
Front: Why are four income levels better than rich versus poor?
Back: Because rich versus poor splits the world into a false gap. Four levels show material life as a ladder of steps, not two boxes. Level 1 is less than $2 per day, Level 2 is $2-$8, Level 3 is $8-$32, and Level 4 is more than $32 per day.

### Card 3
Front: What do Sweden and Saudi Arabia teach about child mortality?
Back: They show that high child mortality is not a fixed national type. Both moved from roughly 240 to 250 deaths per 1,000 births to below 35, but Saudi Arabia moved faster.

### Card 4
Front: What does the World Bank wording change warn you to check?
Back: Check whether an official phrase still matches the data. The phrase developing world lingered even as the distribution had moved beyond a clean two-box map.

### Card 5
Front: What is the difference between Find the Middle and optimism?
Back: Find the Middle asks where people actually cluster before judging. It does not say hardship is gone; it keeps severe poverty visible without letting extremes define everyone.
