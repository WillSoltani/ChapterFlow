# The Blame Instinct

> Reader document — factfulness chapter 9. Key-free rendering for reader-experience judgment.

## Hook
The first culprit may be easy to name, while the repeatable cause stays hidden in forms, incentives, and routines.

## Counterintuition
Blame feels satisfying because a face gives anger a target. Rosling's move is to keep responsibility, then ask what made the harm likely to happen again.

## Fast read
Start with one line at the top of a memo: who gets blamed if this happens again? The first answer is a person. The better answer is the loop that keeps handing that person the same bad choice. Blame spends heat before it buys light.

The blame instinct is the pull to find a villain for harm and a hero for success. It feels clean because a name fits in a headline. Systems thinking means looking at loops, rules, incentives, and delays rather than stopping with the nearest person. Peter Senge made that habit famous in The Fifth Discipline 1990.

Use Ask What Made This Possible, a question that traces the repeatable cause before praise or blame hardens. Hold responsibility in one hand, then use the other hand to trace the repeatable cause. A hero can hide the helpers. A villain can hide the rules.

## Deep read
A system is not a foggy word. It is a set of people, rules, habits, forms, prices, and signals that push behavior. If a mistake keeps returning, the nearest person may be the least useful explanation.

UNICEF procurement shows this. The headline wants a kind savior who made cheap supplies appear. The better explanation points to tender discipline, supply-chain routines, and suppliers able to meet standards. Low prices do not float in on kindness. They are built by tenders, standards, delivery routes, and people who keep boring promises.

That changes praise as much as blame. When a child gets medicine, gratitude should widen. The buyer who posted the tender matters. The warehouse worker matters. The supplier that can deliver at the price matters. The institution matters. Institutions are not marble buildings. They are workers and routines that keep showing up.

The same correction hurts more when the news is ugly. A journalist can spread a warped picture without lying. If the journalist shares the same bad map as the audience, dramatic stories will feel normal. Calling the reporter dishonest may miss the attention market, the weak public facts, and the audience hunger for shock.

Responsibility starts after the causes are named; otherwise the same cause can hand the next person the same bad choice. That sentence matters because the blame check is not an excuse. It asks you to slow the first accusation long enough to find what can be changed. The person may still answer for a choice. The system must answer for making that choice likely.

## Full read
Dr. Mosoka Fallah walks through Monrovia in the Ebola response, and the easy story reaches for one brave rescuer. His work deserves respect. So does the public system around him: contact tracers, local trust, records, and government staff who knocked on doors when fear was high.

The blame instinct has a twin. It hunts villains when things go wrong and worships heroes when things go right. Both habits shrink the map. A famous outsider did not stop Ebola alone. A local worker did not stop it alone either. The response needed people who knew streets, families, rumors, symptoms, and which door would open.

The training version of the story has to keep that whole bridge visible. Dr. Fallah's name belongs in the credit. So do Monrovia contact tracing, trust networks, records, and public workers. If one hero takes the whole slide, the repeatable system disappears.

That is the practical skill. Ask how the result became possible before you decide where praise or anger belongs. If a refugee reaches a boat, do not stop at the smuggler. Ask what EU carrier rules, confiscation risks, and asylum rules did to the safer route. If a birth rate falls, do not stop at a leader or one policy. Ask what changed in families, schools, health, and income before the famous order arrived.

The move is hard because blame pays fast. It gives you a short sentence and a place to put disgust. System work pays slower. You have to read the form, trace the tender, ask who had authority, and count who was missing from the process. That makes you less satisfying at the first loud table.

It also costs comfort. A refugee story used to end with a single bad actor and a clean angry sentence. With the carrier rule and smuggling rule in view, the ending is less clean. The smugglers may still be cruel. The law may still be pushing people toward them. Both claims have to fit.

The same price appears in smaller form at a public library help desk. A learner wants the teacher to say the journalist lied. The evidence may only prove that the facts were skewed and the map was shared. The room gets less satisfied. It also gets closer to repair.

So do not trade blame for vagueness. Trade instant blame for useful blame. First ask what made the outcome possible. Then ask who had power inside that system, who warned early, who was ignored, and which rule can change before the next person repeats the same part.

## Examples
### Example 1: The Repeat Handoff Form
Scenario: At 8:48 a.m., a trainer in the Senge operations room marks handoff form 9-1; If three teams repeat the same late handoff, punishing one clerk leaves the loop intact. Peter Senge's systems thinking asks what keeps reproducing the error.
What to do: Mark the loop beside the person's name before writing the blame sentence.
Why it matters: The comparison keeps responsibility in view without letting anger finish the diagnosis. A reader can copy the move when the same failure returns with a new face.

### Example 2: UNICEF's Repeatable Supply Work
Scenario: At 8:49 a.m., a trainer in the Senge operations room marks handoff form 9-2; Low UNICEF prices come from tenders, standards, suppliers, and delivery routines. A kind donor can help, but kindness does not explain the next shipment.
What to do: Trace the supply chain before praising anyone.
Why it matters: Praise can be too narrow. When a system works, the useful thanks includes the workers and rules that made the good result repeatable.

### Example 3: Mosoka Fallah and the Public System
Scenario: At 8:50 a.m., a trainer in the Senge operations room marks handoff form 9-3; Dr. Mosoka Fallah mattered in Monrovia's Ebola response, and so did contact tracers, local trust, records, and government staff. One hero would hide the bridge that carried the work.
What to do: Keep his name in the credit and name the public workers alongside him.
Why it matters: The coach talk widens credit without draining honor from the person. Good outcomes often need institutions strong enough to carry brave people.

### Example 4: Sincere but Skewed News
Scenario: At 8:51 a.m., a trainer in the Senge operations room marks handoff form 9-4; A journalist can mislead without lying if the newsroom and audience share the same wrong map. Deceit and sincere misconception require different repairs.
What to do: Mark what the journalist may believe, what the audience expects, and what the story leaves out.
Why it matters: The lesson keeps criticism sharp. A distorted map can come from bad incentives and shared gaps, not only from a liar at a desk.

### Example 5: Smugglers and Rules
Scenario: At 8:52 a.m., a trainer in the Senge operations room marks handoff form 9-5; A refugee boat story can include cruel smugglers and still ask how carrier penalties, confiscation risks, smuggling rules, and asylum rights narrowed safer routes.
What to do: Keep moral judgment and add causal judgment.
Why it matters: The before-after contrast shows the cost of stopping at the nearest villain. If a rule narrows the safe path, blaming only the smuggler leaves the path narrow.

### Example 6: The Institution Made Visible
Scenario: At 8:53 a.m., a trainer in the Senge operations room marks handoff form 9-6; An institution is not an abstraction here: it is address records, repeated calls, local trust, and workers following up until doors open.
What to do: Name the routine workers before using the word institution.
Why it matters: The dialogue turns a big word into visible labor. Readers can stop treating institutions as mist and start asking which routines are strong or weak.

## Review cards
### Card 1
Front: When blame takes over, where does attention land first?
Back: It sends attention toward a villain or hero. That feels clear, but it can hide incentives, laws, logistics, institutions, and feedback loops that keep shaping the outcome.

### Card 2
Front: How does Peter Senge's systems thinking change a repeated failure?
Back: It asks what mechanism keeps producing the failure. The named person may still matter, but the loop, rule, or incentive must be found before the repair can last.

### Card 3
Front: Why is UNICEF procurement not a lone-hero story?
Back: The low prices depend on tenders, supply chains, standards, and suppliers that can deliver. The achievement is institutional, not just one generous person's gift.

### Card 4
Front: What does Dr. Mosoka Fallah's Ebola work make visible beyond hero praise?
Back: His Monrovia contact tracing shows local trust, government employees, records, and community knowledge at work. The person matters, and so does the system carrying him.

### Card 5
Front: Why can blaming only smugglers miss part of the refugee boat story?
Back: Carrier penalties, confiscation risks, smuggling rules, and asylum rights can block safer routes. The smuggler may be guilty while the rules still shape the trap.

### Card 6
Front: How would you, in one sentence, connect the single perspective instinct (Ch 8) to the blame-instinct move?
Back: The single perspective instinct leans on one favored tool; the blame instinct settles on one face. Both stop the search early, before the system behind the result is in view.
