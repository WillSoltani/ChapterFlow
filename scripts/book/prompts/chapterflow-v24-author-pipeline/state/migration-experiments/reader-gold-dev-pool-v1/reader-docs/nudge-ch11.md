# How to Increase Organ Donations

> Reader document — nudge chapter 11. Key-free rendering for reader-experience judgment.

## Hook
A donor prompt slides through a license renewal, and silence begins to count as an answer.

## Counterintuition
Why does a life-saving wish fail at a plain form? Because you can favor donation and still let the default cast the vote.

## Fast read
A license form moves across a counter, and the donor line waits near the signature. One person checks it. Another means to, then leaves it blank. A third never sees the line at all because the clerk is rushing. Silence still signs forms.

You may care about organ donation and still lose the choice to the setup. A default is the answer that takes effect when nobody acts. In donation, that answer can be opt-in, opt-out, or a direct required choice. The moral wish is only part of the story. The path on the form decides how easy that wish is to record.

## Deep read
The gear is small: hard choices drift toward the path with the least friction. Organ donation is hard to think about. It asks you to picture death, family grief, and a future patient you will never meet. So the license counter or registry page does quiet work before you speak. If donation needs an extra box, delay often means no. If refusal needs an extra step, delay often means yes. A good default leaves the exit door in daylight.

Eric Johnson and Daniel Goldstein made this point vivid in their work on donation defaults. Their question was not whether people are generous. It was whether a form can hide generosity by making the generous act the harder route. This is why the design levers must stay distinct: opt-in, opt-out, mandated choice, and family consent. Opt-in asks you to join the registry. Opt-out starts from donation and lets you refuse. Mandated choice asks for an answer now. Family consent asks how the choice works when loved ones are in the room.

Allison sees the same lesson in a license office. Earlier, the donor line sat below a stack of routine renewals, and blank space carried the day. Later, the form asks each applicant to pick yes or no before the renewal can close. That change does not force donation. It stops delay from pretending to be a view. The reader's test is plain: find the answer that wins when attention fails, then ask whether that answer serves the chooser by the chooser's own lights.

## Full read
Spain matters because it keeps the story from becoming box worship. Its donation system is often linked with presumed consent, but the stronger lesson is mixed: consent rules work inside a system that also uses transplant coordination and family process. A law can set a starting point. It cannot, by itself, replace trust at a bedside.

That is the hard edge of donation defaults. Higher donation rates are not the only value. A nudge is defensible when it helps people do what they would endorse after a real chance to choose. It is not defensible when it hides refusal, surprises families, or treats the designer's target as the chooser's welfare. If Marc cannot find the opt-out line, the form is no longer just helping his better wish survive delay. It is making refusal cost more than it should.

Mandated choice has a special appeal here. It respects the fact that some people strongly support donation, some strongly refuse, and many postpone the whole thought. A direct question at license renewal can be blunt, but blunt can be kind when the alternative is silent drift. The form can ask, record the answer, and let the person move on. It turns a buried preference into a marked one.

Family consent keeps the policy human. A registry entry may be clear to the state and still feel shocking to a loved one. For that reason, the prompt should be salient before a crisis. Salient means hard to miss. A clear prompt, plain words, and a visible refusal path make later family talks less like ambush and more like carrying out a known choice. Donation design fails when families feel ambushed.

The same lesson reaches ordinary forms. If you have missed one message in a flooded group chat, you know how a small piece of design can decide what gets seen. A donor choice buried under routine license steps can suffer the same fate. The answer may matter more than anything else on the page, yet the page can make it feel like another checkbox.

There is also a trust cost after the form leaves the counter. A family member who never heard the donor wish may read the registry as paperwork from strangers. A coordinator then has to do two jobs at once: honor the recorded choice and keep the family from feeling pushed aside. Better design starts that work earlier, while the chooser is alive and the question can still be plain.

Do not use a default when people lack a fair chance to see it, understand it, and leave it. In that case, do the opposite: ask directly, slow the form down, and make no answer count as no answer. Defaults are powerful because attention is scarce. That power earns trust only when refusal stays practical.

## Examples
### Example 1: Finn's Two Forms
Scenario: Finn has a checked donor box in one hand and a blank donor line in the other, and both are sample driver's license forms. At the training desk, the instructor asks which organ donor registry answer will be recorded if the applicant hurries. Finn says the blank line proves refusal, then catches himself: an opt-in form may record delay, not belief.
What to do: Finn rewrites his training note. He separates a true no from an unanswered opt-in box, then asks clerks to point out the donor line before the license signature.
Why it matters: The classroom mistake carries the whole design problem. A blank answer can look like choice while the form is really letting friction choose.

### Example 2: Allison Closes the Renewal
Scenario: The senior clerk is covering testing, and Allison gets the driver's license renewal counter with the organ donor registry queue already backing up. Earlier opt-in forms let the donor box sit blank while the license printed. The later screen will not close until Allison asks yes or no. Same counter, same rush, one changed rule: delay no longer writes the answer.
What to do: Allison asks the donor question out loud and records the answer before printing the license. She treats no as valid, but she no longer treats silence as no.
Why it matters: The contrast shows what mandated choice adds. It preserves refusal while stopping a rushed license visit from quietly burying support for donation.

### Example 3: Donovan's Spain Audit
Scenario: Policy analyst Donovan keys Spain into the audit column in a health ministry archive room while the next row is still blank. He is reviewing organ donation files after reading Eric Johnson and Daniel Goldstein on default rules. The first note says presumed consent. The file that changes his mind says transplant coordination and family process sit beside it. The box alone did not explain the system.
What to do: Donovan revises the audit finding. He keeps presumed consent in the report, but pairs it with coordination and family consent before drawing a policy lesson.
Why it matters: The quiet find blocks a lazy default story. Spain shows that the starting rule matters most when the institution around it earns trust.

### Example 4: Marc at the Kiosk
Scenario: Driver Marc tugs his license card from the motor vehicle kiosk during his license renewal in the license office lobby, and the slot grips it halfway before flashing the organ donor registry screen. He thinks yes, donation is right. Then he thinks, wait, why is refusal behind another tap? If opt-out is fair, it should not hide. Maybe the default helps people like him follow through. Maybe it also needs a plain door out.
What to do: Marc checks whether the no is as visible as the yes before accepting the default. He lets the helpful path stand only after the exit is clear.
Why it matters: His thoughts protect the liberty claim. Opt-out can steer toward donation, but the steering changes character when refusal becomes hard to use.

### Example 5: Elaine's Four Levers
Scenario: Four levers. Elaine writes them at the top of the reply-all draft before any policy meeting begins: opt-in, opt-out, mandated choice, family consent. The inbox wants a quick answer about presumed consent. Her scarce attention has to go elsewhere first, across the whole donor registry design, because one lever cannot carry the moral work alone.
What to do: Elaine plans the agenda around all four levers. She gives each a question, then refuses to let presumed consent swallow the whole donor policy debate.
Why it matters: The planning scene keeps the menu complete. Donation defaults work through a system, not through one magic setting on a registry form.

### Example 6: Jacqueline's Bedside Call
Scenario: Transplant coordinator Jacqueline draws the consent packet toward her in the hospital hallway, then stops before the signature line. In Spain's organ donation system, presumed consent and transplant coordination meet the family at the bedside. One path presses the legal default and risks making grief feel overruled. The other waits for comfort that may not come in time. She must decide how to ask without treating trust as delay.
What to do: Jacqueline slows the first sentence and names the recorded default, then asks what the family understood before she moves the packet closer.
Why it matters: The dilemma shows why family consent belongs in the framework. A default can help only if the people living the moment do not feel ambushed.

## Review cards
### Card 1
Front: What are the four design levers for increasing organ donation?
Back: The four levers are opt-in, opt-out, mandated choice, and family consent. Each changes a different part of how a donor answer is asked, recorded, or honored.

### Card 2
Front: Why can a blank donor box fail to mean real refusal?
Back: A blank box can record delay, hurry, or confusion. In an opt-in system, the form may count silence as no even when the person supports donation.

### Card 3
Front: How does Spain complicate a simple presumed-consent story?
Back: Spain points beyond the legal default. Presumed consent works with transplant coordination and family process, so trust and execution are part of the result.

### Card 4
Front: Which matters more for organ donor registration, transparent borrowing (Ch 8) or donation defaults, and why?
Back: Donation defaults matter more here because the choice is often made by what happens when attention fails. Transparent borrowing helps compare costs, but the donor case turns on the recorded default and usable refusal.

### Card 5
Front: When does an organ-donation nudge stop being liberty-preserving?
Back: It stops when refusal is hard to find, costly to use, or surprising to the family. A helpful starting path needs a visible and practical exit.

### Card 6
Front: Why is mandated choice different from opt-out?
Back: Mandated choice asks for a yes or no and records that answer. Opt-out starts from donation unless refusal is registered, so delay has a different effect.

## Implementation plan
Core skill: Practice finding the answer that gets recorded when no one acts. Test the Recorded Default means you look for the preset path, then check whether refusal is easy enough to use. The skill matters when a form looks fair but one answer has a head start.

If-then (If a hard choice is easy to postpone): If a hard choice is easy to postpone, then Test the Recorded Default by marking the answer that delay will select.
If-then (If someone calls a default harmless): If someone calls a default harmless, then Test the Recorded Default by walking through the refusal path as if a tired person had to use it.
24-hour challenge: For one day, count the first five defaults you meet in ordinary forms or apps. Beside each, write the answer that wins if you do nothing.
Weekly practice: Keep a daily three-column log for a week: form or app, recorded default, exit path. At week's end, circle the default you trusted least and why.

Donation defaults can save more lives when the form asks clearly, sets a wise starting path, and keeps refusal easy to find.
