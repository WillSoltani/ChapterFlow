# A Dozen Nudges

> Reader document — nudge chapter 16. Key-free rendering for reader-experience judgment.

## Hook
You reach the donation page, mean to be generous, and the timing of one checkbox starts bargaining.

## Counterintuition
Despite looking tiny, a reminder, a prefilled line, or a future pledge can change the choice before willpower gets a vote. The small design is doing the work.

## Fast read
Reagan holds the pledge form and hears Clara say, "You can start later if now is tight." That one sentence changes the room. Reagan was ready to give less because rent is due. Now the choice has moved to future income, where generosity feels less like losing today's groceries.

A dozen nudges are small by design. A reminder reaches you before delay picks for you. A prefilled tax return removes a needless hunt. A future pledge lets your better plan meet your later paycheck. Small nudges work when the exit stays real.

You use the idea whenever a good choice is losing to timing, clutter, or hidden facts. The fix is not a lecture. It is a better place for the cue, a shorter path through the form, or a default you can leave.

## Deep read
A reminder is a choice moved closer. It works because many decisions fail before anyone rejects the good option. You meant to donate, file, enroll, or compare. Then the form moved out of sight, the deadline slid past, or the hard part waited until you were tired.

That is why the design levers matter. Reminders bring the moment back before it closes. Salience makes the key fact hard to miss. Precommitment lets you choose now for a later self who will face pressure. Simplified enrollment lowers the entry cost, but it must keep the way out plain.

Lydia sees this with a return-free filing notice. The wage line is already filled, and the refund estimate sits beside the signature box. She still checks the numbers. But she is no longer building the whole tax return from scraps. The nudge is not the state doing her thinking. It is the system not making her repeat facts it already has.

Give More Tomorrow uses the same logic in a warmer setting. Reagan's generous self is not gone when cash is tight. She is just badly timed. A pledge tied to future income helps her act on a wish she already owns. The small form respects the wish and waits for the income.

Bad forms ask for courage at the worst moment. Good forms meet the moment first.

## Full read
Stuart draws two boxes on the charity sign-up sheet: one for a gift today, one for a pledge from the next raise. The second box can look like a trick if you forget the hard edge. A nudge earns its case by helping the chooser, not by feeding the designer's hunger for a better total.

That sentence matters in ordinary life. A gym can make renewal easy because members forget. A tax office can send a prefilled return because wage data is already there. A charity can ask for future giving because many donors care more than their current cash flow permits. In each case, the designer has power over timing, order, and friction.

Power is why refusal has to stay practical. If Reagan cannot cancel the future pledge without calling during work, the friendly form has become pressure. If Lydia's prefilled tax return hides the correction link, the shortcut punishes the person who spots an error. If an enrollment screen buries the opt-out under shame-filled wording, the default is doing more than helping.

The useful test is plain: does the nudge help the person do what she would likely endorse with more attention, better timing, or clearer facts? Give More Tomorrow passes that test when donors can see the future commitment and stop it. Return-free filing passes when the taxpayer can inspect, correct, and decline the prefilled return.

Good friction blocks harm; bad friction blocks help. A password on a bank transfer can slow fraud. A maze around canceling a pledge can trap a donor. The same design move can protect or exploit, depending on what it makes easy and what it makes hard.

A small nudge also needs a small claim. It can repair a bad moment in the choice path. It cannot prove that the goal is wise, fair, or worth the tradeoff. If the charity spends donor money poorly, a future pledge box does not solve that. If the tax rule is unjust, a cleaner return only makes the bad rule easier to follow.

This is also why a dozen small examples should not become a license for every clever policy. A designer may prefer more donations, more filings, or more enrollment. That preference is not enough. The chooser's own reasons have to remain visible inside the design.

Where the idea breaks is a prefilled tax return with stale income data and no clear correction path. The form may save time for many people, but it cannot handle Lydia's case unless she can fix it before signing. The best small nudge does not ask for trust in the designer. It earns trust by making the next step easier and the refusal honest.

## Examples
### Example 1: Reagan's Later Gift
Scenario: "Commit after the raise," Reagan whispers on the living-room couch, then wonders if that is cheating. Give More Tomorrow asks for charitable giving from future income, not today's rent money. Her first thought is that a real donor gives now. The next thought is colder: if now is the only door, she will give nothing. The pledge box makes the better version possible later.
What to do: Reagan keeps the gift tied to the next raise and writes the cancellation path beside the pledge before she signs.
Why it matters: The inner argument shows why precommitment is not fake virtue. It protects an intention that current cash pressure would otherwise erase.

### Example 2: Lydia's Two Returns
Scenario: The prefilled tax return sits beside the blank tax software, and Lydia has one lunch break to plan the filing. The blank path feels safer because she controls every line. The return-free filing path shows wages, withholding, and a refund estimate already filled. Before touching either form, she weighs scarce attention: rebuild known facts, or spend the break checking what is already there.
What to do: Lydia chooses the plan that starts with inspection, not reconstruction. She will check the prefilled lines first and correct them only if the facts fail.
Why it matters: The tradeoff is about attention. Simplification helps when it frees the chooser to verify the important parts instead of retyping routine facts.

### Example 3: Clara's Pledge Table
Scenario: The gala treasurer calls in sick, and Clara, hired for thank-you notes, gets the Give More Tomorrow pledge table. The director wants charitable giving tonight to cover the shortfall. The donors keep saying the next paycheck is the honest time. Clara is caught between a smaller gift now that feels clean and a future income precommitment that could look like pressure if the exit is hidden.
What to do: Clara prints the future pledge with the cancel route in the same line as the amount, then separates it from the one-time gift box.
Why it matters: The dilemma keeps the hard edge visible. A better total is not enough if donors cannot see what they are agreeing to and how to leave.

### Example 4: Printer At The Desk
Scenario: The prefilled tax return slides from the printer before Ophelie has finished the explanation.
Stuart: "So Lydia can sign and leave?"
Ophelie: "Only after she checks the wage line."
Stuart: "The correction link is on page three."
Ophelie: "Move it beside the signature. A shortcut that hides fixes is not help."
What to do: Ophelie moves the correction step next to the signature line, then tells Stuart to explain the prefilled return as a starting point, not a verdict.
Why it matters: The exchange shows the freedom test in plain form. A simplified return helps only when the taxpayer can inspect and refuse it without a hunt.

### Example 5: Irene's Frozen Upload
Scenario: The payroll upload freezes at the last donor row, and Irene expects the Give More Tomorrow charitable giving campaign to collapse. If people could not give today, why would they pledge later? The reveal comes after the form is fixed: several donors choose future income as precommitment because today's account felt too tight. The delay did not kill the gift. The timing made it usable.
What to do: Irene changes the rollout note so the first choice is not gift or no gift. It is gift now, pledge from future income, or decline.
Why it matters: The surprise teaches the mechanism. Precommitment works when the blocked present is the problem, not when donors lack generosity.

### Example 6: Shirley's Pledge Texts
Scenario: The phone buzzed against the wood, and Shirley smelled coffee going cold.
Clara: Give More Tomorrow charitable giving pledge starts with next paycheck.
Shirley: Future income precommitment, right?
Clara: Yes. Reply STOP if rent changed the plan.
Shirley: Keep it. Thanks for not making me call.
Clara: Done.
What to do: Shirley confirms the pledge in the same thread where the exit is visible, and Clara records the choice without adding a phone maze.
Why it matters: The reminder keeps the promise alive, and the visible refusal keeps it voluntary. Both parts are needed for the nudge to stay clean.

## Review cards
### Card 1
Front: What makes a small practical nudge more than a tiny suggestion?
Back: It changes the decision setting: a reminder, default, disclosure, or precommitment reaches the chooser when the choice is live and still avoidable.

### Card 2
Front: Why does Give More Tomorrow use future income instead of asking only for a gift today?
Back: Future income changes the timing. A donor who feels cash pressure today can still commit to generosity later, as long as the future pledge is clear and cancelable.

### Card 3
Front: How can return-free filing help without taking the tax choice away?
Back: A prefilled return can remove repeated work by using facts the system already has. It stays a nudge only if the taxpayer can inspect, correct, and decline it.

### Card 4
Front: When does simplified enrollment stop protecting freedom?
Back: It stops protecting freedom when exit is hidden, costly, late, or shaming. The design may improve totals, but the chooser still needs a practical way out.

### Card 5
Front: Name the four A Dozen Nudges design levers and what they change.
Back: The levers are reminders, salience, precommitment, and simplified enrollment. They change timing, attention, future choice, and entry friction.

### Card 6
Front: What does medical liability choice (Ch 14) get WRONG if applied naively to Give More Tomorrow?
Back: It may overfocus on consent papers and miss timing. Give More Tomorrow uses charitable giving from future income as precommitment, not a waiver of a legal right.

## Implementation plan
Core skill: Practice placing the choice cue where the decision happens. The cue may be a form, pledge note, deadline, correction link, or exit step. Place The Choice Cue works only when the person can still say no.
If-then (If a good task keeps vanishing after you postpone it): If delay keeps winning, then Place The Choice Cue by putting the form on the surface you touch before starting the task.
If-then (If a later cost is hard to picture): If the future result feels foggy, then Place The Choice Cue by writing the later amount beside today's choice.
If-then (If a default is already selected for you): If the default is checked, then Place The Choice Cue by putting the exit step next to the accept button before you proceed.
If-then (If the designer's preferred outcome looks too convenient): If the screen seems to serve the designer first, then Place The Choice Cue by asking what fact would help you choose by your own lights.
24-hour challenge: In the next 24 hours, before you start your next paperwork task, create one calendar block for it and move the needed form onto your keyboard.
Weekly practice: For one week, tally each time delay picks for you because a form, deadline, or exit step was out of sight. Read the total at week's end.
