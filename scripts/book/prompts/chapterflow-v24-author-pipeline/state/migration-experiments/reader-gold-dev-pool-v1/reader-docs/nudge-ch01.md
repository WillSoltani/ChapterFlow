# Biases and Blunders

> Reader document — nudge chapter 1. Key-free rendering for reader-experience judgment.

## Hook
2 starting points bent 1 Milwaukee estimate; the calculator was never the main risk.

## Counterintuition
You can be careful and still let a first number, vivid memory, or default do quiet work before you notice.

## Fast read
A buried message can sit under a pile of alerts until the moment it matters. You walk into the handoff missing the one note that would have kept you from looking scattered. Start there because bad choices often begin before a choice feels hard. A bad setting can make a good mind look careless.

Thaler and Sunstein call the real person a Human, not an Econ. An Econ is the tidy model who calculates cleanly every time. A Human uses bounded rationality, which means your attention and memory simplify hard calls. Those shortcuts help, but they also bend. The first number can steer an estimate. A vivid story can crowd out base rates. A default can win because changing it takes effort. The repair is not to shame the mind. The repair is to shape the path before the mind slips.

## Deep read
Gaelle sees the Milwaukee question before the rest of the sheet is face down. One group has just been asked to think about Chicago. Another has just been asked to think about Green Bay. Both groups now estimate Milwaukee's population, and the starting point tugs at the answer. The number you meet first can steer the number you give.

That is anchoring. An anchor is a starting point that pulls your later estimate toward it. You may know the first number is only a hint, or even not very useful, but you adjust away from it too little. The mind begins with what is nearby, then corrects if it has time, energy, and reason. Often it has only a little of each.

Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky gave these mistakes a useful shape. The Automatic System is fast and intuitive. It gives you an answer before you have built an argument. The Reflective System is slower and more careful. It can check the first answer, but it does not always get the floor. When the inbox is full, when the form is long, or when a meeting is moving, the quick answer often becomes the working answer.

Anchoring is only one bias. Availability makes a recent crash or loud story feel more common than it is. Representativeness makes a short streak look like a pattern. Loss aversion makes a loss hurt more than the same-sized gain helps. These are not random failures. They are repeatable bends in judgment, and repeatable bends can be planned for.

## Full read
Donna can make the same mistake while trying to be fair. She writes a memo for a benefits form and leaves the current option checked because changing it seems like a push. That looks neutral on the page. It is not neutral in a Human mind. Status quo bias makes the current arrangement sticky, and many people stay with it because attention is scarce.

You can see the moral boundary on Donna's form. The Human/Econ split does not give you a license to trick people. It gives you a reason to stop pretending the setting is blank. A blank-looking form still has an order, a default, a first number, and a frame. Someone chose them, or someone left them there. Either way, they guide behavior.

Donna's next memo line sorts biases by the thing they bend. If the problem is anchoring, look at the starting number. If the problem is availability, ask which story is easiest to recall and whether the base rate is visible. If the problem is representativeness, check the sample size before you trust the pattern. If the problem is loss aversion, notice whether the same fact is being heard as a loss instead of a gain. A nudge begins where repeated error becomes visible.

Milwaukee keeps the lesson plain. Nobody needs to be foolish for Chicago or Green Bay to pull the estimate. The setting handed people a reference point, and correction did not fully erase it. Once you see that, you can design better forms, menus, warnings, and handoffs. Put the helpful number where it belongs. Keep the vivid but rare story from doing all the work. Make the safer default easy to keep and easy to leave.

A group chat shows the same point outside policy. Before a handoff, you can move the needed folder into the path of your next task. Before a chat buries the one urgent note, you can pin the channel or move the note to the shared place where work actually starts. If the note stays loose in the chat, the cost is not abstract. Someone arrives without the needed detail, then spends the first minutes rebuilding trust instead of doing the work. These are small moves, but they respect the fact that attention is not endless.

Pattern is the quiet precondition. A nudge quietly assumes the error repeats often enough for the setting to be changed with care. When the mistake is rare, private, or unknowable from the outside, the right answer may be advice, not architecture. Without a visible pattern, choice design can become guesswork with a friendly name.

## Examples
### Example 1: Chicago Pull
Scenario: Survey participant Gaelle is already writing Chicago in the margin at the library reading table when the Milwaukee population estimate line comes into view. The paper is thin enough for the prior prompt to ghost through. Her pencil slows, then keeps moving. No one has told her to use Chicago as a guide, but the city is now in the room with the blank answer box.
What to do: Gaelle circles the first number she met before writing the estimate. She treats Chicago as an anchor to notice, not as silent background.
Why it matters: The vignette makes anchoring visible in a small motion. The first reference point can bend judgment even when no one means to rely on it.

### Example 2: Green Bay Counter
Scenario: The clipboard clip sticks, and Tracy has to tug the Milwaukee sheet free from under the Green Bay prompt. "That number feels too small," she says. Roland turns his page over. "Mine feels too large." "What did yours ask first?" "Chicago." Tracy taps the Green Bay line with the end of the pen.
What to do: Tracy and Roland compare the question that came before the estimate. They name the starting place before arguing over the final number.
Why it matters: The exchange shows why anchors are not just trusted advice. A nearby reference can pull two careful people in opposite directions.

### Example 3: Buzz Before Base Rate
Scenario: The phone buzzes against the wood, and the vibration makes Nicholas press harder on the pencil. A fresh alert about a crash is still bright in his head as he drafts a warning note for class. Halfway through the second sentence, he sees Kahneman and Tversky's availability problem in his own hand: the loud story has crowded out the base rate he meant to check.
What to do: Nicholas crosses out the vivid lead and writes the base rate beside the alert. He lets the recent story inform the note without letting it run the note.
Why it matters: Availability feels like evidence because memory is easy. Recovery starts when the vivid example is placed next to the quieter count.

### Example 4: Default Memo
Scenario: Donna sets the highlighter cap on the memo line marked Default before the benefits form goes out. Memo to self: Kahneman and Tversky made bias visible, and the checked box is not empty space. If people are busy, many will keep it. Ask who benefits from inaction, how easy exit is, and whether the current option deserves the first position.
What to do: Donna rewrites the memo so the default must earn its place. She adds an exit check before the form reaches anyone's inbox.
Why it matters: The memo carries the action. Defaults shape choices through effort and attention, so the designer has to treat the checked box as a real choice.

### Example 5: Streak Split
Scenario: Sideline coach Arlo moves the cones into a tighter square while the last few shots are still fresh. Three makes in a row make one player look suddenly reliable, so Arlo sketches the next drill around her. Hudson writes the same streak on a damp lineup card, hears Amos Tversky's small-sample warning in it, and adds sample too small before changing the drill sheet.
What to do: Arlo plans from resemblance: hot shots look like a hot player. Hudson marks the small sample first, then waits for more attempts before treating the streak as a signal.
Why it matters: Representativeness turns a short run into a story. The contrast shows why resemblance needs a sample-size check before it guides the next move.

### Example 6: Pattern Before Blame
Scenario: Hudson has barely started the handoff note when Tracy's second message lands under a flood of replies. "I keep missing the one message that matters," Hudson says. Tracy does not tell him to care more. "Kahneman would start with the pattern, not the blame. If the error repeats, design for the repeat." Hudson drags the handoff note out of the chat and into the shared task list.
What to do: Hudson moves the key note to the place where the next task starts. Tracy keeps the focus on a repeated pattern, not on Hudson's effort.
Why it matters: A nudge is possible when mistakes follow a stable pattern. The reframe protects dignity while making the environment easier to repair.

## Review cards
### Card 1
Front: Why split the mind into Automatic and Reflective Systems?
Back: The split explains timing. Fast impressions often arrive first, while slower correction needs attention, effort, and a chance to enter the choice.

### Card 2
Front: What does the Milwaukee estimate show about anchoring?
Back: Chicago and Green Bay act as starting points. People move away from the first reference, but not far enough, so the estimate stays pulled toward it.

### Card 3
Front: How does availability distort a risk judgment?
Back: A vivid or recent example is easier to recall, so it feels more common than it is. The check is to place the story beside a base rate.

### Card 4
Front: Why can a short streak mislead a careful person?
Back: Representativeness makes a few similar results look like a stable pattern. Small samples can contain streaks by chance, so resemblance is not enough.

### Card 5
Front: Why is a default not neutral just because nobody changed it?
Back: Status quo bias makes the current option sticky. Inaction may mean the person was busy, tired, or unsure, not that they endorsed the default.

### Card 6
Front: What makes an error a good target for a nudge?
Back: The error repeats in a pattern. Once the pattern is visible, the choice setting can be changed without calling the person foolish or removing choice.
