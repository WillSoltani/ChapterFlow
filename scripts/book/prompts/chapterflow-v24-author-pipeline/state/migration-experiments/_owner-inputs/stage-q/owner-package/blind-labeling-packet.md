**Task:** Judge whether the candidate preserves the source boundary that contribution mapping does not imply equal fault.

### Material 1: SOURCE_CONTRACT
Contribution mapping does not imply equal fault. Situations can be 90/10 or 70/30, and naming your smaller contribution still matters because it identifies what you can change directly. Contribution mapping is not a claim of equal responsibility; flattening asymmetry is its own distortion.

### Material 2: CANDIDATE

## SQ-002
**Task:** Judge whether the candidate accurately distinguishes deliberate letting go from suppression.

### Material 1: SOURCE_CONTRACT
The deliberate choice to take a grievance somewhere other than a confrontation with the person it concerns, when the honest inventory of the possible conversation shows it cannot produce what the grievance needs. The grievance is acknowledged, processed somewhere it can be received, and consciously released from the confrontation slot. Letting go is not suppression: suppression is unprocessed and underground, redirecting into other behavior. Letting go is deliberate, acknowledged to the self, and routed elsewhere — to a friend who can receive it, a journal entry that names what was needed, or a private acknowledgment of what the situation cost.

### Material 2: CANDIDATE
Letting go is not pretending; it is deciding where the grievance actually belongs. Some grievances need a friend, a journal, time, or a private reckoning rather than a confrontation with the person who caused them. If the person cannot hear it, nothing would change, and you would not feel genuinely resolved after saying it, raising it may keep the grievance alive in a new form. Letting go means processing it somewhere real. Suppression means leaving it underground.

## SQ-003
**Task:** Judge whether the candidate maps each WRAP move to the correct decision villain without causal overclaiming.

### Material 1: SOURCE_CONTRACT
The four villains are narrow framing, confirmation bias, short-term emotion, and overconfidence; each attacks a different stage of choice. Narrow framing makes a decision look like a yes-or-no question when the stronger move is to search for additional options. Confirmation bias turns research into advocacy by collecting evidence that flatters the preferred answer. Short-term emotion can make a temporary fear, attachment, or excitement feel like durable wisdom. Overconfidence makes forecasts feel like facts even when the future contains a wider range of outcomes. The WRAP process exists because each villain needs a process counterweight, not merely a warning label.

### Material 2: CANDIDATE
The four moves name the repair job: Widen your options, Reality-test your assumptions, Attain distance before deciding, Prepare to be wrong. Each move meets a different danger. Narrow framing needs more live paths. Confirmation bias needs better evidence. Short-term emotion needs distance. Overconfidence needs a wider range of futures.

## SQ-004
**Task:** Judge whether the candidate assigns the outside view and close-up evidence their proper, complementary roles.

### Material 1: SOURCE_CONTRACT
A decision maker should zoom out for comparable outcomes and zoom in for lived detail before trusting a prediction. The inside view is vivid but often overconfident because it treats the case as unique. The outside view uses base rates from similar cases and is often more accurate for prediction. Experts are useful when asked for base rates, but less reliable when asked to predict a unique future. Close-up experience adds texture that statistics cannot provide. The strongest reality test alternates between aggregate evidence and lived detail.

### Material 2: CANDIDATE
A forecast gets safer when it moves between two distances. From far away, similar cases show the rough odds. From close up, people and small details show what the choice will ask of daily life. Either lens alone can mislead. Daniel Kahneman's curriculum team had an inside story that made sense. They knew the work, the talent in the room, and the steps on the plan. Then comparable projects pointed to a much longer timeline. The outside view was not smarter because it knew the people better. It was smarter because it had seen more cases. Ask experts for the kind of answer they are best placed to give. A seasoned operator may be weak at naming your one true future, but strong at naming a reference class. How long do projects like this usually take? How often do restaurants like this one make it? What happens when teams with similar plans hit the same wall? Close-up stories warm the average without letting it drive alone. Brian Zikmund-Fisher did not need only medical odds before transplant treatment. He needed to hear what patients lived through: the fatigue, the fear, the dull practical parts of recovery. The odds mattered. The lived detail mattered too. The skill is not picking numbers over stories. The skill is making each lens do its own job.
