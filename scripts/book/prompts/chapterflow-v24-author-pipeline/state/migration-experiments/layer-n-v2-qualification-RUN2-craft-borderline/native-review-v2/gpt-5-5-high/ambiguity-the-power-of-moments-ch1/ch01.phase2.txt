## Questions and choices
Q1. At a dental clinic, a routine cleaning runs longer than planned. The last few minutes are calm, clear, and less painful. Which memory lesson best fits this design choice?
   a) The visit will be judged mostly by the total minutes in the chair.
   b) The calmer finish can improve how the whole visit is remembered.
   c) The staff should focus on shortening the visit and leave the finish alone.

Q2. During move-in, an apartment building is plain but gives each family a bright welcome cart with cold drinks at the elevator. What lesson should the manager take?
   a) Plan no guest moments until the building feels more upscale.
   b) Build one vivid, tellable surprise that changes the ordinary arrival.
   c) Set the drinks out quietly, since the free value carries the memory.

Q3. New nurses finish a hard training course. The director can file their certificates or ask them to name their next unit before the team claps. Which choice fits the moment-design logic?
   a) Make the achievement public so the group can witness the effort.
   b) Mark the crossing into their next unit now, before the change turns into private logistics.
   c) Wait until the new unit starts, since transitions gain meaning only later.

Q4. A neighborhood library wants a Saturday event to be more than a sign-up table. Which plan uses the four ingredients of memorable experiences?
   a) Pick one emotional route and use the same beat for every visitor.
   b) Add more forms so the event feels more organized at the table.
   c) Lift the mood, spark insight, honor effort, and help people meet.

Q5. In a small repair shop, the owner cannot redo the whole space this month. What would most closely match the red-phone lesson?
   a) Use one visible object to signal that the usual script has changed.
   b) Delay all changes until the shop can afford a full redesign of the whole visit.
   c) Rely on steady service alone so no single interaction stands out.

Q6. At a bookstore launch, free cookies sit on a back table and no one mentions them. Which change would make the gift more like a moment?
   a) Mention that the cookies are free, since the cost is the memorable part.
   b) Turn the delivery into a small event, with a request and reveal.
   c) Keep the cookies available in the back, so the gift stays casual.

Q7. Before a basic training group splits into new assignments, the leader has one open hour. What use of that hour best reflects the threshold idea?
   a) Save recognition for a later check-in after each person has settled into the role.
   b) Use the hour for loose conversation, since the real change begins afterward.
   c) Mark the shared crossing now, before the next stage becomes private logistics.

Q8. Customer support fixes a frustrating account lock. The call has one sharp peak of stress, then a steady, clear close. What should the team not assume?
   a) That callers will average each minute of the call with equal weight.
   b) That the stressful peak and the ending can strongly affect later recall.
   c) That a clearer close can help without erasing the earlier stress.

Q9. At a busy cafe counter, a free mint sits by the receipt printer. Customers take one at checkout, but nobody talks about it later. The owner wonders why the gift vanishes. Which diagnosis fits best?
   a) The mint needs a higher cost before customers treat it as worth retelling.
   b) The cafe needs better fixtures before a small gift can become a guest story.
   c) The mint lacks a tellable format, so guests treat it as a small free item.

## ANSWER KEY (with explanations)
Q1: b — The cold-water finding separates duration from recall: a milder ending can change the remembered episode. A shorter clock is not the only path to a better memory.
Q2: b — The Popsicle Hotline works through a vivid, tellable surprise, not luxury or price alone. The cart should make arrival feel like an event, not a quiet perk.
Q3: a — Public recognition changes how an achievement feels. Filing the certificate treats meaning as automatic; waiting misses the threshold before the next stage begins.
Q4: c — The four ingredients are elevation, insight, pride, and connection. One repeated beat or a smoother form table misses the distinct routes a short event can use.
Q5: a — The red phone shows that one concrete object can change the script. The lesson is not to wait for a full redesign or let the whole visit stay evenly ordinary.
Q6: b — The popsicles become memorable through the phone, request, and tray. The gift needs a tellable format; cost and quiet availability are weaker explanations.
Q7: c — The threshold matters before the new stage becomes private logistics. Waiting until later treats the transition as meaningful only after it has already scattered.
Q8: a — The false assumption is equal averaging. The cold-water comparison shows recall can overweight the end, while a clearer close still does not erase earlier stress.
Q9: c — Free alone is not the lesson. The Popsicle Hotline works because the phone and tray make the gift narratable; the mint needs a format, not a higher price or better fixtures.
