## Questions and choices
Q1. Vivian hears a volunteer say a late-night spending lapse means she only needs stronger refusal. Which response fits the three powers?
   a) She should list the tempting stores and promise not to enter them again.
   b) She should decide the purchase shows the saving goal was probably not sincere.
   c) She should name the act to start, the act to stop, and the value to remember.

Q2. When a nurse keeps the goal 'confirm the patient name' on the med cart before handoff, a noisy alert loses some pull. Which brain claim fits that scene?
   a) Prefrontal control helps goals bias attention and response choice when impulses compete.
   b) Self-control sits outside the brain and reflects fixed personal character.
   c) Impulse control works mainly after a behavior is finished and explained.

Q3. A case summary says Phineas Gage survived the tamping-iron accident but later showed changed planning and social restraint. What is the useful takeaway?
   a) Survival and speech can remain while frontal damage disrupts self-command over time.
   b) The case has no value because some dramatic details were questioned later.
   c) The accident proves language skill is the only part of control that matters.

Q4. Amelia keeps vowing to stop sending sharp replies, but she notices them only after they are gone. What should she study first?
   a) Whether her motivation feels intense enough the next morning.
   b) Which cue or urge appears just before her hand hits send.
   c) How to make regret feel costly enough after each message.

Q5. A family ride schedule shows the worst failures happen when someone drops a promise and trust has to be repaired. What does this suggest about willpower's roots?
   a) Willpower developed mainly for private achievement with little social pressure.
   b) Self-control helps people keep commitments that make group life work.
   c) The best control plan is to avoid cooperation whenever conflict appears.

Q6. Brooklyn values running, yet the snack cue wins when the run goal is only a vague thought. What should she infer?
   a) Being important is not the same as being vivid; a value can matter and still lose to a cue that feels louder right now.
   b) The long-term goal needs attention at the choice point to compete.
   c) The craving will fade only after the snack cue is fully removed.

Q7. Your friend says, 'If I really cared about saving money, the sale tab would not tempt me.' How should you read that conflict?
   a) Both motives can be sincere, so support decides whether saving or the sale guides action.
   b) The saving goal must be fake whenever opening the sale tab feels relieving.
   c) The sale urge should be obeyed first because relief reveals the real want.

Q8. A week of notes shows 'eat better' failed, but 'pack lunch before leaving' worked twice and exposed the late-morning trigger. Which next step fits the three-power method?
   a) Make a stronger declaration and stop checking the trigger.
   b) Treat the declaration as proof that commitment is already enough.
   c) Run a small experiment with the behavior, trigger, and feedback.

Q9. A log from Amelia's desk shows automatic speed works until a form conflicts with a current goal. What role does prefrontal control play here?
   a) It handles only abstract puzzles, so a desk routine is outside its range.
   b) It replaces every routine with slow thought even when no conflict appears.
   c) It biases attention and response when routine action clashes with the goal.

## ANSWER KEY (with explanations)
Q1: c — The three powers split the problem into I will, I won't, and I want. Stronger refusal may help, but it misses failures of action and purpose.
Q2: a — Miller and Cohen's model puts active goals in the control loop. The personality-only reading misses the brain system doing the steering.
Q3: a — The core lesson is the split between basic ability and regulation. Doubting every detail or reducing control to speech loses that evidence.
Q4: b — Self-awareness has to catch the choice point. Motivation or regret after the fact cannot redirect a cue Amelia never notices.
Q5: b — The source links self-control to cooperation, restraint, and commitments. A private-achievement reading misses the social pressure.
Q6: b — Importance is not the same as salience. The snack is vivid now, so the run goal needs attention before it can guide action.
Q7: a — Conflicting motives are normal. This frame does not read relief as proof the value is fake; it asks which motive gets support.
Q8: c — A concrete experiment can be observed and adjusted when the cue appears. A declaration alone does not show where behavior breaks down.
Q9: c — Prefrontal control matters when an automatic response conflicts with the current goal. It is not limited to abstract puzzles.
