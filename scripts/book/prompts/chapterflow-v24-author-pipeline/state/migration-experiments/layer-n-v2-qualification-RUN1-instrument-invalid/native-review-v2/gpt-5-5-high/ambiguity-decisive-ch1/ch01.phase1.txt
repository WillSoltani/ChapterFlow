# The Four Villains of Decision Making

## Hook
Bad menus make smart choosers order the wrong meal.

## Fast read
Steve Cole did not treat HopeLab's portable activity device as a one-vendor bet. He put multiple design firms into the first lap, then let the ideas teach the team before anyone had to commit. The choice got better before the answer arrived.

A live option set keeps a bad frame from feeling like fate. A narrow frame makes a rich problem look like yes or no. Once that happens, even smart evidence may only decorate a cramped choice. WRAP is the Heaths' four-part repair process: Widen your options, Reality-test your assumptions, Attain distance before deciding, and Prepare to be wrong. It starts by widening the frame, then checking facts, cooling the heat, and planning for forecasts that miss.

## Deep read
Bias often enters before anyone feels biased. It starts when the frame chooses the menu. Then the favorite option starts hiring facts. Next, the heat of the moment speaks louder than the long term. Last, a forecast feels firm because the spreadsheet has neat rows.

Evidence gets crooked when the favorite answer hires the researcher. Confirmation bias does not require lying. The product lead can sincerely ask for data and still collect only the data that flatters the first plan. That is why WRAP has a second move: Reality-test your assumptions. It asks for contact with evidence that can surprise you, not just evidence that can cheer you on.

Andy Grove's Intel question shows the emotional villain with less drama than the stakes deserved. Intel had built its identity around memory chips, yet microprocessors were becoming the better path. Grove's move was to imagine what a fresh outsider would do. That question did not make outsiders smarter than Intel. It made attachment quieter for a moment.

The four moves name the repair job: Widen your options, Reality-test your assumptions, Attain distance before deciding, Prepare to be wrong. Each move meets a different danger. Narrow framing needs more live paths. Confirmation bias needs better evidence. Short-term emotion needs distance. Overconfidence needs a wider range of futures.

## Full read
The decision lead can run WRAP and still lose the bet. That is not a defect in the process. It is the reason the process is worth using. A good choice is made before the future reports back, so the test cannot be only whether the happy ending arrived.

HopeLab's design race helps here. Cole's team was not buying variety for its own sake. The parallel work gave them comparison data: one firm might see the device as a toy, another as a health tool, and another as a social object. The point was not to delay the choice. The point was to learn before the choice got too expensive to change.

The same discipline changes evidence. When a favorite answer appears early, the mind starts treating research like a campaign. It looks for clean support and calls that diligence. Reality-testing is more humble. It asks what would have to be true for the favorite answer to fail, then looks for that evidence while the decision is still open.

Intel shows why distance is not the same as coldness. Emotion can carry real information: pride, loyalty, fear, and excitement often point to something that matters. The danger is the short-term surge. Memory chips were not just a product line; they were part of Intel's story. Grove's outsider question gave the old story less power over the next call.

Forecasts need the final check because certainty is sticky. Once one future has been pictured in detail, other futures feel less real. The final WRAP move asks the team to name what could go better, worse, or simply different. It does not insult the plan. It keeps the plan from pretending the future has signed a contract.

Good process raises the odds without promising the outcome. That line is easy to forget when a clean process ends with a bad result. A bad result may mean the team missed something, or it may mean uncertainty did what uncertainty does. The fair question is whether the process widened the frame, tested the evidence, cooled the moment, and faced the forecast. A team can answer yes to that question and still meet a rough market.

Here is the counter-case. If the building is on fire, nobody should pause to build a vendor horse race, invite dissenting evidence, and map three futures. People should leave. WRAP is for choices where there is time to shape the frame. It would have been a mistake for Intel to use distance as an excuse to dodge a clear emergency. The process earns trust when it fits the tempo of the choice.

## Key takeaway
Use WRAP as a sequence: widen the frame, test the evidence, cool the emotion, and plan for forecasts that may miss.

## Try this now
The next time a quick call pulls at you, touch your laptop lid and record the cue, shortcut, and likely villain.

## Examples
### Example 1: HopeLab First Lap
The HopeLab planner stands after lunch in HopeLab's project room, one portable activity device sketch clipped beside three firm names. No one else speaks yet. His mind tries the easy sentence: pick the safest vendor and move. Then Steve Cole's broader first lap nags at him. One firm could solve comfort, another could solve play, and another could show what the team has missed.

What to do: The HopeLab planner keeps the first phase parallel. He asks each design firm for a small, comparable pass before naming a winner.

Why it matters: The move protects the choice from a cramped frame. The team buys learning before commitment, not delay for its own sake.

### Example 2: Budget Before Bet
The prototype planner marks the budget wall on Monday morning in HopeLab's planning room. The portable activity device has enough money for depth, but not for waste. She weighs a polished pitch from one firm against smaller first passes from multiple design firms. Steve Cole's lesson sits in the math: the first dollars should purchase comparison, not premature loyalty.

What to do: The prototype planner funds smaller first passes from several firms and sets one shared review standard before a final design partner is chosen.

Why it matters: Planning can widen or shrink a decision before work starts. A small comparison budget can prevent a large commitment to the wrong frame.

### Example 3: Intel Outsider Question
The Intel reviewer reaches the Intel strategy review with two bad pages in his folder. One page protects memory chips and risks missing microprocessors. The other backs microprocessors and wounds the old identity. He is torn between the old business and the new path. Andy Grove's outsider question gives him a way through: what would a new leader do without the memory-chip attachment?

What to do: The Intel reviewer writes the outsider question at the top of the review page and judges the options from that cooler vantage point.

Why it matters: Distance does not erase emotion. It keeps a temporary attachment from posing as long-term strategy.

### Example 4: Search With Teeth
The product lead taps the evidence folder in the product office during a late review call. The decision lead asks, "Which finding would make us change our mind?" The product lead reads the top sheet again. It praises the favored launch, but every source came from friendly users. The WRAP four-step process suddenly sounds less like a slogan and more like a search with teeth.

What to do: The product lead adds a disconfirming-evidence column and sends the decision lead to find users who stopped using the product.

Why it matters: Confirmation bias can look like hard work. A search only tests the choice when some evidence is allowed to hurt the favorite answer.

### Example 5: Forecast Range Reveal
Yvonne pins one clean launch forecast on the board, and the room expects the WRAP four villains to be done once the number looks careful. Then she uncovers the second sheet: three plausible ranges, all built from the same data. The reveal changes the decision cue. She must choose whether to plan for one future or prepare for several.

What to do: Yvonne keeps the range sheet visible and asks what the team would do if the low, middle, or high case showed up.

Why it matters: Overconfidence is not just cheerfulness. It is the shrinking of possible futures until one picture feels like a fact.

### Example 6: Four Villains Thread
The team lead texts from the train platform before the decision meeting: "WRAP check. Which of the four villains is loudest?" A reply comes back: "All of them?" The team lead types four short lines: narrow frame, friendly evidence, hot feeling, single forecast. The four-step process stops being a poster and becomes a map for the next hour.

What to do: The team lead asks the group to name one villain first, then pick the matching WRAP move instead of trying to be broadly careful.

Why it matters: A named process works only when it guides the next action. Naming the villain tells the team which repair the choice needs.

## Review cards
Card 1 — Front: What are the four villains of decision making?
          Back: The villains are narrow framing, confirmation bias, short-term emotion, and overconfidence. Each one bends a different part of the choice.

Card 2 — Front: Which four moves make up WRAP?
          Back: WRAP means Widen your options, Reality-test your assumptions, Attain distance before deciding, and Prepare to be wrong.

Card 3 — Front: Why can a yes-or-no frame harm the choice before evidence is reviewed?
          Back: A tight frame decides which options count. Even a thick evidence file can miss better paths if those paths were excluded at the start.

Card 4 — Front: How does confirmation bias turn research into advocacy?
          Back: The preferred answer starts guiding attention. The search may feel honest, but it gathers friendly evidence and misses facts that could change the call.

Card 5 — Front: What did Andy Grove's outsider question help Intel do?
          Back: It helped Intel loosen the emotional grip of its memory-chip legacy. The question created distance, so microprocessors could be judged more clearly.

Card 6 — Front: What should a good process promise, and what should it not promise?
          Back: It should promise a better way to frame, test, cool, and prepare. It should not promise that uncertainty will disappear or that every result will be right.

## Implementation plan
Title: Practice naming the distortion before you improve the answer
Core skill: Practice naming the distortion before you improve the answer. Ask whether the choice is suffering from a tight frame, friendly evidence, hot feeling, or false certainty. Then use the matching WRAP move instead of trying to be generally careful.
If-then 1: [When a choice arrives as yes or no] If the choice has only two doors, then Name the Villain First and add one real option before gathering more evidence.
If-then 2: [When the evidence file makes your favorite answer feel cleaner] If the proof feels too friendly, then Name the Villain First and look for one fact that could weaken the favorite answer.
If-then 3: [When fear, pride, or excitement speaks loudest] If the current feeling starts driving the call, then Name the Villain First and ask what will still matter after the mood cools.
If-then 4: [When one forecast starts to feel settled] If one future looks too neat, then Name the Villain First and write two other outcomes that could still happen.
24-hour challenge: For the next 24 hours, attach a sticky note to your laptop lid. The next time you feel the pull to rush a choice, record the cue, the shortcut to stop, and the villain you named.
Weekly practice: Once this week, stage one real but low-risk choice. Before deciding, rehearse Name the Villain First under real conditions and write the matching WRAP move.
