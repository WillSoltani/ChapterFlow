## Questions and choices
Q1. In a product review, the HopeLab planner sees his team using WRAP as a loose reminder to slow down. Which reply best fits the model's structure?
   a) The HopeLab planner should use WRAP as a general pause so the team talks more carefully.
   b) The HopeLab planner should match Widen, Reality-test, Attain distance, and Prepare to the four choice stages.
   c) The HopeLab planner should save WRAP for final review after the team has picked a path.

Q2. The prototype planner hears a vendor choice framed as approve this firm or cancel the project. What should she question first?
   a) The prototype planner should gather more vendor data before asking whether the vendor list is too small.
   b) The prototype planner should add real alternatives before the yes-or-no frame hides better paths.
   c) The prototype planner should keep the frame tight so the evidence review stays manageable.

Q3. The product lead starts a research file after the team already likes one plan. Which move best guards against confirmation bias?
   a) The product lead should collect a stronger file for the favorite plan before critics arrive.
   b) The product lead should ask supporters to polish the case so objections are easier to answer.
   c) The product lead should seek disconfirming evidence before the favorite plan sets the search.

Q4. During a long-term budget call, you feel attached to an old service line that may no longer fit. Which response uses the emotion lesson well?
   a) You should ignore the attachment and treat all emotion as noise in the budget call.
   b) You should acknowledge the attachment, then ask what a new leader without it would decide, to gain distance.
   c) You should let the attachment speak, then ask what will still matter later.

Q5. At the launch board, the Intel reviewer sees one neat forecast line and a confident owner. What would most directly counter overconfidence?
   a) The Intel reviewer should map a wider range of plausible outcomes before trusting the forecast.
   b) The Intel reviewer should treat the upbeat forecast as useful proof that the launch is sound.
   c) The Intel reviewer should read confidence as optimism and adjust only if the mood turns negative.

Q6. Before a prototype budget is spent, the launch planner is planning the first phase of a HopeLab-style design search. Which choice follows Steve Cole's logic?
   a) The launch planner should wait until one firm looks safest, then buy a longer single-firm pitch.
   b) The launch planner should cut the field early so the first design brief costs less to manage.
   c) The launch planner should fund parallel first passes so comparison teaches before commitment.

Q7. In a strategy memo, the team lead is stuck between a legacy product and a stronger new market. How should she use the outsider question?
   a) The team lead should ask what a fresh leader would do without the old-product attachment.
   b) The team lead should ask an outsider to decide because outside leaders know the technology better.
   c) The team lead should protect the legacy product until the old attachment fades on its own.

Q8. The decision lead proposes one final review to catch every decision flaw at once. Which response best fits the four-villain idea?
   a) The decision lead should interrupt framing, evidence, emotion, and forecasting with different moves.
   b) The decision lead should hold one careful final review after the whole choice is assembled.
   c) The decision lead should treat the four villains as one caution about being more thoughtful.

Q9. The product lead's team used WRAP and still got a rough outcome. What should she defend about the process?
   a) The product lead should promise that a clean WRAP pass will make the next outcome right.
   b) The product lead should defend the process as improving odds while uncertainty remains.
   c) The product lead should drop the process when the result cannot be known ahead of time.

## ANSWER KEY (with explanations)
Q1: b — WRAP is not one soft warning. Each move counters a different stage: frame, evidence, emotion, and forecast.
Q2: b — Narrow framing happens before data review. More facts can still serve a cramped yes-or-no choice if the option set stays small.
Q3: c — Confirmation bias can be sincere. The repair is to gather evidence that can challenge the favored plan, not just defend it.
Q4: c — The target is short-term distortion, not emotion itself. Distance lets the feeling be heard without letting it own the long-term call.
Q5: a — Overconfidence is misplaced certainty about prediction accuracy. A range of outcomes keeps one imagined future from posing as fact.
Q6: c — Parallel exploration is not indecision. It buys comparison data before the team locks onto one design path.
Q7: a — Grove's question worked by creating distance from attachment. It did not assume outsiders had better technical knowledge.
Q8: a — The villains strike at different stages. A late review cannot restore options or evidence that never entered the choice.
Q9: b — Good process does not guarantee a result. It improves the choice by widening evidence and preparing for variance.
