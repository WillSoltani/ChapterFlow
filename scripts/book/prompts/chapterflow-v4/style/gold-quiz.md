## Question 1

### Prompt
Daria is a senior engineer who just solved a production outage that saved her company a major client. Her director, Raj, was on vacation during the crisis. In the post-incident review meeting with the VP, Daria is deciding how to present what happened. What approach best protects her position while ensuring she benefits from the win?

### Choices
- A) Walk the VP through every technical decision she made, establishing her as the clear hero of the crisis
- B) Frame the resolution as a result of the incident response protocols Raj implemented before his vacation
- C) Downplay the severity of the incident so neither she nor Raj draws attention

### Correct
B

### Explanation

#### Gentle
Nice catch. By connecting the save to Raj's protocols, Daria makes Raj look like someone whose systems work even when he is away. Raj becomes invested in Daria's success because her wins validate his leadership. The temptation to claim the spotlight is strong, but it risks making Raj feel outshone.

#### Direct
Correct. Framing the resolution as flowing from Raj's preparation converts Daria's individual heroics into evidence of Raj's good leadership. This is complementary positioning: Daria's competence makes Raj look better, which makes Raj protect and promote her.

#### Competitive
Expert answer. The trap in option A is that it positions Daria as a substitute for Raj, someone who thrives when he is absent. Option B makes her a complement whose success proves his judgment. Option C wastes a reputational deposit opportunity entirely.

## Question 2

### Prompt
Marcus just received a glowing client testimonial that specifically praises his negotiation skills. His boss, Elena, will see the testimonial in tomorrow's team meeting. Marcus notices that Elena has been unusually short with him lately after he closed two big deals back-to-back. What should Marcus do before that meeting?

### Choices
- A) Forward the testimonial to Elena privately with a note thanking her for coaching him on client relationships
- B) Share the testimonial in the team Slack channel so everyone sees it before the meeting
- C) Hold the testimonial back and wait for a less tense moment to share it

### Correct
A

### Explanation

#### Gentle
Nice catch. Elena's recent coldness suggests she may already feel outshone by Marcus's deal streak. Forwarding the testimonial privately with credit to her coaching lets Elena absorb the win as partly hers before the public meeting. This disarms the threat response before it escalates.

#### Direct
Correct. The signals are clear: back-to-back wins plus a change in Elena's behavior indicate a status threat is building. Option A preempts it by converting the testimonial from evidence of Marcus's superiority into evidence of Elena's mentorship. Option B amplifies the threat. Option C wastes a valuable credit-giving opportunity.

#### Competitive
Expert answer. Marcus is reading the early warning signs correctly. Elena's behavioral shift is the tell. The smart move is to spend the testimonial as credit directed upward before it becomes another data point making Elena feel replaceable. Timing here is everything.

## Question 3

### Prompt
Priya has been applying the strategy of crediting her boss publicly for months, but instead of warming up, her boss has become more suspicious and started micromanaging her work. What does this pattern most likely indicate?

### Choices
- A) Priya is dealing with a deeply paranoid superior for whom more deference increases suspicion rather than building trust
- B) Priya is not giving credit frequently enough to overcome her boss's initial negative impression
- C) Priya's work quality has quietly declined without her noticing, causing legitimate concern

### Correct
A

### Explanation

#### Gentle
Nice catch. When increased deference produces more scrutiny rather than more trust, it signals you are dealing with someone who reads your humility as a calculated move. The chapter warns that this law has a boundary condition: deeply paranoid superiors cannot be reassured, and trying harder makes things worse.

#### Direct
Correct. This is the failure mode Greene identifies. Paranoid superiors interpret deference as manipulation. The diagnostic is clear: if repeated credit-giving produces more surveillance rather than more warmth, the situation is not solvable with this tool. The correct response is distance, not more deference.

#### Competitive
Expert answer. The trap is option B, the instinct to try harder. This creates a spiral: more deference, more suspicion, more deference. Recognizing the boundary condition and switching to a distance strategy separates the sophisticated operator from someone trapped in an escalating loop.

## Question 4

### Prompt
If you consistently frame your contributions as supporting your manager's vision, you risk being seen as someone without original ideas. How does the chapter suggest resolving the tension between strategic deference and building your own reputation?

### Choices
- A) Alternate between crediting your manager and claiming results independently so both signals reach leadership
- B) Treat deference as a temporary phase that builds a foundation of support, then gradually increase independence as your position strengthens
- C) Focus entirely on deference until you are promoted, then switch to an independent reputation overnight

### Correct
B

### Explanation

#### Gentle
Nice catch. The chapter frames this as strategic patience, not permanent submission. Early deference builds resources and protection, then standing grows strong enough to support more independence. The deference is scaffolding, not the finished structure.

#### Direct
Correct. The law prescribes sequencing, not permanent passivity. Phase one: make the boss feel brilliant and secure your position. Phase two: use the accumulated support and resources to build work that speaks for itself. The transition is gradual, not abrupt.

#### Competitive
Expert answer. Option A creates inconsistency that a perceptive boss will notice. Option C is a jarring switch that feels like betrayal. The correct approach is a gradient, slowly increasing independence as your accumulated standing makes the transition natural rather than threatening.

## Question 5

### Prompt
A new employee believes that in a fair workplace, great work should speak for itself without needing to credit anyone else. The chapter presents evidence against this belief. What is the strongest counterargument the employee could make, and why does the chapter still recommend strategic deference despite it?

### Choices
- A) The employee is correct in purely meritocratic environments, but most environments are hierarchies disguised as meritocracies, and the catastrophic cost of misjudging makes deference the safer default
- B) The employee is wrong because all workplaces are inherently political and merit never matters
- C) The employee should test whether their workplace is truly meritocratic by outshining their boss once to see what happens

### Correct
A

### Explanation

#### Gentle
Nice catch. The strongest case against this law is that genuinely secure leaders welcome being outshone. The chapter acknowledges this but argues the risk is asymmetric: unnecessary deference costs you a little visibility, while accidentally triggering a vulnerable superior can cost you everything.

#### Direct
Correct. The chapter does not claim merit never matters. It claims that in hierarchies claiming to be meritocracies, the person evaluating your work is affected by whether it makes them feel strong or weak. The asymmetric risk argument makes deference the dominant strategy under uncertainty.

#### Competitive
Expert answer. Option B is the cynical misread. Option C is empirically dangerous. The sophisticated position is A: acknowledge that meritocracy exists in some environments while recognizing that the cost of being wrong about your environment is so high that the expected-value calculation favors consistent application.
