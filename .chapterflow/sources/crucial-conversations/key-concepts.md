# Crucial Conversations — Key Concepts

Glossary of the stable framework vocabulary used throughout the book.
All entries are original paraphrases written for the ChapterFlow
pipeline. No verbatim definitions from the book are reproduced here.

## The definition of a crucial conversation

A conversation is "crucial" when three conditions are present at once:

1. **High stakes.** Something the speaker genuinely cares about is on
   the line — a job, a relationship, a reputation, a major decision.
2. **Opposing opinions.** The people in the conversation do not already
   agree, and they know it.
3. **Strong emotions.** At least one person is feeling something
   intense enough that it is starting to interfere with their ability
   to think clearly.

Any one of these conditions on its own produces discomfort, but not a
crucial conversation. The combination is what distinguishes a
conversation that will shape events from one that is merely awkward.

## The Pool of Shared Meaning

The central metaphor of the book. Two people in real dialogue are each
pouring their facts, stories, feelings, and opinions into a common
pool. The decision they make at the end of the conversation is only as
good as the pool is full. When both people are contributing what they
actually think, the resulting decision is better and both people feel
committed to it. When one or both are holding back, the decision suffers
and the person holding back usually ends up undermining it later,
sometimes without knowing they are doing so.

Implication: the core skill of crucial conversations is not persuasion.
It is keeping the pool filling while emotions are running high.

## Dialogue

The book's technical term for the state in which both people are still
contributing to the pool. Dialogue is not a tone of voice or a style —
it is a behavioral condition. Two people speaking calmly can be out of
dialogue (both performing politeness while withholding their real
positions). Two people arguing loudly can be in dialogue (both actually
saying what they think). The question is always whether the pool is
still filling, not how loud or calm the room is.

## Silence and Violence

The two ways people leave dialogue under stress. When safety slips,
people either pull their contribution out of the pool (silence) or try
to force meaning into the pool through pressure (violence). Both stop
the real conversation.

**Silence** has three common forms:
- **Masking** — understating, sugarcoating, being vague on purpose.
- **Avoiding** — steering the conversation around the actual issue.
- **Withdrawing** — physically or emotionally leaving the
  conversation (going quiet, changing the subject, ending the
  meeting).

**Violence** has three common forms:
- **Controlling** — cutting others off, speaking in absolutes, forcing
  one view onto the room.
- **Labeling** — reducing the other person to a stereotype or
  category ("a typical engineer," "a classic millennial").
- **Attacking** — insults, threats, belittling.

Most people default to one pattern (silence OR violence) in most
contexts but will switch under extreme pressure.

## Content vs. Conditions

The two channels a skilled operator watches in every conversation.

- **Content** — the topic being discussed.
- **Conditions** — the emotional and relational state of the people
  discussing it: how safe each person feels, whether they are still
  in dialogue, whether they believe they are being heard.

The novice tracks content. The skilled operator tracks both. The
skilled operator also knows that a breach in conditions will tank
even an easy content conversation, while solid conditions can carry
a hard content conversation to a good outcome.

## Start With Heart

The internal skill of clarifying what you actually want from the
conversation before speaking, and noticing when your goal has shifted
under pressure. Driven by two questions asked in the middle of the
heated moment:

1. "What do I really want — for myself, for the other person, for the
   relationship?"
2. "How would I behave right now if that was actually what I wanted?"

The test is whether the answer to the second question matches the
thing the speaker is about to do. Usually it doesn't.

## The Fool's Choice (Sucker's Choice)

The false binary the brain offers up under stress. "I can be honest
OR I can preserve the relationship." "I can speak up OR I can be
kind." "I can tell the truth OR I can keep my job." The binaries feel
true in the moment because the brain under stress narrows its option
set. The skilled move is to notice the binary, refuse it, and ask
"how can I be both?"

## The Path to Action

The book's model of how a person gets from observing something to
doing something about it:

    SEE/HEAR (fact)  →  TELL A STORY (interpretation)
                     →  FEEL (emotion)
                     →  ACT (behavior)

Most people experience this sequence as compressed. They see
something, they feel an emotion, they act. The story step is
invisible to them, so their reaction feels like a direct response to
the facts. It isn't — the reaction is a response to the story they
told themselves about the facts. Different story, different reaction.

The diagnostic move is to reverse the path when you notice a strong
reaction: start with the emotion, work backward to the story, then
check the story against the actual facts.

## Victim / Villain / Helpless Stories

The three most common unhelpful stories people tell themselves under
stress.

- **Victim story** — "This is not my fault." Casts the speaker as an
  innocent party with no responsibility. Feels relieving but cuts
  off any useful action.
- **Villain story** — "It's all their fault." Reduces the other
  person to a single bad trait and uses it to explain everything
  they did. Feels righteous but makes dialogue impossible.
- **Helpless story** — "There's nothing else I could do." Closes off
  options in advance. Feels honest but is almost always false on
  examination.

Counter-questions:
- For Victim stories: "Am I pretending not to notice my own role in
  this problem?"
- For Villain stories: "Why would a reasonable, rational, decent
  person do what this person did?"
- For Helpless stories: "What do I actually want, and what would I
  need to do to get it?"

## Mutual Purpose and Mutual Respect

The two requirements for safety in a conversation.

- **Mutual Purpose** — both people believe the other is working
  toward a goal they both care about. Without it, one person
  assumes the other is there to harm or use them.
- **Mutual Respect** — both people believe the other sees them as
  an equal worth hearing. Without it, the conversation cannot
  continue in good faith, no matter how reasonable the content is.

If either one is missing, the other person will leave dialogue. The
repair always starts with diagnosing which one is missing.

## Contrasting

A two-part statement used to correct a misunderstanding without
collapsing the point being made. Format: "I do NOT mean X. I DO mean
Y." The "do not" half addresses the other person's misreading; the
"do" half reaffirms the actual purpose. Order matters: correct the
misinterpretation first, then restate the honest point.

Example shape: "I'm not saying your work has been bad, and I'm not
trying to start a performance conversation. I am saying the last two
releases have missed their dates, and I want to understand why
before it becomes a bigger issue."

Contrasting only works when the "do not" half is true — when the
speaker actually didn't mean the thing the other person heard.
Mechanical use of the template without genuine meaning will sound
worse than nothing.

## CRIB

The four-step move for rebuilding Mutual Purpose when it has broken
down.

- **C — Commit** to seek a mutual purpose. Declare out loud that
  you want an outcome that works for both of you, and that you will
  stay in the conversation until you find one.
- **R — Recognize** the purpose behind the strategy. Ask what the
  other person is really trying to achieve underneath the position
  they are stating. Most fights are over strategies disguised as
  fights over values.
- **I — Invent** a mutual purpose. If neither of your stated
  positions works for both of you, climb up one level of
  abstraction and find a goal both of you actually share.
- **B — Brainstorm** new strategies that serve that shared purpose.

## STATE My Path

The framework for saying a hard thing without collapsing the other
person's safety.

- **S — Share your facts.** Start with observable, verifiable data —
  the least inflammatory, most objective ground. Not interpretations,
  not characterizations: actual facts.
- **T — Tell your story.** Then share what story you are telling
  yourself about those facts. Own it as a story, not as the truth.
- **A — Ask for others' paths.** Invite the other person to add
  their facts and their story. Genuinely invite — not as a
  rhetorical setup to get them to agree with you.
- **T — Talk tentatively.** Soften the language. "I'm starting to
  wonder," "it seems to me," "I could be wrong, but." Not because
  the speaker is unsure, but because the goal is to keep the other
  person in dialogue.
- **E — Encourage testing.** Explicitly welcome disagreement. Tell
  the other person you want them to push back if the story doesn't
  match their experience, and mean it.

## AMPP

The inverse of STATE — the framework for drawing out someone who is
holding back or escalating.

- **A — Ask.** Start with a genuine open question. Not a leading
  question; a real request for their view.
- **M — Mirror.** If their emotion doesn't match their words, name
  it gently out loud. ("You're saying it's fine, but you seem
  frustrated — am I reading that right?")
- **P — Paraphrase.** When they share, play it back in your own
  words to show you received it.
- **P — Prime.** If they are still reluctant and you have a guess at
  what they might be afraid to say, offer it tentatively as a guess.
  Priming is risky — done wrong it sounds like putting words in
  their mouth — but done with real humility it can give the other
  person permission to confirm a fear they were holding back.

## ABC

After both paths are on the table, the closing moves for working
with genuine disagreement.

- **A — Agree** where you actually agree, and say so explicitly.
- **B — Build** on what the other person said by adding what they
  missed. ("Yes, and.")
- **C — Compare** when you genuinely see it differently. "Here's
  what I'm seeing" — not "you're wrong."

## The four decision-making methods

A decision about how to decide, which should be made explicitly
before the substantive decision.

- **Command** — someone with authority decides. Fast; appropriate
  for low-stakes decisions and emergencies. Cost: the people who
  didn't decide have to be persuaded to care about the outcome.
- **Consult** — the decision-maker gathers input from a defined set
  of people, then decides. Most good organizational decisions get
  made this way. Failure mode: pretending to consult when the
  decision is already made.
- **Vote** — a group decides by majority. Fast, legitimate,
  alienates the minority. Appropriate when any option on the table
  would be acceptable.
- **Consensus** — everyone agrees. Slow, expensive, appropriate for
  decisions where universal buy-in is actually necessary. Most
  overused in groups that confuse consensus with fairness.

## WWWF

The closing move for any crucial conversation that produces a
decision. Ask and answer, out loud:

- **Who** — a specific person. "The team" and "we" are not people.
- **What** — a specific, observable deliverable. "Improve the docs"
  is not a deliverable; "update the installation page with the new
  env var list" is.
- **When** — a specific date. "Soon" is not a date.
- **Follow-up** — how will everyone know the thing was done? Who
  checks? When?

Readers who install WWWF as a reflex at the end of every important
conversation get more out of the framework immediately, independent
of the rest.
