# Crucial Conversations — Chapter Map

This document maps the book's structure to the teaching structure the
ChapterFlow orchestrator should derive for the modern package. The
original book (3rd edition, 2021) has eleven chapters plus a short
"Yeah, But" chapter of tough cases; the ChapterFlow package consolidates
these into a linear ten-chapter teaching sequence optimized for modern
readers.

All chapter titles in this document are original paraphrases, not
verbatim chapter headings from the book. Where the book's exact chapter
titles are stable across editions (e.g., "Start With Heart," "STATE My
Path") the teaching sequence may re-use the same short phrases because
they are part of the widely-documented framework vocabulary, but the
longer titles and all teaching language are original.

## Original book structure (3rd edition, 2021)

The third edition keeps the framework from earlier editions and updates
the examples for remote/hybrid work and identity-charged conversations.
The eleven chapters, in the book's order:

**Part I — What to Do Before You Open Your Mouth**
1. What's a Crucial Conversation? And Who Cares?
2. Mastering Crucial Conversations: The Power of Dialogue
3. Start With Heart: How to Stay Focused on What You Really Want
4. Master My Stories: How to Stay in Dialogue When You're Angry, Scared,
   or Hurt

**Part II — How to Open Your Mouth**
5. Learn to Look: How to Notice When Safety Is at Risk
6. Make It Safe: How to Make It Safe to Talk About Almost Anything
7. STATE My Path: How to Speak Persuasively, Not Abrasively
8. Explore Others' Paths: How to Listen When Others Blow Up or Clam Up

**Part III — How to Finish**
9. Move to Action: How to Turn Crucial Conversations into Action and
   Results
10. Yeah, But: Advice for Tough Cases
11. Putting It All Together: Tools for Preparing and Learning

> **Uncertainty flag:** The exact chapter titles above are paraphrased
> from the stable framework vocabulary, not lifted verbatim from the 3rd
> edition's table of contents. If the orchestrator needs exact titles
> for a bibliographic citation, defer to the user to confirm against a
> physical copy.

## Recommended ChapterFlow teaching structure (10 chapters)

The teaching sequence below reorders two chapters from the book. The
book places "Learn to Look" before "Make It Safe," and places "Master My
Stories" between them; the teaching sequence moves "Master My Stories"
up to sit next to "Start With Heart" because both are internal skills
and share the same underlying move (slow down your own reaction before
acting on the room). This pairing makes the internal/external division
of the book more obvious to a modern reader.

### Chapter 1 — What a Crucial Conversation Actually Is
**Covers:** The book's Chapter 1 — definition of a crucial conversation
(high stakes + differing opinions + strong emotion) and why most people
handle them badly.

**Teaching focus:** Convert the reader's vague "I'm bad at conflict"
into a specific recognition of the three conditions and their own
typical default (avoid, handle badly, handle well). By the end of the
chapter, the reader should be able to name a conversation in their own
recent past that qualified and say which default they used.

**Modern examples to generate:**
- A performance review that both people knew was coming for weeks and
  both spent that time rehearsing in their head.
- A conversation about household finances that keeps almost starting.
- A Slack DM sent at 11pm that the sender regrets by morning.

**Avoid:** Framing the chapter as "conflict = bad." The book is pointed
in the other direction: the cost is in the conversations people skip.

### Chapter 2 — The Pool of Shared Meaning
**Covers:** The book's Chapter 2 — dialogue as a flow of facts, stories,
feelings, and opinions into a shared pool; the diagnostic that every
failed crucial conversation is a pool that stopped filling.

**Teaching focus:** Install the pool metaphor as a durable mental model
the reader can use to diagnose their own conversations in retrospect
and — harder — to notice in real time. Emphasize that the enemy of the
pool is a loss of safety, not a loss of politeness.

**Modern examples to generate:**
- A hybrid meeting where the remote attendees go quiet and the team
  lead mistakes silence for agreement.
- A family dinner where the quietest person has the most to say and
  nobody notices.

**Avoid:** Turning the pool into a mushy "let's all share our feelings"
image. The pool is about information and candor, not about emotional
venting.

### Chapter 3 — Start With Heart
**Covers:** The book's Chapter 3 — focusing on what you really want, and
refusing the Fool's Choice (the false binary between honesty and
relationship).

**Teaching focus:** The internal work of noticing when your own goals
have shifted mid-conversation. The two questions: "What do I really
want?" and "What would I do right now if that was what I wanted?" Plus
the refusal move: when the brain offers a binary, look for the third
option.

**Modern examples to generate:**
- A new manager giving feedback to a long-time peer.
- A co-founder conversation about equity.
- A partner conversation about a family decision where the speaker
  wants to both hold the line and stay connected.

**Avoid:** Framing this as a "state your needs" chapter. The skill is
noticing that under pressure your needs have silently been replaced
with lower-grade goals (win, avoid, not look stupid).

### Chapter 4 — Master My Stories
**Covers:** The book's Chapter 4 — the Path to Action (see → story →
feel → act), the three common unhelpful stories (Victim, Villain,
Helpless), and the counter-questions.

**Teaching focus:** Slow the reader's own Path to Action down in slow
motion until the "story" step becomes visible. The chapter's payoff is
a specific diagnostic: "what story am I telling myself right now?"
followed by "what facts do I actually have?"

**Modern examples to generate:**
- A designer furious about engineering shipping without consulting
  her — the villain story that dissolves into a broken process.
- A parent at a school meeting running a victim story ("there was
  nothing else I could do") that is, on examination, not true.
- A team lead running a helpless story about a difficult stakeholder.

**Avoid:** Any framing that implies emotions are "irrational" or
"wrong." The chapter is not about suppressing feelings. It is about
noticing that feelings follow stories, and that stories can be
revised.

### Chapter 5 — Learn to Look
**Covers:** The book's Chapter 5 — the dual-channel skill of tracking
content and conditions at once, plus the specific signs that one or
both people have left dialogue (silence: masking/avoiding/withdrawing;
violence: controlling/labeling/attacking).

**Teaching focus:** The reader should finish this chapter able to
name the specific move they are about to make ("I am about to mask,"
"I am about to label") and able to spot those same moves in others.
Naming the move is most of the skill.

**Modern examples to generate:**
- A Zoom design review where a junior colleague's face tightens and
  they go silent for fifteen minutes.
- A relationship conversation where one partner starts labeling ("you
  always...") and the other starts withdrawing.
- A text-message thread where masking is easier to spot than in
  speech because the hedging words are right there on the screen.

**Avoid:** Treating silence and violence as a personality binary ("I'm
a withdrawer, she's a fighter"). Everyone does both, usually in
different contexts.

### Chapter 6 — Make It Safe
**Covers:** The book's Chapter 6 — the hinge of the whole framework.
Safety requires Mutual Purpose and Mutual Respect. The repair moves:
genuine apology, Contrasting, and CRIB (Commit, Recognize, Invent,
Brainstorm).

**Teaching focus:** Teach the reader to diagnose which element of
safety is missing (purpose or respect) and deploy the matching repair.
Contrasting in particular is the most practical technique in the book
and should get the most practice.

**Modern examples to generate:**
- Two co-founders fighting about hiring where the CRIB move surfaces
  the actual shared purpose underneath the strategy fight.
- A manager using Contrasting to defuse a direct report who heard
  "your work is slipping" as "I don't believe in you anymore."
- A family member apologizing for real, not performatively.

**Avoid:** Presenting Contrasting as a rhetorical trick. It only works
when the "I do not mean X" half is genuine — when the speaker
actually didn't mean X. Readers who use it as a template without
meaning it will produce something worse than nothing.

### Chapter 7 — STATE My Path
**Covers:** The book's Chapter 7 — the STATE skill (Share facts, Tell
your story, Ask for others' paths, Talk tentatively, Encourage
testing).

**Teaching focus:** Teach STATE as a sequence the reader can follow
under pressure, with particular emphasis on the "Tell your story"
step, which is where most readers either collapse (skipping back to
facts) or overreach (presenting story as verdict). The tentative
language is the second most common failure point.

**Modern examples to generate:**
- A team lead flagging that a peer's project is slipping, STATE
  version vs. blunt version side by side.
- A parent raising a concern about a teenager's screen time.
- A physician raising a concern with a patient about a treatment
  adherence problem.

**Avoid:** Presenting STATE as a script. The sequence matters, but
the reader who performs it mechanically will sound worse than the
reader who ignores it. The skill is the underlying disposition;
STATE is the scaffolding.

### Chapter 8 — Explore Others' Paths
**Covers:** The book's Chapter 8 — AMPP (Ask, Mirror, Paraphrase,
Prime) and ABC (Agree, Build, Compare).

**Teaching focus:** The inverse of STATE — how to draw out someone
who is holding back or escalating. The chapter's high-leverage move is
mirroring ("you're saying it's fine, but you seem frustrated") because
it creates safety for the other person to admit what they were about
to withhold.

**Modern examples to generate:**
- A 1:1 with a disengaged direct report where "how are things?"
  produces "fine" and the manager has to mirror.
- A parent drawing out a teenager who won't say why they're upset.
- An engineer paraphrasing a product manager's concern to
  demonstrate understanding before pushing back.

**Avoid:** Teaching priming as a quick technique. Priming is risky —
done wrong it sounds like putting words in someone's mouth — and
should be taught with explicit cautions.

### Chapter 9 — Move to Action
**Covers:** The book's Chapter 9 — the four decision-making methods
(Command, Consult, Vote, Consensus), and the WWWF closer (Who does
What by When, and how will we Follow up).

**Teaching focus:** Most crucial conversations fail after the talking,
not during. The reader should leave this chapter installing WWWF as a
reflex at the end of every important conversation. The decision-method
vocabulary is a secondary win — it prevents the "I thought we were
voting, you thought we were consulting" failure mode.

**Modern examples to generate:**
- A product team leaving a two-hour meeting feeling "aligned" with no
  WWWF and no change in the backlog three weeks later.
- A family meeting about care for an aging parent.
- A startup all-hands where the CEO says "what do you think?" about a
  decision that is already made.

**Avoid:** Turning this into a project-management chapter. The focus
is on closing the conversation, not on running a task tracker.

### Chapter 10 — Putting It All Together and Tough Cases
**Covers:** The book's Chapters 10 and 11 — "Yeah, But" tough cases
and the closing integration chapter. Consolidate these into one
closing chapter for the package.

**Teaching focus:** Work through the three or four most common tough
cases — significantly more/less power, async text media, hostile
counter-party, identity-charged topic — and show that the framework
doesn't change in them; only the order and emphasis change. Close
with a specific, actionable prompt: pick one real conversation in
your life this week, plan it, and have it.

**Modern examples to generate:**
- A conversation with a manager two levels up.
- A difficult conversation happening over email because the other
  person won't take a call.
- A conversation across a cultural difference where directness means
  different things.

**Avoid:** Turning this chapter into a victory lap. The book's closing
tone is "this is hard, and you will keep getting it wrong, and that
is fine." The teaching prose should honor that.

## Skeleton dossier notes

For the orchestrator's skeleton artifacts, each chapter should include:

- **Core concept** — one sentence summary of the skill.
- **Three examples** — at least one mundane workplace scenario, at
  least one close-relationship scenario, at least one modern-medium
  scenario (text, video, async).
- **Tone object** — gentle, direct, competitive variants where
  applicable. The book's own tone is warm and pragmatic; the teaching
  prose should match.
- **Three-depth variants (EMH)**:
  - Easy — what the skill is, with a clean scenario where it works.
  - Medium — how to spot when you're not doing it, with a scenario
    requiring mid-conversation correction.
  - Hard — what to do when the other person is also skilled, or
    hostile, or the medium is constrained.
- **Recognition test** — a short scenario the reader can diagnose
  (which skill is being broken? what would fix it?).
- **Transition** — how this chapter sets up the next one, with an
  explicit callback on which previous skills are being composed.

## Variant family

`variantFamily: "EMH"` (Easy / Medium / Hard) matches the
`flagship_v4_compatible` output profile.

## Chapter range

Target: **10 chapters** in the release package. The orchestrator may
adjust by ±1 if the natural break suggests otherwise, but should not
exceed 11. The book's own final two chapters ("Yeah, But" and "Putting
It All Together") should stay consolidated into a single closing
chapter in the package.
