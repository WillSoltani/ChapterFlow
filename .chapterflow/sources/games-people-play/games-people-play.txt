GAMES PEOPLE PLAY — STRUCTURED REFERENCE DOCUMENT
===================================================
Author under study: Eric Berne
Original work: Games People Play: The Psychology of Human Relationships (Grove Press, 1964)
Document type: Structured reference material (paraphrased, original summary)
Purpose: Serve as source-of-truth reference for ChapterFlow v12 orchestrator to derive
         original teaching prose. This is NOT a reproduction of the book. All descriptions
         below are original summaries of Berne's publicly known framework, written for
         educational use. No verbatim text from the book appears in this document.

===================================================
PART 0 — AUTHOR AND CONTEXT
===================================================

Eric Berne (1910–1970) was a Canadian-born psychiatrist trained in the psychoanalytic
tradition. He broke with classical Freudian practice in the 1950s to develop what he
called Transactional Analysis (TA), a theory of personality and social interaction
built around three observable "ego states" and the moment-to-moment exchanges
between people.

Games People Play was published in 1964 and became an unexpected popular bestseller.
Its appeal was two-fold: it offered a plain-language vocabulary for patterns of behavior
that readers recognized in themselves and others, and it framed psychological theory
in a way that did not require therapeutic training to understand. The book is structured
in three parts: a theoretical section explaining ego states and transactions, a "thesaurus"
cataloging roughly three dozen recurring social games, and a short closing section on
what it means to live without games.

For purposes of the ChapterFlow pipeline, the teaching goals are:
  - Make Berne's framework legible to modern readers
  - Show how the concept of games applies to everyday life, work, and relationships
  - Update the 1964 framing where its assumptions have not aged well (see criticism-and-limits.md)
  - Equip the reader to recognize and step out of unhelpful interaction patterns

===================================================
PART I — ANALYSIS OF GAMES (theoretical framework)
===================================================

Chapter 1 — Structural Analysis
--------------------------------
Berne's theory starts with a simple claim: at any given moment, a person is operating
from one of three distinct ego states. These are not Freud's id/ego/superego (which are
abstractions) — they are observable ways of talking, thinking, and behaving.

The three ego states are:

  1. PARENT — Patterns learned from authority figures in childhood. The Parent state
     speaks in rules, judgments, "should"s and "shouldn't"s, and carries the voice of
     what was modeled by caregivers. The Parent can be Critical (restrictive, fault-finding)
     or Nurturing (supportive, caretaking). When someone says "that's just how it's done,"
     the Parent state is usually speaking.

  2. ADULT — The reality-processing state. The Adult observes, gathers data, evaluates
     probabilities, and makes decisions based on current information rather than past
     programming or emotional reaction. The Adult is neutral in tone and present-focused.
     It is the only state capable of updating beliefs based on new evidence.

  3. CHILD — Patterns from one's own childhood, still live inside the adult person.
     The Child state contains original emotions, creativity, curiosity, and also old
     adaptations to authority. It comes in variants: the Natural (or Free) Child is
     spontaneous and expressive; the Adapted Child has learned to comply or rebel in
     response to Parent-figures; the Little Professor is intuitive, clever, manipulative
     in a childlike way.

Key insight: everyone has all three states, and healthy functioning means being able
to move between them appropriately. Problems arise when one state contaminates another
(e.g., Parent prejudices presenting themselves as Adult "facts"), or when one state is
excluded entirely (e.g., a person who cannot access their Child for play or emotion).

Teaching implication: This model gives the reader a vocabulary to notice which part of
themselves is "speaking" in a given moment, and to consciously shift. It is less a
diagnostic system and more a live observational tool.

Chapter 2 — Transactional Analysis
-----------------------------------
A "transaction" is a single unit of social exchange: one person says or does something,
and the other person responds. Berne's contribution was to classify transactions by
which ego state sent the message and which ego state it was aimed at.

Three transaction types:

  A. COMPLEMENTARY — The response comes from the ego state that was addressed, and is
     aimed back at the ego state that sent the original message. These transactions
     are stable — the conversation can continue indefinitely because expectations are met.
     Examples: Adult-to-Adult request for information, Adult response. Parent-to-Child
     instruction, Child compliance. Child-to-Parent appeal for comfort, Parent soothes.

  B. CROSSED — The response comes from an unexpected ego state, or is aimed at a
     different ego state than the sender. These transactions break the conversation.
     One person expects an Adult answer and gets a Parent scolding; or one person
     addresses the other's Adult and the other responds from Child. Crossed transactions
     are the moment conversations "go sideways."

  C. ULTERIOR — The transaction has two levels: a social level (what's visibly said)
     and a psychological level (what's really going on). The words are one thing, but
     the actual message and response happen underneath. Berne argues that games are
     always built on ulterior transactions.

Teaching implication: Most communication failures are crossed transactions, and most
manipulation is ulterior. Learning to spot both in real time is a practical skill.

Chapter 3 — Procedures and Rituals
-----------------------------------
Berne distinguishes between several ways people structure time with others, ordered
roughly from least to most intimate:

  - Withdrawal: being physically present but mentally absent (daydreaming in a meeting).
  - Rituals: pre-programmed exchanges that require little Adult thought. Greetings,
    small talk on the elevator, "how are you / fine thanks." Rituals feel hollow but
    they're socially necessary — they signal safety without commitment.
  - Procedures: task-focused Adult-to-Adult exchanges with a practical goal (filling
    out a form, running a meeting with a clear agenda).
  - Pastimes: semi-ritualized conversation topics that groups return to ("PTA" talk
    about schools, "General Motors" talk about cars, complaining about the weather).
    Pastimes fill time safely and let people size each other up.
  - Games: ulterior transactions with a predictable payoff (the rest of the book).
  - Intimacy: genuine, game-free expression between people. Rare and, in Berne's view,
    the only truly satisfying mode of contact.

Teaching implication: People need a certain amount of "stroking" (acknowledgment from
others) to stay psychologically healthy. When they can't get it through intimacy, they
get it through pastimes and games. This is why games are so persistent — they are
reliable stroke-delivery systems, even when the strokes are negative.

Chapter 4 — Pastimes
--------------------
Pastimes are safe, repetitive conversational patterns. They're not games (no ulterior
motive, no clear winner and loser), but they're not intimate either. They're the social
equivalent of warming up before exercise — a way to meet new people, classify them,
and decide whether to go deeper.

Common pastime types Berne identified include:
  - "PTA" (complaining about delinquents, schools, kids today)
  - "General Motors" (cars, trucks, machines)
  - "Who Won" (sports results)
  - "Grocery" (prices, shortages, how things used to be cheaper)
  - "Wardrobe" (clothes and appearance)
  - "How To" (procedures and techniques)
  - "Ever Been" (places traveled, things seen)

Pastimes are low-risk because no one has to commit to anything or reveal anything real.
They can go on forever without escalating. They also serve as the entry hall from which
games are launched: you meet someone through a pastime, then discover you play
compatible games with them, and upgrade the relationship.

Chapter 5 — Games (the formal definition)
-------------------------------------------
Berne's technical definition: a game is an ongoing series of complementary ulterior
transactions progressing toward a well-defined, predictable outcome. Games are always
basically dishonest (the ulterior level contradicts the social level), and the outcome
— the "payoff" — is usually some form of bad feeling that both players are, underneath,
looking for.

Every game has these structural features:

  - THESIS: the basic plot or scenario of the game, including the roles, sequence of
    moves, and the eventual switch (the moment the social-level story collapses into
    the psychological-level payoff).
  - ANTITHESIS: what the other person would have to do to refuse to play — the move
    that breaks the game. The antithesis is usually uncomfortable because it requires
    ignoring the social-level rules and responding to the ulterior level directly.
  - DYNAMICS: the underlying psychological mechanism that keeps the game running.
  - ADVANTAGES / PAYOFFS: what the player gets out of running the game. Berne lists
    six of these:
      1. Internal psychological advantage — stabilizes the player's mental defenses
      2. External psychological advantage — avoids feared situations
      3. Internal social advantage — structured contact that feels familiar
      4. External social advantage — provides topics for pastimes later
      5. Biological advantage — supplies strokes (positive or negative) reliably
      6. Existential advantage — confirms the player's basic position about life
        ("I always get screwed," "people can't be trusted," etc.)

Berne also introduces the concept of LIFE POSITIONS — the four fundamental stances a
person takes toward themselves and others:
  - I'm OK, you're OK (healthy, intimate, game-free)
  - I'm OK, you're not OK (projective, blaming, often persecutory)
  - I'm not OK, you're OK (depressive, self-critical, often martyr-like)
  - I'm not OK, you're not OK (futile, often the position behind serious self-destruction)

Games are played to confirm the player's existing life position, over and over.

Teaching implication: Understanding why games persist — because they pay off, even
when the payoff is bad feelings — is more useful than trying to shame people out of
playing them. The route out is not willpower but finding a better source of strokes
(intimacy, meaningful activity, autonomy).

===================================================
PART II — A THESAURUS OF GAMES
===================================================

Berne catalogs roughly three dozen named games, grouped by the social setting where
they typically occur. Each entry below gives: (a) the game's familiar name, (b) the
thesis in plain terms, (c) the typical payoff, and (d) the antithesis — the move that
refuses to play.

Note on the game names: the names are Berne's original coinages, often ironic, and
are part of the factual historical record of the book. Some are dated and gendered
in ways that would be rewritten today (see criticism-and-limits.md).

---------------
A. LIFE GAMES
---------------
Life games are run over years or decades and tend to shape the player's whole
biography.

  * ALCOHOLIC
    Thesis: The player (Alcoholic) drinks, behaves badly, then is blamed, rescued,
    and eventually forgiven — cycling through a cast of Persecutor, Rescuer, and
    Patsy roles (and sometimes a Connection who supplies the drink).
    Payoff: confirmation that the player is bad and that life is unmanageable;
    attention and structured contact; avoidance of intimacy.
    Antithesis: refuse to play any of the roles. Neither persecute nor rescue.
    Decline the drama on all sides.

  * DEBTOR
    Thesis: the player takes on obligations they cannot realistically meet (loans,
    mortgages, promises), then structures their life around the drama of creditors,
    collection, escape, and near-miss survival.
    Payoff: a sense of purpose and drama; proof that the world is against them.
    Antithesis: refuse to lend; set clean boundaries that remove the drama.

  * KICK ME
    Thesis: the player behaves in ways that invite rejection, punishment, or
    criticism, then reacts with hurt astonishment when the predictable response
    arrives ("why does this always happen to me?").
    Payoff: confirmation of the "I'm not OK" life position; stroked by pity.
    Antithesis: do not kick. Respond with neutral Adult courtesy and refuse to
    play the persecutor role.

  * NOW I'VE GOT YOU, YOU S.O.B. (NIGYSOB)
    Thesis: the player looks for justified reasons to be outraged at someone, then
    uses the moment of catching them to release anger that was stored up long
    before the incident.
    Payoff: guilt-free expression of rage; moral superiority.
    Antithesis: perfect procedural correctness that gives no opening for the catch.

  * SEE WHAT YOU MADE ME DO (SWYMD)
    Thesis: the player's mistake is blamed on the person who interrupted, disturbed,
    or distracted them. The real mistake could easily have happened alone, but the
    presence of a witness lets the player offload responsibility.
    Payoff: avoidance of responsibility; control over others (they learn to keep
    their distance); confirmation that others are intrusive.
    Antithesis: refuse to accept the blame; calmly point out the real cause.

---------------
B. MARITAL GAMES
---------------
Games that typically occur between spouses or long-term partners.

  * CORNER
    Thesis: one partner maneuvers the other into a position where any choice they
    make proves the accusation against them. If they agree to the outing, they're
    "only doing it to humor me." If they decline, they're "never willing to try
    anything." There is no winning move.
    Payoff: confirmation that the partner is hopeless; avoidance of intimacy.
    Antithesis: notice the trap and name it openly; decline to choose inside the
    frame offered.

  * COURTROOM
    Thesis: the couple brings disputes to a third party (friend, therapist, family
    member) for judgment, each presenting their case as if to a judge. The real
    game is getting the third party to side with one partner against the other.
    Payoff: moral vindication; continuation of the dispute under cover of "seeking
    help."
    Antithesis: the third party refuses to judge and instead asks both partners to
    talk directly to each other.

  * FRIGID WOMAN (and its male equivalents)
    Thesis: one partner alternates between inviting closeness and withdrawing from
    it, keeping the other perpetually frustrated and therefore under control. The
    game's 1964 framing assumes a gendered dynamic that modern readers should
    update: the pattern is about intimacy avoidance, not gender.
    Payoff: proof that sex and intimacy are dangerous; moral high ground; control.
    Antithesis: refuse to pursue. Leave the door open without chasing through it.

  * HARRIED
    Thesis: the player overcommits to tasks, responsibilities, and crises until
    they collapse — at which point they feel entitled to rest, pity, and release
    from obligation. The exhaustion is both real and manufactured.
    Payoff: permission to stop; admiration for heroic effort; avoidance of
    confronting what the player actually wants.
    Antithesis: refuse to admire the busyness; help the player choose one thing
    at a time.

  * IF IT WEREN'T FOR YOU (IWFY)
    Thesis: the player blames the limits of their life on their partner. "If it
    weren't for you, I could have gone to art school / moved abroad / taken that
    job." The player often chose the partner precisely because they offered a
    convenient reason not to attempt the feared thing.
    Payoff: protection from the fear of trying and failing; moral grievance.
    Antithesis: remove the restriction. "Go ahead." Watch what happens.

  * LOOK HOW HARD I'VE TRIED
    Thesis: the player performs maximum visible effort at an impossible task so
    that when it fails, they can claim exemption from the outcome. Often played
    in marital therapy itself — the player is there to document their effort for
    the eventual divorce.
    Payoff: moral vindication; freedom from responsibility; preservation of the
    "I'm OK, you're not OK" position.
    Antithesis: name the pattern; refuse to serve as the witness to the trying.

  * SWEETHEART
    Thesis: the player tells a seemingly affectionate story about their partner
    in front of others — "sweetheart always..." — where the punchline is a veiled
    insult. The partner cannot object without looking humorless; laughing along
    confirms the slight.
    Payoff: aggression with plausible deniability; dominance; stored grievance
    released safely.
    Antithesis: the partner names the pattern directly and asks it to stop.

---------------
C. PARTY GAMES
---------------
Short-duration games that appear in social gatherings.

  * AIN'T IT AWFUL (AIA)
    Thesis: participants gather to deplore the state of the world, their jobs,
    their families, the economy, the younger generation, etc. The content is
    real complaints, but the function is bonding through shared grievance.
    Payoff: social strokes through agreement; avoidance of intimacy or action.
    Antithesis: reframe toward curiosity or action. Ask what they would actually
    do about it.

  * BLEMISH
    Thesis: the player enters any situation scanning for a flaw — a spot on the
    glassware, a typo in the document, an imperfection in the host's decor — and
    derives satisfaction from finding it. The flaw becomes the only topic.
    Payoff: superiority; protection from feeling inadequate (by finding others
    inadequate first).
    Antithesis: acknowledge the flaw calmly and move on.

  * SCHLEMIEL
    Thesis: the player commits minor offenses — spilling a drink, breaking an
    object, making a rude remark — and then, upon being forgiven, commits another.
    The forgiveness is the prize, not the offense.
    Payoff: proof that the player can misbehave and still be accepted (stroked).
    Antithesis: do not forgive on request. "Yes, you did that, and it was not okay."

  * WHY DON'T YOU — YES BUT (WDYYB)
    Thesis: the player presents a problem and invites suggestions. Each suggestion
    is met with "yes, but..." and a reason it won't work. The goal is not a solution;
    it is to demonstrate that no one can solve the problem, confirming the player's
    helplessness and the others' inadequacy.
    Payoff: confirmation that the problem is insoluble; strokes from others trying.
    Antithesis: refuse to offer solutions. Ask the player what they have already
    tried, or what they would recommend to a friend in the same situation.

---------------
D. SEXUAL GAMES
---------------
Berne's 1964 framings of sexual games are the most dated material in the book,
carrying assumptions about gender and desire that have not aged well. The
underlying patterns — control through attraction, jealousy as a social weapon,
avoidance dressed as desire — are real, but should be described in modern language.
See criticism-and-limits.md for notes on updating these.

  * LET'S YOU AND HIM FIGHT (LYAHF)
    Thesis: the player (usually cast as female in Berne's framing, though the
    pattern is gender-neutral) sets up a competition between two others for
    their attention. The payoff is neither competitor — it is the contest itself.
    Payoff: attention, drama, avoidance of committing to anyone.
    Antithesis: the pursuers refuse to compete. They talk to each other directly.

  * RAPO (original name; modern framing would change this)
    Thesis: the player signals sexual interest, allows the approach, then withdraws
    with indignation at the last moment. The game has three intensities in Berne's
    framing: mild flirtation, serious social embarrassment of the approacher, and
    an extreme third-degree variant involving formal accusation.
    Note: the naming and framing here are the most problematic in the book. The
    teaching frame in modern ChapterFlow material should focus on the broader
    pattern of invitation-followed-by-withdrawal as a control strategy, without
    the original name's implications.
    Antithesis: the approacher reads signals carefully, moves slowly, and accepts
    withdrawal as a "no" without drama.

  * THE STOCKING GAME
    Thesis: the player makes a provocative gesture ostensibly innocent in context
    (adjusting clothing in a particular way, in front of a particular audience)
    designed to provoke attention and then let the player deny that attention was
    sought. In Berne's framing this is specifically the behavior of a person whose
    Adult has been taken over by their Child's exhibitionism.
    Payoff: attention plus deniability.
    Antithesis: do not comment; do not react.

  * UPROAR
    Thesis: two people (often parent and adolescent, or spouses) escalate an
    argument to the point where one storms out and slams a door. The argument's
    content is a cover; the real function is to create enough distance to prevent
    intimacy.
    Payoff: avoidance of closeness; confirmation that the other person is impossible;
    structured contact that never threatens.
    Antithesis: refuse to escalate. Keep voice level, stay in the room, return to
    the original topic.

---------------
E. UNDERWORLD GAMES
---------------
Games Berne observed in criminal or quasi-criminal contexts.

  * COPS AND ROBBERS
    Thesis: the player (robber) commits crimes designed, at the ulterior level, to
    be caught. The chase, the almost-escape, the eventual capture is the real game,
    not the loot. Payoff is confirmation of the player's criminal identity and
    avoidance of life outside that identity.
    Payoff: identity, drama, structured contact with pursuers.
    Antithesis (social): recognize the pattern and intervene before it becomes
    entrenched, particularly in adolescence.

  * HOW DO YOU GET OUT OF HERE (prison game)
    Thesis: prisoners near the end of their sentence arrange to get caught at
    something that extends their stay. The outside world is frightening; the
    prison is structured and familiar.
    Payoff: avoidance of reintegration; structured time; preservation of identity.
    Antithesis: the system provides meaningful transition support.

  * LET'S PULL A FAST ONE ON JOEY
    Thesis: a con in which the mark believes they are in on the scam and is
    therefore willing to cooperate — until they discover they are the mark. The
    game depends on the mark's own willingness to cheat another party; without
    that willingness the con cannot run.
    Payoff: money for the con artist; for the mark, a grim confirmation that
    you cannot trust anyone.
    Antithesis: refuse to participate in any scheme that requires cheating an
    absent third party.

---------------
F. CONSULTING ROOM GAMES
---------------
Games Berne observed in therapy settings — by patients, but also sometimes by
therapists.

  * GREENHOUSE
    Thesis: the patient becomes an enthusiastic amateur therapist, using
    psychological vocabulary to observe and label their own feelings and other
    people's, while avoiding any actual change. The theory talk substitutes for
    emotional experience.
    Payoff: feels like progress without the risk of change.
    Antithesis: the therapist redirects to immediate feeling and concrete behavior.

  * I'M ONLY TRYING TO HELP YOU (ITHY)
    Thesis: a therapist (or parent, teacher, friend) offers help that the recipient
    predictably refuses or fails to use. When the help fails, the helper has proof
    that the recipient is hopeless and the helper is blameless. The helper's
    preferred position is "I'm OK, you're not OK — and I tried."
    Payoff: moral vindication for the helper; avoidance of genuine responsibility
    for the outcome.
    Antithesis: the recipient accepts the help fully and uses it. Or: the helper
    examines what they are actually trying to accomplish.

  * INDIGENCE
    Thesis: a welfare recipient performs the appearance of seeking work while
    ensuring that no job can actually be accepted. The caseworker, who needs
    clients to keep their job, plays the cooperating role. The system sustains
    itself on the surface of effort without the substance of change.
    Note: Berne's 1964 framing here reflects the welfare-reform politics of the
    era and should be updated with care.
    Payoff: stability for both sides; avoidance of feared change.
    Antithesis: clean accountability that breaks the pretended effort.

  * PEASANT
    Thesis: the patient treats the therapist as a wise sage whose every utterance
    is profound. The patient gets the stroking of being in contact with greatness;
    the therapist gets adoration. Nothing changes because the patient's role is
    to marvel, not to work.
    Payoff: strokes for both; avoidance of the uncomfortable work of change.
    Antithesis: the therapist refuses the pedestal.

  * PSYCHIATRY
    Thesis: a patient who has been in many therapies uses the specialized
    vocabulary of each school to conduct an intellectual tour of their problems.
    The content of what they say is about psychology; the function is to display
    knowledge and avoid the work.
    Payoff: intellectual strokes; avoidance of emotion.
    Antithesis: the therapist stays with immediate experience.

  * STUPID
    Thesis: the player presents themselves as slower, dumber, or less capable than
    they are. When tasks fail, the failure is expected and forgiven. Others lower
    their expectations permanently.
    Payoff: freedom from expectation and responsibility; confirmation of the
    "I'm not OK" position, which is familiar.
    Antithesis: hold expectations steady; refuse to lower the bar.

  * WOODEN LEG
    Thesis: the player uses a real or claimed disability — "what do you expect
    from a man with a wooden leg?" — as a permanent exemption from normal
    accountability. The disability is real; the game is the way it is deployed.
    Payoff: exemption; sympathy; avoidance of the feared attempt.
    Antithesis: hold the player accountable for what they can do, while fully
    accommodating what they cannot.

---------------
G. GOOD GAMES
---------------
Berne's acknowledgment that not all games are destructive. A "good game" is one
whose social contribution outweighs the complexity of its motives — i.e., the
payoff is still ulterior and imperfect, but the net effect on the world is positive.

  * BUSMAN'S HOLIDAY
    Thesis: the player takes their work expertise on vacation — a cook goes on a
    food tour, a doctor volunteers at a clinic abroad, a programmer goes to a
    hackathon on their week off. The motives are mixed (identity, escape from
    boredom, desire to be useful) but the effect is benign.
    Payoff: strokes, meaningful activity, structured time.

  * CAVALIER
    Thesis: the player expresses admiration, attention, and romantic compliments
    in a way that is explicitly free of sexual pursuit. The pleasure is in the
    giving of the stroke, not in the expectation of reciprocation.
    Payoff: strokes, social warmth, identity as a graceful person.

  * HAPPY TO HELP
    Thesis: the player offers help sincerely and without the trap that "I'm Only
    Trying to Help You" contains. The helper does not need the recipient to fail;
    success is what the helper wants.
    Payoff: meaningful contribution; strokes from gratitude.

  * HOMELY SAGE
    Thesis: the player, usually past middle age, shares hard-won wisdom with
    younger people without insisting on being heeded. Knowledge is offered as
    a gift, not a test. The listener is free to take it or leave it.
    Payoff: legacy, meaning, connection across generations.

  * THEY'LL BE GLAD THEY KNEW ME
    Thesis: the player commits to becoming notable in some honest way — a body
    of work, a reputation, a contribution — so that later their friends can take
    pleasure in having been close to it. The motive is partly vanity, but the
    work is real.
    Payoff: identity, accomplishment, and eventual legitimate social credit.

===================================================
PART III — BEYOND GAMES
===================================================

The closing section of the book argues that games, for all their social utility,
are a substitute for something better: direct, honest, game-free contact.

The Significance of Games
-------------------------
Games are how most people spend most of their social time. They are learned early
(Berne traces them to children's mimicry of parental patterns), they are handed down
across generations, and they are deeply sticky because they work — they deliver
strokes on a reliable schedule.

The Players
-----------
Berne is not contemptuous of people who play games. He notes that games are a
creative adaptation to the problem of needing strokes in a world that does not
always offer them freely. The problem is not that people play games; the problem
is that they do not have access to anything better.

A Good Life
-----------
Berne proposes that the goal is not to win games but to become able to stop playing
them — not because games are morally wrong, but because there is something better
available to a person who does not need them. That something is intimacy: direct,
ulterior-free contact between people.

Autonomy
--------
The final chapter names three qualities that together constitute autonomy — the
capacity to live without games:

  1. AWARENESS — the capacity to perceive the world directly, in the present, rather
     than through the filters of Parent programming or Child reaction.
  2. SPONTANEITY — the freedom to choose and express feelings from the full range of
     one's ego states, rather than being locked into pre-programmed responses.
  3. INTIMACY — game-free directness in relation to another person.

These three qualities are not a technique. They are what remains when the games fall
away. Berne's closing position is that autonomy is available, but it requires giving
up the strokes that games provide, and this is much harder than it sounds.

TEACHING NOTES FOR CHAPTERFLOW ORCHESTRATOR
===================================================

Recommended chapter breakdown for the ChapterFlow v4-compatible package (the
orchestrator should decide the final structure, but this is a sensible default):

  Chapter 1: The Three Voices Inside You (Ego States)
  Chapter 2: What a Transaction Is and Why They Go Wrong
  Chapter 3: Ulterior Transactions and the Anatomy of a Game
  Chapter 4: Why Games Feel So Hard to Stop (Strokes, Payoffs, Life Positions)
  Chapter 5: Life Games (Alcoholic, Kick Me, NIGYSOB, etc.)
  Chapter 6: Marital Games (the intimacy-avoidance patterns)
  Chapter 7: Party and Social Games (Ain't It Awful, Why Don't You Yes But)
  Chapter 8: Professional and Therapy-Room Games (I'm Only Trying to Help You, etc.)
  Chapter 9: Games That Aren't Worth Fighting (the "good games")
  Chapter 10: What Comes After Games (Awareness, Spontaneity, Intimacy)

For each chapter the orchestrator should derive:
  - Modern, non-gendered examples (the 1964 examples are frequently unusable)
  - Workplace and digital-age applications (Berne predates email, social media, remote work)
  - A recognition test so the reader can spot the pattern in their own life
  - A specific antithesis move they can try
  - Honest acknowledgment of where the framework is useful vs. where it flattens
    complexity (see criticism-and-limits.md)

Do not reproduce Berne's original case studies verbatim. The orchestrator should
generate fresh illustrative scenarios that teach the same pattern.
