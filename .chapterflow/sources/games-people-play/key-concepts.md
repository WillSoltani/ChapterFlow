# Key Concepts — Transactional Analysis Glossary

Original concepts from Eric Berne's Transactional Analysis framework, as presented
in Games People Play (1964) and developed further in his later work. All definitions
below are original summaries written for this reference document.

## Ego States

**Ego state** — A coherent pattern of feeling, thinking, and behaving that a person
moves in and out of. Berne claimed these are observable in real time, not inferred
from theory. Every person has all three at all times, though at any given moment
only one is "in charge."

**Parent (P)** — The ego state containing everything a person absorbed from authority
figures in early life: rules, judgments, prohibitions, care, worry, "shoulds." Speaks
in the voice of what was modeled.
- **Critical Parent** — restrictive, fault-finding, punitive
- **Nurturing Parent** — soothing, protective, caretaking
- Both sides are drawn from external models; neither is original to the person.

**Adult (A)** — The reality-processing ego state. Observes, gathers current data,
estimates probabilities, decides based on present evidence rather than past
programming. Neutral in tone. The only ego state capable of updating itself.

**Child (C)** — The ego state containing the person's own childhood feelings and
adaptations, still live inside the adult.
- **Natural Child (Free Child)** — spontaneous, expressive, playful, curious
- **Adapted Child** — the part that learned to comply with, rebel against, or work
  around authority figures
- **Little Professor** — an intuitive, manipulative, cleverly creative aspect of
  the Child that often operates outside conscious awareness

## Structural Problems

**Contamination** — When one ego state is masquerading as another. The most common
form: Parent prejudices presenting themselves as Adult observations ("I'm just
stating facts"). Can also run the other way: Child fears presenting as Adult
caution.

**Exclusion** — When one ego state is cut off and rarely used. A person with an
excluded Child cannot play or feel; a person with an excluded Adult cannot reason
under emotional pressure; a person with an excluded Parent cannot hold norms or
make moral judgments.

## Transactions

**Transaction** — A single unit of social exchange. One stimulus, one response.
The unit of analysis in transactional analysis.

**Complementary transaction** — The response comes from the ego state that was
addressed and returns to the state that sent the stimulus. Conversations built
of complementary transactions can continue indefinitely without breakdown.

**Crossed transaction** — The response comes from a different ego state than
expected, or is aimed at a different ego state than the sender's. Crossed
transactions break the conversation. They are the signature moment of
"misunderstanding."

**Ulterior transaction** — A transaction with two levels: the social level (what
is said out loud) and the psychological level (what is actually being communicated).
Berne holds that games are always built on ulterior transactions.

**Angular transaction** — An ulterior transaction in which one ego state is
openly addressed at the social level while a different ego state is secretly
addressed at the psychological level. Classic example: a salesperson making
an Adult-to-Adult remark about a product that is really a Child-level
appeal to the buyer's vanity.

**Duplex transaction** — An ulterior transaction in which both levels address
different ego states simultaneously. This is the structure of most games.

## Strokes and Time Structuring

**Stroke** — A unit of social recognition. Any acknowledgment from another
person that says "I know you exist." Can be positive (warmth, praise) or negative
(criticism, anger). Berne's claim: humans need strokes the way they need food,
and a person deprived of strokes will accept negative strokes rather than none
at all.

**Stroke economy** — The implicit rules in a culture or family about when and
how strokes can be given, received, asked for, or refused. Many cultures teach
people to give strokes sparingly, which creates stroke scarcity and increases
the appeal of games that deliver strokes reliably.

**Time structuring** — The six ways Berne identified for filling time with other
people, from least to most contact:
1. **Withdrawal** — physically present, mentally absent (daydreaming, scrolling)
2. **Rituals** — pre-programmed social exchanges ("how are you?" "fine")
3. **Pastimes** — semi-structured small talk on safe topics
4. **Activities (Procedures)** — task-focused work or projects
5. **Games** — ulterior-transaction sequences with a predictable bad-feeling payoff
6. **Intimacy** — game-free, ulterior-free direct contact

The paradox: the higher up the list, the more strokes are available, and the more
risk. Most people live mostly in the middle of the list.

## Game Structure

**Game** — A sequence of complementary ulterior transactions progressing to a
well-defined, predictable outcome that includes a bad feeling (the payoff). Games
are always basically dishonest.

**Thesis** — The basic plot of the game, including roles, sequence of moves, and
the switch to the payoff.

**Antithesis** — The move that would refuse to play the game. Usually uncomfortable
because it requires ignoring the social-level cue and responding at the psychological
level.

**Switch** — The moment in a game when the social-level story breaks down and the
ulterior payoff is delivered. The switch is often marked by a sudden reversal of
roles (Karpman's drama triangle, developed later, formalized this).

**Payoff** — What each player actually gets out of the game. Usually a stored-up
bad feeling (grief, anger, guilt, confusion) that is released in a socially
structured way. The payoff confirms the player's basic life position.

**Advantages** — Berne's six categories of what keeps a player running a game:
1. **Biological advantage** — reliable supply of strokes (even negative ones)
2. **Existential advantage** — confirmation of the player's life position
3. **Internal psychological advantage** — maintenance of psychic equilibrium
4. **External psychological advantage** — avoidance of feared situations
5. **Internal social advantage** — structured contact that is safer than intimacy
6. **External social advantage** — material for future pastimes ("let me tell you
   what happened")

## Life Positions

Berne identified four basic positions a person takes toward themselves and others,
usually formed in early childhood and then confirmed repeatedly through games:

1. **I'm OK, you're OK** — The only healthy position. Compatible with game-free
   intimacy. Rare as a default, achievable as a practice.
2. **I'm OK, you're not OK** — Projective, blaming, often persecutory. The person
   is protected from their own inadequacy by locating it in others. Games from
   this position tend to be about catching others in the wrong.
3. **I'm not OK, you're OK** — Depressive, self-critical, often martyr-like. The
   person sees themselves as fundamentally less than others. Games from this
   position tend to be about inviting confirmation of inadequacy.
4. **I'm not OK, you're not OK** — Futile, hopeless. The person sees both self and
   others as broken. Games from this position tend to be destructive and short;
   the worst are played in earnest.

## Scripts (developed after Games People Play)

Berne's later work developed the concept of **life scripts** — unconscious plans
for a life, based on decisions made in childhood, enacted over decades. Games are
the scenes of a script; scripts are the whole play. Games People Play focuses on
games, but the orchestrator should be aware of scripts as the larger framework
Berne eventually built around them.

## Autonomy (Berne's closing construct)

**Autonomy** — The state of being able to live without games. Constituted by three
qualities:
1. **Awareness** — perceiving the world directly in the present, unmediated by
   Parent programming or Child reaction
2. **Spontaneity** — freedom to respond from any ego state as the situation calls for
3. **Intimacy** — direct, game-free contact with another person

Berne treats autonomy as a direction, not a destination. No one is fully autonomous
all the time. The point is the movement toward it.

## Terms the orchestrator should define for modern readers

When generating chapter content, the orchestrator should define these terms in
plain language on first use, and should not assume the reader has any prior
exposure to Berne or to psychoanalytic theory:

- ego state
- transaction
- complementary / crossed / ulterior
- stroke
- pastime
- game (in Berne's technical sense)
- thesis / antithesis (of a game)
- payoff
- life position
- script (brief mention only)
- autonomy (awareness, spontaneity, intimacy)

## Terms to avoid or handle carefully

- **"Frigid"** (original name of one of the marital games) — dated and clinical,
  should be reframed as intimacy-avoidance
- **"Rapo"** (original name of a sexual game) — the name and 1964 framing are
  unusable; focus on the underlying pattern of invitation-followed-by-withdrawal
- **"Peasant"** (therapy game) — fine as a name but the cultural framing should
  be softened
- **"Stupid"** (therapy game) — fine as a name but should be handled with care
  since real intellectual limitations exist and should not be confused with the game

In all cases, the orchestrator should name the pattern clearly without reproducing
Berne's 1964 rhetorical choices.
