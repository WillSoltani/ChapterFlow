# Difficult Conversations — Modern Applications

Original analysis of how the framework applies to specific
modern contexts the book touches on but does not fully develop.
Written for the ChapterFlow pipeline. No verbatim text from the
book is reproduced here. All scenarios are invented fresh.

## 1. Remote and hybrid teams

The framework was designed in a pre-2010 professional world of
in-person meetings, hallway conversations, and 1:1s that
happened face to face. A distributed team in 2026 is a different
environment, and several of the framework's assumptions need
adjustment.

**The identity conversation gets louder over video.** Face-to-
face, the speaker has continuous embodied feedback — a warm
glance, a nod, a steadying hand on a shoulder — that stabilizes
the identity conversation in real time. On video, those cues
are compressed into a two-inch square or absent entirely. The
identity quake hits harder because the reassuring signals are
not reaching the speaker's nervous system the way they would in
person. A skilled operator compensates by being more explicit
about what in person would have been tacit: naming the care
out loud ("I'm raising this because I want us to keep working
well together"), pausing to check how the other person is
doing, and extending more explicit reassurance about what the
conversation is not about.

**The three-conversations diagnostic is harder to perform in
real time on video.** In person, the speaker can often read the
feelings conversation on the other person's face as it happens
— the micro-expressions, the shift in posture, the sudden
quiet. On video, those signals are attenuated. The skilled
operator has to ask more explicit questions more often: "How
is this landing for you?" "What's going on for you right now?"
These questions would feel intrusive in person; on video they
are necessary.

**Written async conversations lose the feelings layer almost
entirely.** See the next section for treatment.

**Scenario to generate:** A manager is giving a performance-
related piece of feedback to a direct report over Zoom. The
direct report's face registers something — shock, hurt, maybe
anger — and then the camera cuts out. The manager, in person,
would pause and wait. Over video, with the other person
literally not visible, the manager has to explicitly ask: "I
lost you for a second — are you still there? How is this
landing?" The skilled move is not to push on through the
silence as if it were agreement.

## 2. Asynchronous text: Slack, email, DMs

Text-based conversations are harder than speech for the
framework and specifically harder for Difficult Conversations
than for more technique-focused frameworks, because the
framework's strongest move — the stance of genuine curiosity —
is almost invisible in text.

**Harder, because the three conversations collapse into one.**
In text, the "what happened?" conversation is visible (the
words are on the screen) but the feelings and identity
conversations are almost entirely inside each person's head,
invisible to the other. The receiver supplies the tone; under
stress they supply the worst tone their brain can invent. The
sender, meanwhile, has no real-time feedback that the
receiver is reading something very different from what the
sender meant.

**Harder, because intent and impact diverge more in writing.**
A sentence the writer intended as a gentle check-in can read
as a pointed rebuke. A clarifying question can read as an
accusation. The writer's intent is locked at send time; the
reader's perception of impact is shaped by everything from
the day's prior stressors to the hour of the day to the
presence or absence of an emoji. The space between intent and
impact widens in writing, and the framework's
intent/impact-disentanglement move becomes both more
necessary and harder to perform.

**Harder, because the internal voice goes unchecked.** When
the listener is in the room with the speaker, the listener's
internal voice at least has to share attention with the
speaker's physical presence. When the listener is reading a
message at 11pm in bed, the internal voice has the whole
field to itself. The listener can construct elaborate stories
about what the writer meant, and nothing in the medium will
interrupt those stories.

**Easier, because text gives the speaker time.** The same
medium that invites reactivity also lets a careful sender
rewrite a message seven times before sending it. The
expression form — observation, internal response, story being
told — is easier to execute in text than in speech if the
sender uses the time. The sender can literally lay out each
part, check for the failure modes (overstatement,
understatement, walking-it-back), and send a cleaner version
than they could ever produce out loud.

**Easier, because the third story opener can be written and
checked.** The opener is the most important sentence in a
difficult conversation, and in text the writer can draft it
seven times before sending. The skilled text operator uses
this.

The skilled text operator does four specific things:

1. **Waits.** When the impulse is to reply immediately, wait
   an hour, or a day. Nearly every charged text reply is
   better for having sat.
2. **Names the medium.** "This feels like a conversation
   that would be easier on a call — do you have 15 minutes
   tomorrow?" is almost always the right move when a text
   thread is heating up.
3. **Over-checks intent.** In a charged exchange, explicitly
   stating intent before the content — "I'm writing this
   because I want us to figure out the project together, not
   because I'm trying to relitigate yesterday" — costs a
   sentence and prevents a full misread.
4. **Writes the third story, not the first.** In text, more
   than anywhere else, the speaker should describe the
   situation as a neutral observer would. The reader cannot
   hear the warmth that might have softened a first-person
   version in speech.

**Scenario to generate:** A manager writes "we need to talk
about yesterday's meeting" in a Slack DM at 10pm on a
Wednesday. The direct report reads it in bed, spirals through
the night, and walks into Thursday's conversation already
half-wounded. A third-story version of the same opening:
"Hey, no rush — when you have a minute tomorrow, I'd love to
compare notes on how yesterday's meeting went. I've been
thinking about a couple of things and I want to hear what
you're seeing before I commit to any view."

## 3. Performance reviews and feedback conversations

The framework has been extensively adapted for workplace
feedback, and the authors themselves extended it in
Thanks for the Feedback (Stone and Heen, 2014), which is
specifically about the receiving side of performance
conversations.

Three adaptations matter most:

- **The three conversations are always in play in a
  performance review.** The "what happened?" conversation is
  about the specific work product. The feelings conversation
  is almost always present (pride, hurt, resentment about
  perceived unfairness, gratitude for recognition) and is
  almost always suppressed by both parties in the name of
  professionalism. The identity conversation is the loudest:
  most professionals' sense of self is heavily bound up in
  their work, and a performance conversation is an identity
  conversation whether the parties name it or not.

- **The manager's identity conversation is usually stronger
  than they realize.** Managers delivering hard feedback are
  often quietly running their own identity conversation ("am
  I a good manager? am I being fair? am I being too harsh? am
  I being too soft?") and the internal static makes it
  harder for them to listen to the actual person in front of
  them.

- **"Intent vs. impact" is the central move.** Feedback
  almost always requires the giver to separate what they
  meant (the intended development signal) from what the
  receiver heard (the perceived judgment), and to take
  responsibility for both.

**Scenario to generate:** A principal engineer is giving a
year-end review to a senior engineer who has technically met
their goals but has been a source of friction with two
adjacent teams. The engineer is expecting a glowing review
because the metrics are good. The principal is considering
how to name the friction without torpedoing the relationship.
The novice move: lead with the good news, bury the friction
in a "one thing to work on" bullet. The skilled move: name
the three conversations out loud at the start — "I want to
talk about your technical work, which has been strong; I
also want to talk about how the work has been landing with
the other teams, which is harder; and I want us both to be
honest about how this conversation is going to feel."

## 4. Parenting, especially with teenagers

The framework translates surprisingly well to conversations
with adolescents, with two adjustments.

First, the identity conversation is louder and more fragile
in adolescence than at any other time in the human lifespan.
Teenagers are in the middle of a multi-year project of
figuring out who they are, and almost any parental comment
lands in the middle of the identity conversation whether the
parent intended it to or not. The parent who wants to raise
a concern about homework is, from the teenager's side,
raising a concern about who the teenager is. Grounding the
identity — the parent's, but also helping to ground the
teenager's — is the single most important move.

Second, the framework's "the feelings matter" point collides
with the adolescent default of "I'm fine." "I'm fine" in a
teenager is almost always masking a specific feeling the
teenager does not have the vocabulary or the safety to name.
The skilled parent learns that "I'm fine" is data, not
resolution, and that the correct response is not to demand
more content but to create more safety — to make the
conversation lower-stakes, less final, and easier to exit.

**Scenario to generate:** A parent is raising a concern with
a sixteen-year-old about a drop in grades. The novice opener:
"We need to talk about your grades." The skilled (third-
story) opener: "I saw the progress report. I'm not trying to
ground you or take your phone — I just want to understand
what's been happening, because something seems to have
shifted and I'm not sure what. What are you seeing from
inside?" The parent also has to be prepared to sit with "I
don't know" as a real answer, rather than pushing for the
explanation that would resolve their own worry.

## 5. Long-term partnerships and marriage

Difficult Conversations is used extensively in couples
therapy-adjacent contexts — not as a therapy itself, but as
reading material couples work through with or without a
counselor. The framework's three-conversations diagnostic is
particularly useful for partnerships because the recurring
fights in long-term relationships are almost always
mis-diagnosed arguments over the "what happened?" layer
when the real content is the feelings and identity layers.

Three specific patterns:

- **The recurring argument about chores, money, or time is
  almost never about chores, money, or time.** It is about
  one or both partners' feelings about being taken for
  granted, not seen, or not mattering enough. Trying to
  resolve the "what happened?" argument (whose turn it was to
  unload the dishwasher) will not work because the real
  argument is in the feelings layer.

- **The partner who "always wants to talk about things" and
  the partner who "always wants to drop it" are locked in a
  role assumption.** Both roles are contributions, not faults.
  The pattern persists because both roles are easier than the
  thing neither partner is doing, which is naming the pattern
  itself and asking what it is about.

- **Identity conversations are loudest when a partnership is
  changing.** New baby, new job, retirement, major loss — any
  life transition that shifts what each partner is to the
  other will open the identity conversation for both. Partners
  who try to handle the transition as a series of "what
  happened?" conversations without acknowledging the identity
  work underneath will find the practical conversations
  impossible.

**Scenario to generate:** A couple has been having the same
fight about weekend schedules for two years. Each time it
looks like an argument about whose turn it is to pick the
plans; each time it ends with both partners feeling hurt.
The skilled move is to stop having the schedules argument and
start having the feelings conversation it is actually about:
"I don't think we're really fighting about the schedule. I
think I'm feeling like my preferences don't count here, and
I'd like to know if you're feeling something similar from
your side."

## 6. Clinician-patient conversations

The framework has been adapted for healthcare communication,
and the authors' work (especially Thanks for the Feedback) has
been influential in medical training. A number of hospitals
and medical schools use derivatives of it for training on how
to deliver bad news, discuss goals of care, handle
treatment-adherence conversations, and manage conflict
between clinicians and families.

The adaptations that matter in this context:

- **The power asymmetry is extreme and usually invisible to
  the clinician.** A patient nodding and agreeing is usually
  silencing themselves, not consenting. The skilled clinician
  treats "okay, doctor" as data, not resolution, and actively
  checks for masking.

- **The feelings conversation is often the entire
  conversation.** A patient learning they have a serious
  diagnosis is not in a state to have a problem-solving
  conversation. The only useful move for the first ten
  minutes is acknowledgment of the feelings — naming the
  grief, the fear, the disorientation — and refusing to push
  into plan-of-care material until that has happened.

- **The identity conversation for the patient is enormous.**
  A diagnosis can be an identity quake: am I still the kind
  of person who lives independently? who works? who is a
  parent? The skilled clinician knows that the first
  consultation after a major diagnosis is not really about
  the disease; it is about what the disease means for the
  patient's self-image, and everything else has to wait.

**Scenario to generate:** A physician is having a goals-of-
care conversation with a patient whose cancer has stopped
responding to treatment. The novice move: present the
treatment options, ask the patient to choose. The skilled
move: spend the first ten minutes on the feelings and
identity conversations ("I know this is not the
conversation either of us wanted to be having. Before we
talk about what's next, tell me what's going on for you
right now.") and only move to the treatment options once
the patient is in a state to actually hear them.

## 7. Public-sphere conversations and online disagreement

The framework was not designed for public, many-to-many
conversations, and it does not fully translate to them.
Mutual good faith is hard to establish with strangers, the
performance dynamics of public conversation (where the real
audience is onlookers, not the interlocutor) reward exactly
the moves the framework classifies as failures, and the
three conversations are impossible to run in parallel when
the medium is 280 characters at a time.

Nonetheless, several pieces of the framework translate:

- **The purpose check still applies.** The question "what
  do I really want from posting this, and is this
  conversation the right way to serve it?" will often save
  the user from posting the thing they are about to post.
- **The identity conversation still applies.** Most public
  arguments are driven by the poster's identity conversation
  (am I the kind of person who lets this go unanswered? am I
  a coward if I don't respond?). Naming the identity
  conversation to oneself is the first move.
- **The learning stance still applies, but sparingly.**
  There are a small number of people in any public
  conversation who are actually there to learn. For those
  people, a real invitation to joint inquiry can work. For
  everyone else, it will be read as a trap.

**Teaching implication:** The ChapterFlow package should
include at least one scenario acknowledging that some
conversations are not worth having at all, and that the
skill of difficult conversations includes the skill of
recognizing when not to start one. The public sphere is the
clearest example.

## 8. AI-mediated communication

A newer application that the 2010 edition does not address
at all (predating widespread AI drafting tools) and that the
ChapterFlow package should acknowledge. When one or both
parties are drafting messages with AI assistance, several
things change:

- **The expression form becomes easier.** An AI-assisted
  drafter can lay out the observation, the internal response,
  and the story being told in separate paragraphs, edit each
  one, and send a message that would have been difficult to
  produce in one live pass. This is a real gain.
- **Intent and impact diverge further.** An angry first draft
  laundered by an AI into a polite, neutral-sounding message
  hides the sender's real state from the receiver. The
  receiver now has to interpret not only the words but the
  gap between the words and the sender's likely state.
- **The third-story opener can be workshopped.** The
  drafter can generate five different openers and pick the
  one that actually feels honest, rather than settling for
  the first one that comes to mind.
- **The "speaking from your own experience" form risks
  becoming generic.** AI drafting tends toward smoothed,
  conventionally-worded prose. The specificity that gives
  the expression form its power can get flattened into
  something that sounds like any other professional message.

**Teaching implication:** A short note in the closing
chapter acknowledging that AI-assisted drafting changes the
texture of text-based difficult conversations — for better
and for worse — and that the framework's underlying moves
(the three conversations, genuine curiosity, separation of
intent from impact) are if anything more important when the
surface of the message is being polished by a tool.

## 9. Cross-cultural conversations

See criticism-and-limits.md for the fuller treatment. In
short: the framework's implicit model is North American
professional-class directness, and it does not translate
cleanly to all cultural contexts. The skilled cross-cultural
practitioner keeps the framework's substantive moves (clarify
what you want, notice the three conversations, separate
intent from impact, ground the identity, listen from the
inside out) and adjusts the surface moves (how explicit to
be, how directly to name a feeling, how quickly to move to
problem-solving) to what the specific context can hold.

**Teaching implication:** At least one scenario in the
closing chapter should model this adjustment explicitly,
with a side-by-side of a move that reads as respectful in
one context and as rude in another. The package should
resist the temptation to treat directness as a universal
virtue.

## 10. Conversations that should not happen at all

A final modern application the book does not treat directly
but that the ChapterFlow package should surface: some
difficult conversations should not happen, and the skill of
recognizing which is which is itself part of the framework.

Conversations that should not happen include:

- **Conversations with an abuser** who will use the
  conversation as an opportunity for further harm.
- **Conversations whose goal is to change the other person's
  character** rather than to address a specific behavior or
  understanding.
- **Conversations the speaker is rehearsing as a way of
  avoiding the real conversation they need to have with
  themselves.**
- **Conversations that would cost the less-powerful party
  more than the conversation could possibly return.**
- **Conversations that the relationship has already outlived
  the capacity to repair,** where the honest move is an
  ending rather than a repair.

**Teaching implication:** The chapter on purpose should
include an explicit treatment of when the right move is not
to have the conversation at all. This is not the same as
avoidance, which is what the book's first chapters warn
against. It is the different skill of recognizing that some
conversations are not the tool the situation needs.
