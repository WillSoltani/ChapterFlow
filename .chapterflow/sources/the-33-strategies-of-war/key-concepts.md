# The 33 Strategies of War — Key Concepts

Glossary of the stable framework vocabulary used throughout the book.
All entries are original paraphrases written for the ChapterFlow
pipeline. No verbatim definitions from the book are reproduced here.

## The strategic frame of life

Greene's core premise — worth stating at the top because every
concept in the book presupposes it — is that life is strategic in a
stronger sense than most people admit. People pursue their own
interests; those interests frequently conflict; the conflicts are
resolved by skill, preparation, and positioning rather than by
fairness or good intentions; and the operator who treats the world
as strategic outperforms the operator who treats it as primarily
moral or emotional.

The framing is deliberately provocative. The teaching prose should
preserve the provocation (soft versions of the claim do not produce
the same strategic alertness) while being more explicit than Greene
is about the contexts in which strategic framing is not the right
frame.

## Grand Strategy

The long-horizon plan that gives meaning to short-horizon decisions.
An operator with grand strategy can lose individual battles
cheerfully because the loss is paid for by a larger gain elsewhere.
An operator without grand strategy fights every battle as if it
were the war, wins many of them, and finds that the wins do not add
up.

The test for whether an operator has grand strategy: ask them what
they are working toward on a five- or ten-year horizon, specifically
and concretely, and ask them how today's decisions serve it. If they
cannot answer, they are operating tactically.

## Self-Directed Warfare

The book's opening claim that the first opponent in any conflict is
the self. Before any external move is possible, the operator has to
manage their own emotional reactivity, their own tendency to fight
yesterday's war instead of today's, and their own drift toward
comfort. The four self-directed strategies (Polarity, Guerrilla-
War-of-the-Mind, Counterbalance, Death-Ground) are the foundations
on which every other strategy in the book rests.

## Presence of Mind

The capacity to access the broader picture, the longer time horizon,
and the strategic move even under emotional load. Under stress, the
mind narrows. The operator who is angry, frightened, or embarrassed
loses access to options they would have seen clearly an hour later.
Presence of mind is the discipline of not acting from the narrow
place.

The book treats this as ritual — something cultivated deliberately
through a specific personal practice (physical exercise, a delay
rule before decisions, a trusted second opinion, written analysis
before acting). The practice varies by operator; the discipline of
having one is universal.

## Death Ground

A position in which retreat is impossible and the operator must
either succeed or be destroyed. Greene's claim — drawn from Sun Tzu
and the classical military tradition — is that death-ground
conditions produce extraordinary performance because they remove
the low-effort options. The strategic move, when comfort is making
the operator slack, is to deliberately put themselves on death
ground (burn the boats, publicly commit to the deadline, constrain
the resources) so that the easy escapes are gone.

The reversal: death-ground tactics are dangerous when applied to an
operator who is already at the edge. The strategy is for the
comfortable, not the overwhelmed.

## Mission-Driven Command (Auftragstaktik)

Greene's term for the German military tradition in which subordinates
are given the purpose and the intent, not the method, and are
trusted to find the method themselves. The alternative is specified-
step command, in which every action is prescribed from above.

Mission-driven command produces adaptive teams and requires
competent subordinates. Specified-step command produces predictable
teams and is appropriate when the subordinates lack the context to
decide well. Most modern teams are badly over-specified.

## Perfect Economy of Force

The discipline of declining fights. Resources (time, attention,
energy, money, political capital) are finite, and the undisciplined
operator spends them on every fight that presents itself. The
disciplined operator declines most fights — even the ones they
would win — to preserve resources for the fights that matter most.

The test for every potential fight: what does winning get me, and
what does losing cost me? If both answers are "very little," the
fight is not worth having.

## Deterrence

The posture that makes attacking you costly enough that no rational
opponent attempts it. The cheapest victory is the one where the
opponent decides not to fight at all, and deterrence is the
capacity that produces that outcome.

Critical caveat: deterrence is not bluff. A reputation not backed
by actual capacity collapses the first time it is tested, and once
collapsed cannot be rebuilt. Deterrence requires that the threatened
response be real.

## The OODA Loop (Observe, Orient, Decide, Act)

Drawn from the work of Colonel John Boyd. The decision cycle every
operator runs through repeatedly: observe the situation, orient
your understanding of it, decide on a move, act. The operator
whose loop is faster than the opponent's loop wins, almost
regardless of other factors, because by the time the opponent acts
on their observation, the operator has already moved on to the next
decision and the opponent's action is targeted at a position that
no longer exists.

The implication is that operational tempo — the speed of the whole
decision cycle — is itself a strategic resource.

## Center of Gravity

The one target in any opponent's position that, if broken, brings
everything else down. Rarely the most obvious target. Could be a
supply line, a commander's confidence, a political ally, a funding
source, a reputational asset, or an assumption underneath the
opponent's whole strategy. The skilled operator identifies the
center of gravity through reconnaissance and goes for it, ignoring
the more visible targets that would be expensive to attack and
would not actually decide anything.

Identifying the center of gravity is the use of the Intelligence
Strategy; attacking it is the use of the Center-of-Gravity Strategy.
The two work together.

## The Turning Movement

The attack on the flank rather than the front. Every defense has a
strong front and a weak flank; attacking the front directly is
expensive, and finding the flank and attacking it instead is cheap.
The skill is the discipline to look for the flank rather than
charging the front, even when the front is the obvious target.

In non-military terms: the competitive move that refuses to
challenge the incumbent on the incumbent's strongest dimension and
instead finds the dimension on which the incumbent is structurally
weak.

## Fait Accompli

The cumulative strategy of small moves that individually fall
beneath the threshold at which the opponent would oppose them, and
collectively produce a change the opponent would never have
permitted as a single large move. By the time the opponent
recognizes what has been built, the situation is past the point at
which it can be reversed.

The strategy is patient, cumulative, and often invisible. It is
also the most frequent complaint of operators who lost a slow game
they did not know they were playing.

## Indirect Approach

Liddell Hart's phrase, adopted by Greene: the principle that the
route around the obstacle is usually faster than the route through
it. A consistent pattern across the book. The flank is cheaper than
the front. The misperception is more decisive than the bombardment.
The alliance that neutralizes the opponent is better than the
battle that defeats them. Readers who learn to look for the
indirect move first will find it most of the time.

## Moral High Ground

The posture of being seen, by the watching third parties, as the
just side of a conflict. In any dispute with an audience, the side
read as righteous has a structural advantage: opponents who attack
them look like aggressors, the audience sympathizes, and casualties
create political costs for the other side that the operator's own
casualties do not.

The posture requires substantive consistency. A single hypocrisy
detected by the audience collapses it instantly. Performative
righteousness without substantive backing is worse than none.

## Envelopment (Annihilation)

The strategy of complete encirclement — closing off every escape,
every ally, every fallback so that the opponent has nowhere to
retreat. The book treats this as the highest offensive art and also
the highest risk; envelopment commits the attacker's forces fully
and leaves no fallback if the encirclement fails.

Greene is explicit that envelopment is for moments of decisive
advantage, not for routine engagements. Operators who attempt
envelopment without the resources to complete it are exposed
catastrophically.

## The Exit Strategy

The discipline of knowing when and how to stop. The hardest moment
in any campaign is when continuing costs more than stopping but
the operator's sunk investment makes stopping feel impossible. The
skill is to articulate, before the campaign begins, what the
conditions for exit are, and to honor those conditions when they
arrive — even when every psychological pressure (sunk cost, public
commitment, personal identification) says "keep going."

## Misperception

The strategy of presenting the opponent with a picture of the
situation that is not the real one. The misleading elements are
embedded in enough true facts that they become invisible, and the
opponent's whole strategic picture is shifted without their
knowing.

The ethical line: shaping perception through selective framing of
real facts is legitimate strategic communication. Inventing false
facts is propaganda. The teaching prose should make the distinction
every time the strategy is discussed.

## The Strategy of the Void

The strategy of making yourself diffuse, indirect, and hard to
target. The opponent who cannot locate you cannot hit you.
Appropriate for weaker parties, guerrilla contexts, and operators
whose strength lies in staying underneath the threshold of the
opponent's attention.

## Blitzkrieg (Speed-as-Weapon)

Drawn from the German doctrine of fast combined-arms advance.
Greene's version is the principle that speed is itself a weapon —
that an operator who moves faster than the opponent can react
cycles through several decisions before the opponent finishes
processing the first one. The OODA loop is the underlying frame;
blitzkrieg is the operational expression.

## Alliance (True Interest Overlap)

The strategy of constructing arrangements in which your interests
and the interests of more powerful parties genuinely align, so
that advancing yourself also advances them. The legitimate version
requires finding real overlap; the morally ambiguous version
manufactures the appearance of overlap where none exists.

The book is clearer about the strategic content than about the
ethical distinction. The teaching prose should hold the line:
genuine alignment is legitimate strategic partnership; manufactured
alignment is manipulation.

## The Dirty Strategies (Part V) — Framing Note

Greene's final eleven strategies (Misperception, Ordinary-Extra-
ordinary, Righteous, Void, Alliance, One-Upmanship, Fait Accompli,
Communication, Inner-Front, Passive-Aggression, Chain-Reaction)
are explicitly the most morally ambiguous material in the book.
The book itself acknowledges this and proceeds without much
additional ethical framing.

The teaching prose should be more explicit. Each of these
strategies has a legitimate version (indirect strategy by parties
with genuinely opposed interests) and an illegitimate version
(manipulation or coercion of parties whose interests are not in
fact opposed). The chapter treatments should name the line every
time, and should not teach the strategies as if the line did not
exist.
