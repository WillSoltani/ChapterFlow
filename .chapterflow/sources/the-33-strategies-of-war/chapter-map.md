# The 33 Strategies of War — Chapter Map

This document maps Robert Greene's 33 strategies into a consolidated
10-chapter teaching sequence for the ChapterFlow package. Greene's
own structure has five parts and 33 strategies, which is far too many
for a teaching package. The map below groups related strategies
around their underlying principles so that the reader leaves with a
small number of high-leverage moves rather than a long list they will
not remember.

All chapter titles in this document are original paraphrases. Where
short phrases from the book's stable framework vocabulary reappear
(e.g., "death ground," "center of gravity"), they are used because
they are part of the widely-documented military and strategic
vocabulary Greene himself is paraphrasing from older sources.

## Original book structure (2006 first edition)

**Part I — Self-Directed Warfare**
  1. The Polarity Strategy — Declare War on Your Enemies
  2. The Guerrilla-War-of-the-Mind Strategy — Do Not Fight the Last War
  3. The Counterbalance Strategy — Do Not Lose Your Presence of Mind
  4. The Death-Ground Strategy — Create a Sense of Urgency and Desperation

**Part II — Organizational (Team) Warfare**
  5. The Command-and-Control Strategy — Avoid the Snares of Groupthink
  6. The Controlled-Chaos Strategy — Segment Your Forces
  7. Morale Strategies — Transform Your War into a Crusade

**Part III — Defensive Warfare**
  8. The Perfect-Economy Strategy — Pick Your Battles
  9. The Counterattack Strategy — Turn the Tables
  10. Deterrence Strategies — Create a Threatening Presence
  11. The Nonengagement Strategy — Trade Space for Time

**Part IV — Offensive Warfare**
  12. Grand Strategy — Lose Battles but Win the War
  13. The Intelligence Strategy — Know Your Enemy
  14. The Blitzkrieg Strategy — Overwhelm Resistance with Speed
  15. Forcing Strategies — Control the Dynamic
  16. The Center-of-Gravity Strategy — Hit Them Where It Hurts
  17. The Divide-and-Conquer Strategy — Defeat Them in Detail
  18. The Turning Strategy — Attack the Soft Flank
  19. The Annihilation Strategy — Envelop the Enemy
  20. The Ripening-for-the-Sickle Strategy — Maneuver Them into Weakness
  21. The Diplomatic-War Strategy — Negotiate While Advancing
  22. The Exit Strategy — Know How to End Things

**Part V — Unconventional (Dirty) Warfare**
  23. Misperception Strategies — Blend Fact and Fiction
  24. The Ordinary-Extraordinary Strategy — Take the Line of Least Expectation
  25. The Righteous Strategy — Occupy the Moral High Ground
  26. The Strategy of the Void — Deny Them Targets
  27. The Alliance Strategy — Seem to Work for Others' Interests
  28. The One-Upmanship Strategy — Give Rivals Enough Rope
  29. The Fait Accompli Strategy — Take Small Bites
  30. Communication Strategies — Penetrate Their Minds
  31. The Inner-Front Strategy — Destroy from Within
  32. The Passive-Aggression Strategy — Dominate While Seeming to Submit
  33. The Chain-Reaction Strategy — Sow Uncertainty and Panic

> exact subtitle phrasing may differ slightly from the physical 2006

## Recommended ChapterFlow teaching structure (10 chapters)

The consolidation is driven by the observation that most of Greene's
33 strategies are variations on a smaller set of underlying moves.
The teaching sequence below groups related strategies so that each
chapter teaches one underlying move with several facets, rather than
asking the reader to distinguish between, for example, the Turning
Strategy and the Divide-and-Conquer Strategy as if they were separate
lessons.

### Chapter 1 — The First Enemy Is You (Self-Directed Warfare)
**Covers:** Strategies 1–4 (Polarity, Guerrilla-War-of-the-Mind,
Counterbalance, Death-Ground).

**Teaching focus:** The foundational claim of the book — that the
first opponent in any conflict is the self. The reader should leave
the chapter with four specific self-directed moves: name the real
opposition honestly, abandon the strategy that previously worked,
develop a practice for presence of mind under pressure, and use
constraint deliberately to convert comfort into performance.

**Modern examples to generate:**
- A mid-career professional who refuses to acknowledge which
  colleagues are structurally positioned against her advancement.
- A founder whose first-company playbook no longer fits the current
  market.
- A negotiator who notices the narrowing of her own thinking under
  pressure and has a specific practice for widening it back out.
- A writer who publicly commits to a deadline to force finish.

**Avoid:** Framing self-directed warfare as self-help. The register
is strategic, not therapeutic.

### Chapter 2 — Leading Through Other People (Organizational Warfare)
**Covers:** Strategies 5–7 (Command-and-Control, Controlled-Chaos,
Morale).

**Teaching focus:** Most of what the reader will accomplish in any
large endeavor will be accomplished through other people. The chapter
teaches three moves: give mission rather than method, segment forces
for adaptability, and attach the work to a cause larger than itself.

**Modern examples to generate:**
- A team lead who specifies purpose instead of steps and gets a more
  adaptive team than the micromanaging peer.
- A startup that organizes around small autonomous product units
  rather than one large monolithic org.
- A leader whose team would walk through walls because the work is
  framed as a crusade, and the leader visibly believes it.

**Avoid:** Treating morale as motivation technique. The book's
insistence that a manufactured crusade is detected and backfires
should be preserved.

### Chapter 3 — Choosing Not to Fight (Defense, Part 1)
**Covers:** Strategies 8 and 11 (Perfect-Economy, Nonengagement).

**Teaching focus:** Resources are finite, and most battles are not
worth fighting. The chapter teaches the discipline of declining
fights and, when stronger opponents appear, of trading ground for
time. This is the chapter where the book's defensive wisdom lives
most concentrated.

**Modern examples to generate:**
- A manager who lets most office-political battles slide and is
  fresh for the one that matters.
- A startup that withdraws from a market segment when a giant
  enters, rather than fighting on the giant's ground.
- A professional who declines a high-status fight she would lose
  and uses the saved energy to build a better position.

**Avoid:** Making this chapter feel like advice to be passive. The
discipline is of selective engagement, not conflict avoidance.

### Chapter 4 — Absorbing and Returning an Attack (Defense, Part 2)
**Covers:** Strategies 9 and 10 (Counterattack, Deterrence).

**Teaching focus:** Two related defensive moves. Counterattack is
about the discipline of absorbing an opening attack without panic
and moving on the opening it creates. Deterrence is about cultivating
a posture that makes attacking you costly enough that opponents
decline the attempt.

**Modern examples to generate:**
- An executive who lets a rival's accusation play out fully, then
  responds with overwhelming evidence at the moment the rival has
  overcommitted.
- A company whose competitive moats are visible enough that no rival
  bothers attacking that segment.
- A professional whose reputation precedes them into rooms they have
  not entered.

**Avoid:** Framing deterrence as bluff. The book is clear that a
reputation not backed by actual capacity collapses the first time
it is tested.

### Chapter 5 — The Long View (Grand Strategy and Intelligence)
**Covers:** Strategies 12 and 13 (Grand Strategy, Intelligence).

**Teaching focus:** Two foundational offensive principles. Grand
strategy is the long-horizon plan that gives meaning to
short-horizon decisions. Intelligence is the investment in actually
understanding the opponent before acting. Together they are the
prerequisites for every offensive move in the book.

**Modern examples to generate:**
- A career built around a coherent multi-decade trajectory vs. a
  career built around taking the best-looking opportunity each year.
- A negotiator who spends a week researching the counterparty's
  actual incentives before the meeting and knows things the
  counterparty does not know they have revealed.
- A founder who revises the grand plan when the market reveals
  something new.

**Avoid:** Presenting grand strategy as rigid long-term planning.
The skill is the combination of a clear long horizon and the
willingness to revise.

### Chapter 6 — Speed, Initiative, and the Decisive Point (Offensive, Part 1)
**Covers:** Strategies 14, 15, and 16 (Blitzkrieg, Forcing,
Center-of-Gravity).

**Teaching focus:** The three offensive moves that decide fast
campaigns. Speed (move faster than the opponent can react), initiative
(set the terms of the engagement), and the decisive point (hit the
one target whose loss brings everything else down).

**Modern examples to generate:**
- A startup whose decision speed is a structural advantage over the
  incumbent.
- A negotiator who controls the agenda, the sequence, and the pace
  of a meeting.
- A competitor who attacks not the rival's strongest product but
  the assumption underneath the rival's whole strategy.

**Avoid:** Turning speed into a virtue of its own. The chapter should
emphasize that speed wasted on bad decisions is worse than no speed
at all.

### Chapter 7 — Indirection: The Flank, the Wedge, and the Long Maneuver (Offensive, Part 2)
**Covers:** Strategies 17, 18, and 20 (Divide-and-Conquer, Turning,
Ripening-for-the-Sickle). (Strategy 19, Annihilation, is reserved for
Chapter 10 because its high-risk character makes it a different
register.)

**Teaching focus:** The cluster of offensive strategies that favor
indirection over direct confrontation. Split the opponent by driving
wedges into their coalitions; attack the soft flank rather than the
strong front; maneuver the opponent into a position from which they
collapse on their own.

**Modern examples to generate:**
- A lobbyist who finds the issue on which two members of a voting
  coalition disagree and uses it to peel one of them off.
- A competitor who refuses to challenge the incumbent on the
  incumbent's strongest dimension and instead finds the dimension
  on which the incumbent is structurally weak.
- A negotiator who structures the early phases of a deal so that
  the counterparty's only remaining option in the final phase is
  to accept terms they would have refused at the start.

**Avoid:** Any framing that suggests indirection is manipulation
by default. The book is clear that these are strategic moves that
assume legitimate opposed interests.

### Chapter 8 — Negotiated Outcomes and Clean Exits (Offensive, Part 3)
**Covers:** Strategies 21 and 22 (Diplomatic-War, Exit).

**Teaching focus:** Two underappreciated offensive skills. The first
is to pair pressure with a sincere negotiating channel, so that the
opponent always has a face-saving path to settlement. The second is
to know when and how to stop — to honor pre-committed exit
conditions even when every psychological pressure says "keep going."

**Modern examples to generate:**
- An executive who fires the legal opening move while privately
  offering a face-saving exit to the counterparty.
- A founder who shuts down the failing product line at the right
  moment vs. the founder who keeps it alive for two more years.
- A professional who leaves the job at the right time vs. the
  professional who stays one year too long and leaves on bad terms.

**Avoid:** Treating the exit strategy as quitting. The frame is of
a disciplined, pre-committed decision that serves the larger plan.

### Chapter 9 — The Line of Least Expectation (Unconventional, Part 1)
**Covers:** Strategies 23, 24, 26, 27, 29 (Misperception,
Ordinary-Extraordinary, Void, Alliance, Fait Accompli). This
consolidates five of the Part V strategies into one chapter about
asymmetric and indirect moves that avoid direct engagement altogether.

**Teaching focus:** The operator who is weaker, or who simply has
better things to do than direct confrontation, has a suite of
indirect moves available. Shape perception through selective framing
of real facts. Vary your own texture so opponents cannot predict
you. Make yourself diffuse and hard to target. Find alignments of
interest with more powerful parties. Take small bites that aggregate
past the point at which the opponent could have resisted.

**Modern examples to generate:**
- A negotiator who refuses to play the role the counterparty has
  cast them in.
- A professional who finds the senior person whose ambitions are
  served by her own success and quietly aligns with them.
- A bureaucrat who shifts policy through a sequence of small
  changes, each beneath the threshold at which opposition would
  mobilize.

**Avoid:** Sliding into the register of manipulation. The ethical
line should be explicit in this chapter: shaping perception with
real facts is legitimate framing; inventing false facts is
propaganda, and the chapter should name the difference.

### Chapter 10 — The Edge Cases and When to Put the Framework Down (Unconventional, Part 2, and Closing)
**Covers:** Strategies 19, 25, 28, 30, 31, 32, 33 (Annihilation,
Righteous, One-Upmanship, Communication, Inner-Front,
Passive-Aggression, Chain-Reaction), consolidated into a closing
chapter that addresses the strategies with the highest cost, the
highest ethical risk, and the greatest need for explicit framing.

**Teaching focus:** The high-risk, high-cost, ethically ambiguous
edges of the framework, plus the meta-skill of knowing when strategic
framing is itself the wrong frame. The reader should leave the
chapter with three things: an understanding of what the dirty
strategies actually do and when they apply; a clear sense of the
ethical limits the book itself underplays; and an explicit list of
situations in which the framework should be put down (intimacy,
grief, genuine collaboration, contexts where the other side is not
in fact an opponent).

**Modern examples to generate:**
- A legal strategy that systematically closes off every avenue the
  counterparty might use — with the explicit note about the
  conditions under which this is legitimate.
- An executive who refuses to fight the rival who is on a collision
  course with the board, and is the natural successor.
- A weaker operator who builds parallel structures under a more
  powerful one — with the ethical framing about the line between
  legitimate positioning and subversion.
- A situation in which the reader should stop using the framework
  entirely because the counterparty is not in fact an opponent.

**Avoid:** Either romanticizing or flatly condemning the dirty
strategies. The register is strategic honesty — naming what the
moves do, when they apply, and where the ethical limits lie.

## Skeleton dossier notes

For each chapter, the orchestrator's skeleton artifacts should
include:

- **Core principle** — one sentence distilling the underlying move
  (not the individual strategies).
- **Three examples** — at least one mundane workplace scenario, at
  least one higher-stakes scenario, and at least one scenario in
  which the reader must judge whether to use the move at all.
- **Tone object** — direct and unsentimental, matching the book's
  register while softening the occasional admiration for ruthlessness.
- **Three-depth variants (EMH)**:
  - Easy — what the move is, with a clean modern scenario.
  - Medium — how to spot the moment where it applies (and where
    it doesn't), with a scenario requiring judgment.
  - Hard — the ethical edge and the "do not use this here" case.
- **Recognition test** — a short scenario the reader can diagnose:
  which strategy is being used, and is it being used well?
- **Transition** — how the chapter sets up the next one, explicit
  on which previous moves are being composed.

## Variant family

`variantFamily: "EMH"` (Easy / Medium / Hard) matches the
`flagship_v4_compatible` output profile.

## Chapter range

Target: **10 chapters** in the release package. The orchestrator
may adjust by ±1 if a natural break suggests otherwise, but should
not exceed 11. Resist the temptation to expand to accommodate all
33 strategies as separate chapters; the consolidation is
deliberate.
