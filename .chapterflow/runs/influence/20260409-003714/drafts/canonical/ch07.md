A decision can keep talking long after the moment of decision is over. Once a person has said yes, taken a side, signed up, or described themselves in a certain way, the earlier signal can start shaping what later choices feel permissible. Commitment leaves an echo, and the echo can sound like obligation.

That is the chapter's first serious claim. Commitment and consistency work because people do not like feeling scattered across time. A prior choice can become more than history. It can become a reference point that later behavior feels pressed to honor. The old signal begins carrying forward into the next decision.

The force grows when the commitment becomes visible. A private preference can matter, but a public stand is harder to revise because reversal now threatens more than convenience. It can threaten how a person appears to others and how coherent they appear to themselves. The earlier statement starts demanding loyalty simply because it was made.

That is where the chapter becomes sharper than a generic warning about stubbornness. The pressure is not only social embarrassment. It is also self-story maintenance. Once a person begins seeing a position, role, or promise as part of who they are, changing course can feel like self-betrayal even when the evidence has changed. Consistency starts protecting identity before it protects truth.

The chapter stays credible because it does not mock consistency outright. Reliable people need to follow through. Commitments can help groups coordinate, help individuals persist, and keep every new mood from rewriting a serious promise. The point is not that steadiness is fake. The point is that steadiness can become automatic enough to stop asking whether the original commitment still deserves obedience.

That is why the pressure point is so easy to miss. A small earlier yes can quietly tax later resistance. A plan announced aloud, a role accepted in public, a preference written down, or a self-description repeated often enough can all make revision feel more expensive than reevaluation. The person starts defending continuity before they have rechecked the current facts.

The hardest tension of the chapter follows from that. Commitment can be a form of integrity, but it can also become outsourced judgment wearing the costume of integrity. The defense is not to become flaky or anti-promissory. It is to ask whether the earlier signal still fits the present evidence. If the answer is thin, then consistency is no longer serving truth. It is merely protecting an older version of the self from being revised.
