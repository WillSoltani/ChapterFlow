The professor mailed Christmas cards to strangers, and many of them mailed cards back. That is a ridiculous result if you think people first ask whether the relationship is real, whether the gift was deserved, or whether repayment makes any sense. It becomes much less ridiculous once you see the chapter's point. Repayment pressure can arrive before those questions finish forming.

Reciprocation works because people are trained, socially and morally, to answer generosity with generosity. The rule is useful enough that ordinary life would get colder without it. Cooperation would be harder, favors would feel riskier, and shared life would lose one of its most basic stabilizers. That is why the chapter does not treat reciprocity as a flaw in human nature. It treats it as a strength that can be turned.

The turn begins with the idea of uninvited debt. You do not have to ask for the first favor to feel the second pressure. A free sample, a small gift, a thoughtful gesture, or a concession in a negotiation can all create the same internal shift: now something is owed. That is what makes the chapter more unsettling than a simple argument about gratitude. Gratitude is warm. Obligation is heavier. Once a person feels they have received, they often stop asking whether the giving was clean in the first place.

That is why what comes free can become expensive. The chapter's warning about unequal exchange is not abstract. A small initial favor can produce a much larger return because the repayment is being measured against the discomfort of indebtedness, not against the objective size of the gift. The imbalance is the point. If the first move is cheap for the persuader and psychologically costly for the recipient to ignore, then the trade is already tilted.

The rejection-then-retreat pattern shows how powerful that tilt becomes once concession enters the picture. The persuader opens with a request large enough to be refused, then retreats to the smaller request they wanted all along. The target feels two pressures at once. The smaller request now looks more reasonable by contrast, and the persuader's retreat feels like a concession that should be answered with one of their own. The yes no longer feels like surrender. It can feel like fairness.

That is why the Watergate example matters in this chapter. The large early plans make the later plan look sane partly because the earlier asks absorb the shock. The discussion is no longer being judged against a neutral baseline. It is being judged against what just seemed excessive. Reciprocation does not only work through gifts. It also works through softened demands. A concession can function like a gift when the other side starts feeling obliged to meet it halfway.

The chapter's hardest point is that healthy reciprocity and manipulated obligation can look similar from the outside. Real cooperation often does involve giving, returning, adjusting, and sharing. You cannot defend yourself by treating every gift as a trap. But you also cannot stay clear-eyed if you refuse to ask when generosity is being used to install debt. The defense Cialdini offers is narrower and tougher than cynicism. Notice the pressure. Ask whether the first move was genuine or strategic. Then decide whether you owe gratitude, proportion, or nothing at all.
