Core Claim:
This chapter argues that people often value things more when those things feel limited, closing, or harder to access. The chapter's narrow point is that scarcity can intensify desire and compress judgment by making threatened loss feel urgent.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
This chapter is about fewness-backed pressure rather than authority-backed pressure. It is impossible to confuse with authority because the force does not come from who seems entitled to lead. It comes from what seems in danger of becoming unavailable.

Author Logic Chain:
First the author shows that limited availability changes how strongly people want something.
Then the author shows that closing access and fewness can make people react faster and think more narrowly.
Then the author shows why the defense must separate actual value from the pressure of possible loss.

Required Anchors:
- limited-availability or closing-access opening example

- loss pressure narrowing judgment
- defense move that checks whether scarcity changed value or only changed urgency

Frameworks or Terms Introduced:
- scarcity: increased persuasive force produced by limited availability
- fewness: the signal that access is constrained or closing
- loss pressure: urgency produced by the threat of missing out

Specific Applications:
- limited seats or timed opportunity cues in work decisions
- school opportunities that feel more attractive once spots seem few
- personal decisions where closing access changes desire

Common Misreadings:
- scarce always means valuable
- urgency always means the choice is good
- the right defense is to ignore every limited option

Counterarguments or Limits:
Scarcity can matter for legitimate reasons because limited opportunities are sometimes genuinely more consequential. The problem is not limited availability itself. The problem is letting threatened loss rewrite value faster than evidence can be checked.

Previous Chapter Bridge:
Chapter 5 showed how rank and expertise can stand in for proof. Chapter 6 moves the pressure into availability, where limited access can make a choice feel stronger without improving its substance.

Next Chapter Bridge:
Move from scarcity into commitment and consistency, where persuasion comes less from fewness than from the force of prior choices and the need to stay aligned with them.

Moral Complexity:
Required framing is disciplined evaluation rather than anti-desire rhetoric. The prose should preserve the fact that limited opportunities can matter while showing how quickly fewness can narrow judgment and inflate want.
