Core Claim:
This chapter argues that people often decide what is true, safe, or appropriate by looking at what others appear to be doing. Social proof becomes especially powerful under uncertainty, when many others seem aligned, and when those others feel similar to the observer. The chapter's deeper warning is that copied certainty can be manufactured.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
This chapter is about borrowed consensus rather than warmth or debt. It is impossible to confuse with liking because the force does not come from affection toward one person. It comes from using visible group behavior as evidence.

Author Logic Chain:
First the author shows that popularity itself can operate as persuasion.
Then the author shows that uncertainty, mass behavior, and similarity intensify imitation.
Then the author shows how social proof can be staged, misread, or weaponized, which makes defense necessary.

Frameworks or Terms Introduced:
- social proof: treating others' behavior as evidence of what is correct
- uncertainty: condition that makes people look outward for guidance
- pluralistic ignorance: group inaction or mistaken belief maintained because everyone is reading everyone else
- similarity / peer-suasion: copying others who seem like oneself
- engineered consensus: staged popularity or imitation cues used to create compliance

Specific Applications:
- bestseller / most-popular / fastest-growing claims
- emergency response and bystander hesitation
- online or campus adoption cascades driven by visible participation

Common Misreadings:
- the crowd is always wrong
- popularity always means fraud
- social proof only works on obviously insecure people

Counterarguments or Limits:
Social proof is often useful because others really do know something you do not. The problem is that the signal can be inaccurate, staged, or drawn from the wrong comparison group.
