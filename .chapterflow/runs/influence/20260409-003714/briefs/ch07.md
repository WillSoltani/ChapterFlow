Core Claim:
This chapter argues that people often feel pressure to keep later behavior aligned with earlier choices, statements, or visible identities. The chapter's narrow point is that commitment can stabilize action but can also lock judgment to yesterday's signal after today's evidence has changed.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
This chapter is about self-alignment pressure rather than scarcity pressure. It is impossible to confuse with scarcity because the force does not come from what is disappearing. It comes from what the person already said, chose, or publicly signaled.

Author Logic Chain:
First the author shows that prior choices create momentum for later consistency.
Then the author shows that public or identity-linked commitments can harden into lock-in.
Then the author shows why the defense must test whether consistency is serving truth or only protecting a past signal.

Frameworks or Terms Introduced:

- consistency pressure: the pull to stay aligned with what was already said or done
- public stand: a visible signal that raises the cost of reversal

Specific Applications:
- work decisions driven by earlier commitments or announced plans
- school settings where public role choices become hard to revise
- personal situations where identity language keeps a bad choice alive

Common Misreadings:
- consistency is always integrity
- changing your mind always means weakness
- the right defense is to distrust every commitment

Counterarguments or Limits:
Consistency can matter for legitimate reasons because stable commitments make cooperation and follow-through possible. The problem is not staying aligned by itself. The problem is letting an earlier signal outrun the evidence now available.
