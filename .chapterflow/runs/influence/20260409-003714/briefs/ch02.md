Core Claim:
This chapter argues that people feel strong pressure to repay what they have been given, even when the first move was small, unwanted, or strategic. Reciprocation works because obligation arrives faster than full evaluation of motive. That makes favors, gifts, concessions, and samples unusually powerful influence tools. The chapter's deeper warning is that what looks free can quietly install a debt.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
This is the book's first concrete lever chapter. It is impossible to confuse with the overview chapter because it is built around indebtedness, repayment pressure, uninvited debt, unequal exchange, and the door-in-the-face concession pattern.

Author Logic Chain:
First the author shows that people repay gifts and favors with surprising reliability.
Then the author shows how that obligation can be engineered through samples, favors, and concessions.
Then the author shows why reciprocation is hard to resist even when the first move was manipulative.

Frameworks or Terms Introduced:
- reciprocation: pressure to repay what has been given
- uninvited debt: obligation triggered by favors or gifts not requested
- unequal exchange: return payment exceeds the original favor
- reciprocal concessions: one side gives ground, creating pressure for the other side to do the same
- rejection-then-retreat / door-in-the-face: large initial request makes smaller target request easier to accept

Specific Applications:
- free samples and trial offers
- political and institutional favor-trading
- negotiation requests designed to extract a smaller yes after a larger no

Common Misreadings:
- reciprocation works only when you like the other person
- a free gift is harmless unless it has obvious strings attached
- saying no once ends the pressure

Counterarguments or Limits:
Reciprocation is socially useful in real cooperative life. The problem is not exchange itself. The problem is when obligation outruns genuine consent or proportionality.
