Core Claim:
This chapter argues that agreement becomes easier when the asker is liked. That liking can come from real friendship, but it can also be manufactured through attractiveness, similarity, praise, repeated contact, cooperation, and positive association. The chapter's deeper warning is that affection can blur the boundary between the person and the deal.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
This chapter is about affinity rather than debt. It is impossible to confuse with reciprocation because the pressure does not mainly come from owing something back. It comes from warmth, resemblance, admiration, and emotional spillover.

Author Logic Chain:
First the author shows that people are more compliant with those they like.
Then the author shows how liking can be built strategically through attractiveness, similarity, flattery, contact, cooperation, and association.
Then the author shows why the right defense is to separate the person from the merits of the request.

- halo effect: one favorable trait coloring broader judgment
- similarity: common traits or mirrored cues increasing rapport
- compliments / flattery: praise creating openness and lowered resistance
- contact and cooperation: repeated exposure and shared effort building warmth
- association: positive or negative feelings transferring through linkage

Specific Applications:

- celebrity or prestige association in products and campaigns

Common Misreadings:
- liking only matters in close friendships
- a warm person is automatically trustworthy
- this chapter is just generic networking advice

Counterarguments or Limits:
Liking is not fake by definition. Warmth and cooperation can be real social goods. The problem is that feeling good about a person can silently take over judgment about the proposal itself.
