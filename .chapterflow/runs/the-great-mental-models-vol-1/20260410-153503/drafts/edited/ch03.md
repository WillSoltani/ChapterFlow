Some ideas feel strong because nothing can touch them. Every bad result gets reinterpreted as support. Every exception becomes part of the story. Every failed prediction gets explained away after the fact. Karl Popper's point is that this kind of invincibility is often a warning sign. A claim that survives because nothing could count against it has not shown unusual strength. It has slipped away from the conditions that would let you learn whether it is weak.

That is why falsifiability matters. A serious claim does not only gather support. It exposes itself to the risk of being shown wrong. Popper's line is severe because the standard is severe: if no conceivable event could refute the theory, then the theory is not doing the kind of work science is supposed to do. The claim may still feel elegant or complete. But once every outcome fits, it stops telling you which realities it cannot survive.

The chapter then makes confirmation look cheaper than people want it to look. Confirmations are easy to find when you already want the theory to win. You notice the supporting case, forget the awkward case, and reinterpret the mixed case. A stronger theory asks more from itself. It makes risky predictions. It says in advance what would count for it and what would count against it. The value comes from the danger it is willing to face.

That is why risky prediction matters more than retrospective neatness. A theory that forbids very little cannot lose very much. A theory that forbids more is taking a genuine chance on the shape of reality. After the result is known, clever people can almost always tell a story that makes the theory look smarter than it was. The useful question is not whether the explanation sounds smooth afterward. The useful question is whether the claim exposed itself before the outcome arrived.

Peter Cathcart Wason brings the model down from philosophy into habit. People naturally search for confirming evidence instead of designing real disproof. That bias makes weak theories sticky because the mind keeps feeding them support. A person can feel careful and rigorous while doing little more than arranging fresh applause for an idea they never truly risked.

That pressure appears far beyond science. A manager says a new sales script works, but defines success so loosely that any result can be called evidence. A student writes a thesis so vaguely that no finding could really count against it. A person says they are a great judge of character, then quietly forgets every confident miss. In each case the problem is not lack of explanation. It is lack of exposure.

The discipline here is uncomfortable because it changes what you protect. Instead of protecting the belief, you protect the test. You decide in advance what evidence would make you stop speaking as if the claim still deserves confidence. That standard is harsher, but it is also what keeps a theory from buying endless safety through reinterpretation.

The chapter does not claim that every important truth can be reduced to one easy break test. The frozen sources support something narrower and more practical. If you want an idea to earn trust, make it vulnerable to reality. That is what separates a theory that learns from a theory that only survives by changing costume.

Once you adopt that discipline, the next question gets sharper. If a belief should be tested hard, what remains when you strip away decoration and rebuild from what cannot be argued away? That is the opening move into first principles thinking.
