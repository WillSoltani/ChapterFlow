# Chapter 6: Necessity and Sufficiency

Legal approval is late, so a product launch stalls. Everyone can see that the approval matters. Then the reasoning starts to drift. One person talks as if the approval alone would make the launch ready. Another talks as if receiving it would prove the broader plan was sound. A required condition has quietly been mistaken for a guarantee.

That is the confusion this model fixes. A necessary condition is something an outcome cannot happen without. A sufficient condition is something that is enough to produce the outcome. One is required. The other completes the result. Sometimes a factor can be both, but not by default. Treating them as interchangeable leads people to overstate causes, overtrust plans, and misread failures.

The practical test is straightforward. Remove a necessary condition and the outcome cannot happen. Add a sufficient condition under the relevant setup and the outcome occurs. The first question asks what the result depends on. The second asks what would actually bring it about. People often answer one and assume they answered both.

That distinction travels well. A hiring manager may start treating one credential as if it guarantees performance. A student may assume one study habit is enough for a top grade. A team may behave as if a required approval is the whole launch plan. The visible factor is real, but it is carrying more explanatory weight than it earned.

The model matters because it improves diagnosis. When a result appears, ask whether the factor was required, enough, both, or neither. When a result fails, ask whether a required condition was missing or whether the favored ingredient was never enough in the first place. The distinction keeps one ingredient from impersonating the whole causal story.

It also blocks a second error. A condition can be sufficient in one setup without being the only possible path. A tactic may work, yet other tactics could also work. If you confuse enough with only, you turn one successful route into an exclusive explanation and start planning too narrowly.

Nothing complicated is required here. The chapter stays practical on purpose. It is a tool for cleaner explanation: separate prerequisites from guarantees, separate one working route from all working routes, and ask better questions before you commit to a diagnosis.

That sets up the next model cleanly. Once the conditions are sorted with more precision, the next task is to follow consequences beyond the first visible effect.
