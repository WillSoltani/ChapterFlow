# Chapter 9: Causation vs. Correlation

Two lines rise together on the dashboard, and the room immediately starts explaining. One metric must be causing the other. The story feels natural because the pattern is visible, repeated, and easy to point at. That is exactly when caution matters most. Two things moving together is not yet proof that one is producing the other.

That is the chapter's core distinction. Correlation means co-movement. Causation means real producing influence. The gap matters because a pattern can be genuine without being explanatory. The pair may share a driver. The timing may mislead. The visible relationship may be real while the first story about it is still wrong.

The model does not ask you to ignore patterns. It asks you to slow down the explanation reflex. Patterns are worth noticing because they often point to something important. But noticing is only the beginning. The next question is whether there is a mechanism that justifies the causal story being told.

That makes the chapter practical. If two business metrics rise together, ask whether one is truly driving the other or whether both are reacting to something else. If a study habit appears near a good result, ask whether it caused the result or merely traveled beside the broader setup that produced it. If a personal routine lines up with a better mood, ask whether the routine is the driver, one contributor, or just nearby in time.

The temptation is always the same: a vivid pattern arrives, and the mind rushes to finish the story. The chapter matters because it interrupts that rush. It keeps useful pattern recognition from turning into lazy explanation.

The limit stays narrow on purpose. This is not a technical methods lecture. It is a practical warning. See the pattern. Respect the pattern. Then ask whether the mechanism is real before talking as if causation has been earned.

That clears the path for inversion. Once you become more careful about causal stories, it becomes easier to attack a problem backward by asking what reliably produces the failure you want to avoid.
