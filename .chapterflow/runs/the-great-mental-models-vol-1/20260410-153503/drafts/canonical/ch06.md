# Chapter 6: Necessity and Sufficiency

A product team delays a launch because legal approval has not arrived yet. Everyone in the room agrees that the approval matters. Then the discussion slips. One person starts talking as if approval alone would make the launch ready. Another person talks as if getting approval would prove the plan was sound. The mistake is subtle but expensive. A required condition has quietly been treated like a guarantee.

That confusion is what this model corrects. A necessary condition is something an outcome cannot happen without. A sufficient condition is something that, if present, is enough to produce the outcome. One is required. The other is enough. Those can overlap, but they are not the same by default. Missing that difference distorts diagnosis, planning, and explanation.

The easiest way to feel the distinction is to keep the question concrete. If a factor is necessary, remove it and the outcome fails. If a factor is sufficient, add it under the relevant conditions and the outcome occurs. The first question is about dependence. The second is about completion. People often answer one while pretending they answered the other.

That is why the model matters outside formal logic language. In ordinary life, we often over-credit one ingredient because it mattered in one visible case. A hiring manager can start treating one credential like a guarantee of performance. A student can treat one study habit like a guaranteed path to a top grade. A team can treat a required approval like the whole launch plan. In each case, a real factor gets promoted into a total explanation.

The discipline here is diagnostic humility. When a result appears, ask whether the factor in front of you was required, enough, both, or neither. If the result failed to appear, ask whether a required condition was missing or whether the factor you trusted was never enough in the first place. The model keeps people from making one ingredient carry more causal weight than it deserves.

It also protects against the opposite mistake. Sometimes a condition is sufficient in one setup but not exclusive across all setups. A manager may see one tactic work and start treating it as the only route. That is another confusion. Enough does not automatically mean only. A path can produce the outcome without being the sole possible path.

So the chapter is not teaching semantic hair-splitting. It is teaching cleaner reasoning about dependence and production. What must be there? What would actually get you there? Which factor is only one ingredient? Which factor is one workable route among several? Those questions sharpen planning because they separate prerequisites from guarantees and routes from monopolies.

The limit is worth keeping in view. Thin support means the model should stay practical and narrow. It does not need symbolic notation or long technical detours to be useful. Its job is simpler: stop treating prerequisites like promises, stop treating one successful route like the only route, and use the distinction to make explanations more honest.

That prepares the next move in the book. Once the conditions behind an outcome are clearer, the natural question is what happens after the first visible effect. That is where second-order thinking begins to matter.
