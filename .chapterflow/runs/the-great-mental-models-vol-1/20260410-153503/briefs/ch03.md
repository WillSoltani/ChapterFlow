Core Claim:
Falsifiability is the discipline of stating an idea in a way that could actually be shown wrong. Popper's point is that easy confirmation proves little because people can usually find support for what they already want to believe. A stronger claim takes risk by forbidding certain outcomes and exposing itself to possible refutation. The model matters because it shifts the thinker from defending identity and intuition toward building tests that could genuinely break the theory.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
This chapter is specifically about break-tests, not about generic skepticism. Popper's science-versus-pseudoscience line, risky prediction language, and Wason's confirmation-bias frame all converge on one distinctive pressure: a claim that survives only because every outcome can be explained after the fact is weaker, not safer.

First the author shows that confirmations are cheap.
Then the author defines falsifiability as exposure to disproof.
Then the author shows why risky predictions and real tests create stronger knowledge than after-the-fact explanation.

Frameworks or Terms Introduced:

- risky prediction: a claim stakes itself on an outcome that could have gone otherwise
- confirmation trap: searching for support instead of serious disproof

Specific Applications:

- testing personal assumptions instead of collecting only flattering evidence
- spotting systems of belief that survive by reinterpretation rather than real exposure

Common Misreadings:
