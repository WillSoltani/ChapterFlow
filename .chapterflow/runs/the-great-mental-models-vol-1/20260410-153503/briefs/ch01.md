Core Claim:
Every map, model, metric, and summary is a reduction rather than the thing itself. The chapter warns that people make bad decisions when they treat an abstraction as complete reality instead of as a tool with limits. The right move is not to reject maps but to keep updating them from contact with the territory, to ask what the mapmaker included or omitted, and to notice when the map starts reshaping reality around itself. The model matters because modern life is full of dashboards, resumes, plans, rules, and expert narratives that look complete before they have earned that authority.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
This chapter is impossible to confuse with another one because it is about representations going stale, omitting risk, or distorting action. Bezos calling the customer-service line, Newton and Einstein replacing one map with another, and Jane Jacobs pushing back on city plans all show the same pressure from different angles: useful abstraction becomes dangerous when it stops answering to reality.

Author Logic Chain:
First the author establishes that maps and models are always reductions.
Then the author shows how people mistake the reduction for the world, using metrics, documents, and scientific models.
Then the author gives three working rules for safer model use and closes by showing that maps can even alter the territory they claim only to describe.

Specific Applications:

Common Misreadings:
- assuming the chapter says data is worthless
- treating the model as an excuse to ignore expert guidance entirely

Counterarguments or Limits:
Maps are necessary because the territory is too complex to carry whole. The useful distinction is not map bad, territory good. The real limit is that direct contact is costly and partial too, so the skill is disciplined updating rather than romantic anti-abstraction.
