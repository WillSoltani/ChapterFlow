Core Claim:
Second-order thinking is the discipline of tracing what happens after the first obvious effect. The model matters because first-order outcomes are often visible, simple, and tempting, while downstream consequences are where many decisions become expensive, unstable, or unexpectedly powerful. The chapter should keep returning to the practical question: and then what?

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
This chapter is specifically about consequence chains, not about generic long-term thinking. Its mechanism is to move from immediate payoff to later reactions, constraints, and knock-on effects. The distinguishing edge is that second-order thinkers do not stop at the first visible result.

Author Logic Chain:
First the author shows why first-order thinking feels attractive and incomplete.
Then the author frames second-order thinking as following consequences beyond the initial effect.
Then the author shows why better decisions often come from seeing the later costs or later compounding that other people miss.

Counterarguments or Limits:
The model is useful because it forces later effects into view, but the chapter should avoid pretending every chain can be fully mapped. Keep the focus on better practical foresight, not perfect prediction.
