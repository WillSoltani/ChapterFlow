Core Claim:
The circle of competence model says the critical skill is not claiming broad expertise but knowing where your earned knowledge is reliable and where it is not. Inside the circle, judgment carries an edge. Near the perimeter, the edge shrinks and error risk rises. Outside the circle, confidence often persists after competence is gone, which makes the chapter a warning against overreach as much as a guide to focus.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
This chapter is specifically about perimeter awareness, not generic humility. Rose Blumkin's narrow but powerful expertise, the chess-grandmaster example, and Munger's edge language all point to one non-generic mechanism: success compounds when you know what game you are qualified to play.

Author Logic Chain:
First the author defines circle of competence as earned knowledge with a boundary.
Then the author shows why perimeter awareness matters more than circle size.
Then the author uses focused examples to argue that honest limitation produces better decisions and better long-run positioning than broad pretense.

Frameworks or Terms Introduced:
- circle of competence: the domain where your judgment is earned and reliable
- perimeter: the boundary where your edge starts thinning
- confidence lag: confidence falls more slowly than competence when you move outside the circle

Specific Applications:
- choosing projects that match real expertise
- asking when an impressive signal in one domain is being overextended into another
- saying "I don't know" before a weak guess hardens into a bad commitment

Common Misreadings:
- turning the chapter into advice to stay small forever
- confusing insecurity with accuracy

Counterarguments or Limits:
Expansion matters and the source explicitly allows it through work. The chapter is not anti-growth. The limit is that honest expansion is slower than ego claims, so growth requires experiments at the edge without pretending the edge has already disappeared.
