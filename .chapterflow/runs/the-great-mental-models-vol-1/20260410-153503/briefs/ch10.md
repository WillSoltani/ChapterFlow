Core Claim:
Inversion is the discipline of approaching a problem backward by asking what would create failure, stupidity, or the outcome you want to avoid. The model matters because many problems become clearer when the thinker stops chasing brilliance directly and instead removes the causes of bad results.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
This chapter is specifically about reverse-framing, not generic contrarianism. Its distinctive edge is that a problem often becomes easier once the question flips. Instead of asking only how to succeed, inversion asks what would reliably make the effort fail.

Author Logic Chain:
First the author shows why direct attack can leave a problem fuzzy.
Then the author frames inversion as solving by reversing the question.
Then the author shows why avoiding stupidity can outperform trying to invent brilliance from scratch.

Counterarguments or Limits:
The model is useful because reverse framing exposes blind spots, but the chapter should avoid pretending inversion is the only way to think. Keep it practical: use backward analysis to surface avoidable failure modes, then return to forward action.
