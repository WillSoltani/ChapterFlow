# The Art of War — Chapter Map

Research basis: Project Gutenberg #17405 text-only Lionel Giles edition, Project Gutenberg #132 annotated Lionel Giles edition, Wikisource Giles contents page, Britannica on Sunzi, Wikipedia on _The Art of War_, Cambridge _Early China_ on deception and indirection, and modern reception literature on business and educational leadership.

## Chapter 1: Laying Plans
- **Core subject**: Sun Tzu frames war as a matter of state survival and insists that strategic success begins before battle, through comparison, estimation, and control of perception. The chapter establishes the book's master logic: disciplined assessment first, deceptive execution second.
- **Key concepts**:
  - The five constant factors: Moral Law, Heaven, Earth, the Commander, and Method and discipline
  - Seven comparative questions for forecasting victory or defeat
  - Adapting plans to favorable circumstances rather than clinging to fixed formulas
  - Deception as the normal operating method of warfare
  - Temple calculations as the pre-battle decision system
- **Notable examples/evidence**:
  - War is introduced as a life-or-death matter for the state, not a side contest
  - Sun Tzu turns strategy into an audit process by listing seven concrete comparison questions
  - The chapter's operational examples are all acts of misdirection: seeming weak, feigning disorder, attacking the unprepared
  - The closing contrast between “many calculations” and “no calculation at all” gives the book its planning ethic
- **Source richness**: rich
- **Concept density**: high
- **Key quotes**:
  - “The art of war is of vital importance to the State.”
  - “All warfare is based on deception.”
  - “Thus do many calculations lead to victory, and few calculations to defeat.”

## Chapter 2: Waging War
- **Core subject**: This chapter shifts from abstract assessment to the economics of campaigning. Sun Tzu argues that even justified war becomes self-defeating when it drags on, because prolonged campaigns destroy weapons, morale, treasury, and peasant livelihood.
- **Key concepts**:
  - The true cost of fielding a major army
  - Speed as a strategic and fiscal necessity
  - The danger of protracted warfare
  - Foraging on the enemy to preserve one's own state
  - Reward structures that turn enemy resources into usable strength
- **Notable examples/evidence**:
  - The chapter opens with a detailed cost estimate for maintaining a force of one hundred thousand men
  - Sun Tzu links distant supply, rising prices, and heavy exactions into a political-economy argument
  - Captured chariots, provisions, and prisoners are treated as strategic assets rather than trophies
  - The chapter closes by tying campaign efficiency directly to national peace or peril
- **Source richness**: rich
- **Concept density**: high
- **Key quotes**:
  - “There is no instance of a country having benefited from prolonged warfare.”
  - “Bring war material with you from home, but forage on the enemy.”
  - “In war, then, let your great object be victory, not lengthy campaigns.”

## Chapter 3: Attack by Stratagem
- **Core subject**: Sun Tzu ranks strategic methods and argues that the best victory preserves rather than destroys. The central claim is that the highest art is to break the enemy's plans, cohesion, and will before destructive battle becomes necessary.
- **Key concepts**:
  - Winning whole and intact instead of shattering what one wants to possess
  - Breaking resistance without fighting
  - A hierarchy of methods: plans, alliances, field army, siege
  - The costliness and stupidity of siege warfare
  - Command autonomy and the necessity of knowing both self and enemy
- **Notable examples/evidence**:
  - Sun Tzu contrasts capture intact with destruction at every scale: country, army, regiment, detachment, company
  - Siege is criticized through a concrete time-cost argument: months of preparation and heavy losses
  - The list of five essentials for victory turns strategic wisdom into a practical command checklist
  - The final “know the enemy and know yourself” formula becomes the book's most reusable diagnostic rule
- **Source richness**: rich
- **Concept density**: high
- **Key quotes**:
  - “Supreme excellence consists in breaking the enemy's resistance without fighting.”
  - “The worst policy of all is to besiege walled cities.”
  - “If you know the enemy and know yourself, you need not fear the result of a hundred battles.”

## Chapter 4: Tactical Dispositions
- **Core subject**: This chapter explains how strong commanders manufacture security before searching for decisive action. Sun Tzu separates what the commander can control from what depends on the enemy, then argues that error-free positioning is the basis of apparently effortless victory.
- **Key concepts**:
  - Securing oneself against defeat before seeking attack
  - Defensive and offensive postures as functions of relative strength
  - Victory by avoiding mistakes rather than seeking dramatic heroics
  - The sequence from measurement to victory
  - Complete victory as the result of structural advantage
- **Notable examples/evidence**:
  - The chapter distinguishes between making defeat impossible and forcing victory at will
  - It uses earthy analogies: hidden defence in the earth, striking attack from heaven, a pound against a grain
  - The method chain of Measurement, Estimation, Calculation, Balancing of chances, and Victory gives the chapter a quasi-engineering feel
  - The image of pent-up waters bursting through a chasm illustrates how preparation converts to overwhelming release
- **Source richness**: rich
- **Concept density**: high
- **Key quotes**:
  - “The good fighters of old first put themselves beyond the possibility of defeat.”
  - “He wins his battles by making no mistakes.”
  - “The victorious strategist only seeks battle after the victory has been won.”

## Chapter 5: Energy
- **Core subject**: Sun Tzu explains how organization and indirect action multiply force. The chapter is less about raw power than about generating momentum through combinations, timing, and coordinated appearances that make a large force act with the concentrated impact of a single blow.
- **Key concepts**:
  - Control of large forces through division, signs, and signals
  - Direct and indirect methods as a generative pair
  - Combined energy rather than dependence on heroic individuals
  - Simulated disorder, fear, and weakness as managed effects
  - Momentum, timing, and release
- **Notable examples/evidence**:
  - Sun Tzu uses analogies of music, color, and taste to show how few elements can produce endless combinations
  - The falcon strike and crossbow trigger convert timing into a theory of decision
  - The chapter links deception to disciplined structure: only a well-ordered force can successfully simulate disorder
  - Rolling logs and stones supply the signature image of accumulated momentum
- **Source richness**: rich
- **Concept density**: high
- **Key quotes**:
  - “Indirect tactics, efficiently applied, are inexhaustible as Heaven and Earth.”
  - “Simulated disorder postulates perfect discipline.”
  - “The clever combatant looks to the effect of combined energy.”

## Chapter 6: Weak Points and Strong
- **Core subject**: This chapter shows how to impose your own timing, point of contact, and strategic geometry on the enemy. Sun Tzu treats advantage as something created by forcing the opponent to divide, reveal, and overprepare while you remain concentrated and hard to read.
- **Key concepts**:
  - Forcing the enemy to respond rather than merely reacting
  - Attacking undefended points and holding unattackable ones
  - Invisibility, secrecy, and control over information
  - Concentration versus enemy dispersion
  - Tactical fluidity, expressed through the water analogy
- **Notable examples/evidence**:
  - The chapter uses relief pressure as a tactic: attack some other place the enemy must save
  - It turns knowledge of place and time into a multiplier of concentration
  - The recurring contrast is between your unified whole and the enemy's scattered fractions
  - The water comparison gives the book one of its clearest statements against fixed methods
- **Source richness**: rich
- **Concept density**: high
- **Key quotes**:
  - “The clever combatant imposes his will on the enemy.”
  - “Appear at points which the enemy must hasten to defend.”
  - “There are no constant conditions.”

## Chapter 7: Maneuvering
- **Core subject**: Sun Tzu turns movement into a problem of cost, tempo, and psychology. The chapter warns that the shortest path is not always the quickest result and that maneuver succeeds only when logistics, guides, signaling systems, and morale management all work together.
- **Key concepts**:
  - Turning devious routes into direct advantage
  - The cost of forced marches and stripped logistics
  - Reliance on local guides and knowledge of the ground
  - Rapidity, concealment, and concentrated force
  - Mood management and disciplined self-possession
- **Notable examples/evidence**:
  - The chapter quantifies attrition from forced movement at one hundred, fifty, and thirty li
  - Gongs, drums, banners, and flags are presented as tools for focusing mass attention
  - Sun Tzu maps morale over the course of a day: keen in the morning, flagging by noon, homeward in the evening
  - He ends with cautionary maxims on bait, desperate foes, and leaving an outlet when surrounding an enemy
- **Source richness**: rich
- **Concept density**: high
- **Key quotes**:
  - “In war, practise dissimulation, and you will succeed.”
  - “Let your rapidity be that of the wind, your compactness that of the forest.”
  - “A whole army may be robbed of its spirit.”

## Chapter 8: Variation in Tactics
- **Core subject**: This chapter is about disciplined flexibility. Sun Tzu argues that generals fail not because they lack facts, but because they cling to one mode of action when circumstances require them to bend rules, blend advantage with disadvantage, and refuse destructive habits of mind.
- **Key concepts**:
  - Situational exceptions: roads, armies, towns, positions, and even commands that should be refused
  - Tactical variation as practical intelligence rather than improvisational chaos
  - Blending advantage and disadvantage in planning
  - Readiness over wishful thinking
  - The five dangerous faults of a general
- **Notable examples/evidence**:
  - The chapter's opening list is a catalogue of forbidden defaults: not every road is to be followed, not every order obeyed
  - Sun Tzu pairs hope with caution by insisting leaders think through downside and upside together
  - The closing diagnosis of recklessness, cowardice, quick temper, wounded honor, and over-solicitude offers a character-based theory of failure
- **Source richness**: rich
- **Concept density**: medium
- **Key quotes**:
  - “There are roads which must not be followed.”
  - “Rely not on the likelihood of the enemy's not coming, but on our own readiness to receive him.”
  - “These are the five besetting sins of a general.”

## Chapter 9: The Army on the March
- **Core subject**: Sun Tzu combines fieldcraft, camp hygiene, reconnaissance, and diagnostic reading of enemy behavior. The chapter teaches commanders how to read terrain, dust, birds, formations, and camp behavior as signals while preserving authority and discipline within their own ranks.
- **Key concepts**:
  - Rules for mountains, rivers, marshes, and plains
  - Searching likely ambush and spy zones
  - Reading signs of enemy movement, exhaustion, and deceit
  - Diagnosing weakness inside one's own camp
  - Pairing humane treatment with iron discipline
- **Notable examples/evidence**:
  - The chapter proceeds terrain by terrain with concrete instructions for where to camp and how to fight
  - It treats environmental clues as intelligence: startled birds, dust patterns, and animal movement
  - It also reads social signals: whispering soldiers, shifting banners, too many rewards, too many punishments
  - The final lines connect discipline to the commander's ability to combine humanity with enforceable order
- **Source richness**: rich
- **Concept density**: high
- **Key quotes**:
  - “Movement amongst the trees of a forest shows that the enemy is advancing.”
  - “When the soldiers stand leaning on their spears, they are faint from want of food.”
  - “Soldiers must be treated in the first instance with humanity, but kept under control by means of iron discipline.”

## Chapter 10: Terrain
- **Core subject**: Sun Tzu classifies ground and then shifts to failures caused not by nature but by command error. The chapter's deeper claim is that terrain only becomes advantage when a general can estimate enemy strength, use authority well, and align battlefield choice with strategic reality.
- **Key concepts**:
  - Six kinds of terrain: accessible, entangling, temporising, narrow passes, precipitous heights, distant positions
  - Six calamities caused by command failure
  - Knowing when to fight or abstain despite sovereign pressure
  - Leadership that is both parental and authoritative
  - Complete knowledge through enemy, self, Heaven, and Earth
- **Notable examples/evidence**:
  - Each terrain type comes with a concrete prescription about occupation, waiting, retreat, or enticement
  - The chapter names six self-inflicted disasters: flight, insubordination, collapse, ruin, disorganisation, and rout
  - The “spoilt children” analogy captures the cost of kindness without authority
  - The final formula extends the book's earlier self/enemy maxim by adding Heaven and Earth
- **Source richness**: rich
- **Concept density**: high
- **Key quotes**:
  - “The natural formation of the country is the soldier's best ally.”
  - “If fighting is sure to result in victory, then you must fight, even though the ruler forbid it.”
  - “If you know Heaven and know Earth, you may make your victory complete.”

## Chapter 11: The Nine Situations
- **Core subject**: This is the book's largest chapter and its most elaborate treatment of strategic psychology. Sun Tzu classifies nine kinds of ground and argues that deep penetration, speed, secrecy, and controlled desperation can transform scattered troops into a cohesive force capable of extraordinary action.
- **Key concepts**:
  - The nine varieties of ground and their proper responses
  - Rapidity and unpredictability as strategic essentials
  - Cohesion through depth, movement, and danger
  - Seizing what the enemy holds dear
  - Command secrecy, misdirection, and no-retreat commitment
- **Notable examples/evidence**:
  - The chapter turns geography into a full campaign logic, from dispersive ground to desperate ground
  - The _shuai-jan_ snake illustrates responsive unity under attack from any direction
  - “Burns his boats and breaks his cooking-pots” becomes the signature image of irreversible commitment
  - The closing comparison between maidenly coyness and a running hare dramatizes strategic concealment followed by explosive release
- **Source richness**: rich
- **Concept density**: high
- **Key quotes**:
  - “Rapidity is the essence of war.”
  - “Throw your soldiers into positions whence there is no escape.”
  - “At first, then, exhibit the coyness of a maiden; afterwards emulate the rapidity of a running hare.”

## Chapter 12: The Attack by Fire
- **Core subject**: Sun Tzu narrows in on one tactical instrument, but the chapter is really about disciplined escalation. Fire is useful only when timing, wind, and follow-through are right, and even then the commander must refuse to let temper override strategic gain.
- **Key concepts**:
  - Five methods of fire attack
  - Proper seasons, winds, and timing windows
  - Coordinating attack with environmental developments
  - Fire and water as force multipliers rather than substitutes for judgment
  - Restraint: no campaigns for spleen, pique, or vanity
- **Notable examples/evidence**:
  - The five fire targets operationalize what might otherwise be an abstract exhortation
  - Sun Tzu distinguishes attacking into a fire, waiting, and staying put depending on flame behavior
  - The chapter broadens from arson tactics to political prudence with its warnings about anger, vengeance, and irrecoverable destruction
- **Source richness**: limited
- **Concept density**: medium
- **Key quotes**:
  - “Those who use fire as an aid to the attack show intelligence.”
  - “Move not unless you see an advantage.”
  - “A kingdom that has once been destroyed can never come again into being.”

## Chapter 13: The Use of Spies
- **Core subject**: The final chapter argues that intelligence is the cheapest and most decisive instrument in war. Sun Tzu closes the book by making foreknowledge, human sources, and counterintelligence central to victory, thereby linking the opening emphasis on calculation to a concrete mechanism for better decisions.
- **Key concepts**:
  - Foreknowledge as the differentiator of wise sovereigns and generals
  - Intelligence as a way to shorten war and reduce national waste
  - Five classes of spies: local, inward, converted, doomed, surviving
  - Converted spies as the hinge of the whole system
  - Secrecy, generosity, and sagacity in managing intelligence networks
- **Notable examples/evidence**:
  - The chapter opens by returning to the fiscal burden of war, making intelligence a humane economy measure
  - Sun Tzu explicitly rejects spirits, experience alone, and deduction alone as sources of foreknowledge
  - The five-spy taxonomy is the book's most explicit organizational design for information gathering
  - Historical references to I Chih and Lü Ya anchor the chapter in regime formation as well as battlefield success
- **Source richness**: rich
- **Concept density**: high
- **Key quotes**:
  - “What enables the wise sovereign and the good general to strike and conquer... is foreknowledge.”
  - “Knowledge of the enemy's dispositions can only be obtained from other men.”
  - “Spies are a most important element in war.”
