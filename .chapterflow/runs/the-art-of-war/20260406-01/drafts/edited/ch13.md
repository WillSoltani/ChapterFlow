# The Use of Spies

Count the cost before you count anything else. A hundred thousand men mobilized and marched into the field. A thousand ounces of silver spent every day. Seven hundred thousand families unable to work the land that feeds them. Hostile armies sitting across from each other for years, waiting for the single day that decides it all. Sun Tzu opens the final chapter of his book not with a doctrine but with a ledger. The argument is economic before it is tactical: any ruler who spends that much on armies and refuses to spend a fraction of it learning what the other side actually knows is committing an act of inhumanity. He is not being prudent. He is gambling with the lives of people he is supposed to protect.

The claim that follows is the sharpest in the book. What enables the wise sovereign and the good general to strike and conquer, and achieve things beyond the reach of ordinary men, is foreknowledge. Sun Tzu then rules out every alternative source categorically. Spirits cannot provide it. Inductive reasoning from past events cannot produce it. Deductive calculation from heavenly patterns cannot reach it. Knowledge of the enemy's dispositions can only be obtained from other men. This is not a practical preference between two methods of roughly equal reliability. It is a ruling: calculation can produce probability, but only human sources can produce foreknowledge — certainty about the actual situation as it exists right now.

That ruling generates the chapter's central structure: five types of human source, each addressing a different problem. Local spies are drawn from the people who already live inside the enemy's territory — they know the roads, the faces, the schedules, the pressure points. Inward spies are officials inside the enemy's own apparatus — they know the plans, the doubts, the frictions of power. Converted spies are the enemy's own intelligence operatives, detected and turned to carry intelligence back to the commander who has flipped them. Doomed spies carry deliberately false information out through a channel the enemy trusts, at the cost of the spy's capture and, implicitly, life. Surviving spies are those who go in and come back out with direct intelligence from the enemy's position. When all five types operate simultaneously, the system becomes what Sun Tzu calls the divine manipulation of threads. When no single channel is the only one, the enemy cannot determine which intelligence is genuine and which has been planted.

The reason the converted spy sits at the center is not rank but function. It is through his prior knowledge of the enemy's intelligence structure that local and inward spies can be identified and deployed correctly. It is through his knowledge of what the enemy already believes that the doomed spy's false information can be calibrated precisely enough to be convincing. It is through his knowledge of the enemy's collection patterns that surviving spies know when and where to operate without exposure. The other four types are, in a specific sense, downstream of him. Remove the converted spy and the system loses its key. He must be treated with the utmost liberality — rewarded more than any other, managed with greater care, and never terminated when discovered. The instinct to punish a detected enemy spy is exactly wrong. The converted spy is the enemy's system, running in reverse.

Two costs the chapter describes without resolving. The doomed spy must either not know that the information he carries is false and is meant to destroy him, or he must have a commitment to the mission that outweighs his own survival. The converted spy may retain incentives that could pull him back toward the side that trained him, and a commander cannot know from the inside whether that has happened. The chapter's instruction — treat the converted spy with the utmost liberality — is the management answer. Whether it is sufficient is a question Sun Tzu does not ask.

Go back to the temple in Chapter 1. The general who wins a battle makes many calculations before the fight. Those calculations require accurate knowledge of the enemy's actual position. For twelve chapters, the book described what to do with that knowledge once it is in hand. This final chapter names where the knowledge comes from. The five spy types are the information infrastructure that makes every prior chapter executable. Without them, the temple calculations run on assumption, inference, and analogy — exactly the sources this chapter rules out. Spies are a most important element in war because on them depends an army's ability to move. The loop closes here. The book that opened with a general doing calculations in a quiet room ends by naming the human network that made those calculations worth anything at all.
