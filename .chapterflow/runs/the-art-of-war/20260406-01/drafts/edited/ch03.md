# Attack by Stratagem

Begin at the worst outcome. A general has been laying siege to a walled city for six months. His men have built mantlets and movable shelters and mounds against the walls, a project that took the better part of a year. Now, unable to contain his anger at the delay, he orders the assault. His men go forward like swarming ants. A third of them die. The city remains untaken. Sun Tzu opens his third argument here, not with a success story but with the most expensive failure in the catalog, because everything else he says is organized by contrast with this image.

The contrast runs between methods ranked by what they preserve and what they cost, rather than between siege and non-siege. At the top of Sun Tzu's ranking is breaking the enemy's plans before any confrontation becomes necessary. Next is preventing the junction of their forces, disrupting the alliances and coalitions that would make them strong. Below that is engaging their army in the field, which is costly but bounded. At the bottom is the siege. Supreme excellence consists in breaking the enemy's resistance without fighting. The hierarchy is economic, not sentimental.

The economic logic is specific. What makes the top of the hierarchy better is that it preserves what you wanted in the first place. Sun Tzu opens with the preserve-intact principle: taking an enemy country whole and intact is better than shattering it; capturing an army entire is better than destroying it. By the time you have besieged a city and lost a third of your men and finally broken through the walls, the city is badly damaged and your own force is significantly weaker. You win and you are still worse off than a commander who never needed to fight. The ranking is about what kind of winning counts as actually winning, not about which tactics look most sophisticated.

A separate axis of failure runs through the middle of Sun Tzu's argument. He names three ways a ruler can ruin his own army from above: commanding advance or retreat while ignorant of the army's actual conditions, trying to govern soldiers the way he governs a civilian bureaucracy, and assigning officers without understanding what the situation requires. Each of these erodes the army's cohesion and its confidence in command. A commander can choose the right method from the hierarchy and still lose because the authority structure above him has been corrupted. Sun Tzu is making a structural observation about command integrity, not lodging a complaint about bad rulers in the abstract.

"Breaking resistance without fighting" is the hardest method in the hierarchy to execute well, not the easiest path to conflict avoidance. It requires the most precise knowledge of the opposing side, because a commander who does not know the enemy's plans cannot disrupt them. A commander who does not understand the alliances holding the opposing coalition together cannot dissolve them. A commander who has not done the five-factor comparison from the first chapter cannot locate the pressure point that collapses resistance before battle is required. The no-fight outcome is available to the best-prepared commander and unavailable to everyone else.

This is where the formula that closes Sun Tzu's argument becomes more than an aphorism. Know the enemy and know yourself, he writes, and you need not fear the result of a hundred battles. Know yourself but not the enemy: for every victory gained, you will suffer a defeat. Know neither: you will lose every time. The formula is the epistemic map of the hierarchy. The commander who has both kinds of knowledge has access to all four methods. The commander who has only one kind can fight adequately but not strategically. The commander who has neither can only proceed toward the most expensive option. A siege represents what happens when a commander has not done the knowledge work that makes the better options available, not merely a bad tactical choice. The next question is how to begin making defeat impossible before any of the better options can be reached.
