# The Attack by Fire

The fire agent is already in place. It has been there for days, positioned by someone who knew the season was coming — the dry weeks when the wind would shift into the constellations associated with rising air. The general does not act yet. He watches the sky. He is waiting for a specific day, not for urgency or anger to peak, but for conditions he identified before he positioned the agent. When the conditions arrive together, he acts. Not before.

Chapter 12 opens with a taxonomy before it opens with a theory. Five targets for fire attack: soldiers in camp, stores, baggage trains, arsenals and magazines, and dropping fire directly on the enemy. The order is not arbitrary. Soldiers can be replaced by recruitment. Stores, baggage, and arsenals take longer — they require production, transport, time that campaigns cannot wait out. The five-target list is a hierarchy of how long it takes the enemy to recover from the loss, and the logic underneath it is that the highest-leverage disruption destroys not the people conducting the campaign but the conditions that make the campaign possible.

Four conditions must be in place before fire attack is justified: the agent must already be positioned, the season must be dry, the day must fall when wind is rising, and the wind direction must be known. No condition substitutes for another. Acting before all four are met consumes the agent, alerts the enemy, and produces an action with a lower probability of success than the situation demanded. The chapter's instruction when conditions are incomplete is not to adapt — it is to wait. The precondition framework makes deferral the correct response to partial readiness. A commander who acts on three of four conditions has not shown initiative; he has forfeited the advantage the fourth condition was designed to secure.

The response doctrine for fire already burning inside the enemy camp is the chapter's most operationally demanding section. When fire breaks out inside, respond immediately from outside. But: if the enemy's soldiers remain quiet after the fire, do not attack. Wait. Watch. If no upheaval follows, the fire may be contained, the enemy may be prepared, or the moment may have passed. The external assault must be timed to the internal chaos, not to the lighting of the fire. The chapter identifies two symmetrical failure modes: attacking before the fire has done its work, when the enemy can still mount a coherent response; and attacking after the enemy has recovered, when the chaos that justified the external assault has already subsided. The correct window lies between these two moments, defined by observable upheaval — and cannot be calculated in advance. It requires someone watching in real time, reading the evidence as it appears, and deciding that the moment is now.

Fire takes. Water reinforces. An enemy may be intercepted by water — supply lines cut, movement blocked — but the enemy is not robbed of what sustains the capacity to fight. Water is additive: it adds to an existing dynamic, strengthens what is already in motion. Fire is multiplicative: it destroys the substrate, the stores and materials and shelter on which further resistance depends. Eliminating those conditions is different in kind from overcoming resistance that is still intact. The distinction is not between strength and weakness of attack; it is between what kind of damage is done. Water makes a force stronger. Fire eliminates the conditions under which the enemy's force can continue to exist.

The chapter closes with an argument that reframes everything that preceded it. A kingdom that has once been destroyed can never come again into being. The dead cannot be brought back to life. The language of irreversibility — the same logic that governs the choice of fire over water, the caution in the precondition framework, the precision in response timing — is now applied to the question of why any of this discipline matters. The enlightened ruler is heedful. The good general is full of caution. Move only when advantage is certain, victory calculable, and danger avoidable. When it is to your advantage, make a forward move. When it is not, stay where you are.

Anger may in time change to gladness. Vexation may be succeeded by content. But a kingdom destroyed does not come back. The dead stay dead. The chapter's precondition framework is not technical caution. It is the recognition that fire's irreversibility demands a different standard of justification than reversible actions. A commander who moves from anger — who wages war because he is affronted, because he desires satisfaction, because the emotion in the moment makes action feel necessary — has abandoned the precondition framework at the moment it governs most. The framework asks: is the advantage certain? Is victory calculable? Can the danger be avoided? These are not questions that anger can answer. Anger is not a precondition. It is the condition under which preconditions are most likely to go unchecked.
