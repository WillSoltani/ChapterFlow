# Terrain

A general walks the ground before the battle. Not because he does not trust the maps. Because a map tells him what the ground is; the walk tells him what the ground does. The distance between those two things — what terrain is and what terrain permits — is the gap Chapter 10 opens and refuses to close prematurely.

Sun Tzu begins with a taxonomy. Six terrain types: accessible, entangling, temporizing, narrow passes, precipitous heights, positions at great distance. But the classification is not the point. The point is that each type carries a specific decision rule, and the rule is not a preference but a constraint. Accessible terrain rewards the first mover — occupy the high sunny position, guard your communications, and you fight with advantage. Entangling terrain punishes the advancing force that fails: once in, you cannot return, so do not go unless the enemy is genuinely unprepared for your coming. Temporizing terrain converts patience into an offensive weapon — neither side benefits from moving first, so the correct move is to withdraw and entice, then strike when part of the enemy has come forward. Narrow passes: hold them fully if you arrive first; do not enter them if the enemy holds them fully. Precipitous heights: occupy the high sunny side first and wait; if forestalled, do not follow into the heights, withdraw and lure the adversary down. Positions at great distance: when forces are equal, do not provoke the fight; the engagement is structurally against you before a sword is drawn.

The six calamities follow. An army experiences flight, insubordination, collapse, ruin, disorganization, or rout — and in every case, Sun Tzu locates the cause in the general, not in the enemy. Flight follows from being ordered against a force ten times larger. Insubordination follows from soldiers stronger than their officers. Collapse follows from officers stronger than their soldiers. Ruin follows from senior officers who fight on their own account from resentment, before the commander can determine whether the position is right for battle. Disorganization follows from a weak general whose orders are unclear, whose ranks have no assigned duties, whose army is arranged in slovenly fashion. Rout follows from a general who cannot estimate the enemy's strength and sends inferior forces against superior ones without picked soldiers at the front. The catalogue is not a list of misfortunes. It is a diagnostic index: every failure has a command address.

Between the taxonomy and the calamities sits the chapter's sharpest claim. Knowing the terrain is necessary. It is not sufficient. The general who can name the six types and recite their rules, but cannot estimate the adversary, calculate the difficulties, and weigh the distances — that general has information without leverage. The test of a general is not knowledge. It is what he does with knowledge in motion.

From this the chapter draws the conclusion that unsettles the most. If fighting will produce victory, the general must fight even if the ruler forbids it. If it will not, he must not fight even at the ruler's command. This is not a claim about the general's authority over the sovereign. It is a claim about the structure of information. The ruler commands from a distance; he cannot have the field condition. Only the general on the ground has it. The duty is to the state's welfare, not to the instruction — and the instruction was issued without the knowledge the general now holds. Override is licensed when the divergence is factual and field-bound. It is not licensed by preference, by resentment, or by strategic disagreement in the abstract.

The chapter closes on the question every sustained command relationship must answer. Regard your soldiers as beloved children and they will follow into the deepest valleys, will stand by you unto death. But care without enforcement produces soldiers who are useless — spoilt children, unable to be directed. Enforcement without prior care produces soldiers who resist even fair orders, because the order arrives before the relationship that can carry it. Both are required. The error is treating them as opposites on a dial. They are not alternatives; they operate together, with care as the continuous foundation and discipline as the standard that care makes trustworthy. Neither replaces the other; neither works alone.

The walk the general takes before the battle teaches him what the map cannot: which ground commits him the moment he steps onto it, which ground rewards patience, which ground punishes the first mover and which rewards them. The same walk, taken inward — across the command structure, across the relationships he has built or failed to build with the people he leads — tells him whether his army is actually ready to act on what he has learned. Terrain without an army that can use it is a map. Army without terrain knowledge is courage without direction. The chapter holds both in the same hand and asks whether the general, when the moment comes, will be able to close the distance between them.
