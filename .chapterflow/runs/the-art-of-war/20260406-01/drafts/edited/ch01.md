# Laying Plans

The part of war Sun Tzu takes most seriously happens the night before the fighting starts, in a room with a map and a lamp and no witnesses. Picture the general walking into the ancestral temple to do the calculations. The army is already camped. The supplies are already counted. The decision to move has not been made. What happens in that room is the part of strategy the rest of the book is built to protect.

That is the smallest and strangest claim here. War is worth taking seriously not because it is dramatic but because it is expensive. Sun Tzu opens by saying that the art of war is of vital importance to the State, and he does not mean it in the tonal sense of "a big deal". He means that a country can be ended by a bad military decision. Whole populations live or die based on what happens in a room where one person is still deciding. That framing turns the general from a warrior into an auditor, and the auditor's job is to forecast whether a fight is already lost before it begins.

The forecast runs on five variables. Sun Tzu calls them the Moral Law, Heaven, Earth, the Commander, and Method and discipline. It is easy to hear that list as a menu, but it functions as a system. The Moral Law is whether your people trust their ruler enough to follow him into something deadly. Heaven is time: night, day, weather, season, and the narrow windows when action is possible. Earth is the space you fight in, with its distances, exits, tight passes, and open ground. The Commander is the individual judgment of the person in the room with the lamp. Method and discipline is whether your organization can actually move supplies and enforce orders once the decision is made. Social cohesion, time, space, individual judgment, and organizational plumbing. Each piece only matters because the other four are in play. A brilliant commander with broken supply lines is still going to lose.

What Sun Tzu does next is the part that surprises readers who expected aphorisms. He takes those five factors and turns them into seven comparison questions. Which of the two rulers has the deeper moral authority. Which general is stronger. Which side has the advantages of Heaven and Earth. Whose discipline is more rigorously enforced. Whose army is larger. Whose officers and soldiers are better trained. Whose rewards and punishments are more consistently applied. The point of the seven questions is that none of them are asked in isolation. Each one places your side next to the other side and runs the comparison. The audit is relational. A general who has studied only his own army has not done the audit at all.

Then comes the turn. Once the audit is finished and the decision to move is made, Sun Tzu says that all warfare is based on deception. This is the line most people remember, and it is also the line most often flattened. For Sun Tzu, deception does not work as a moral permission; it works as an operational method. If your audit is correct and your opponent's audit is correct, then the audits describe the same situation, and the stronger side wins. Deception is how the weaker side prevents that. When Sun Tzu tells a general to seem unable when able, or inactive when using force, or near when he is far away, he is describing a repertoire for corrupting the opponent's audit of you. Feign disorder to pull him out of his defensive position. Pretend weakness so he stops preparing for a real attack. Attack where he is unprepared because his own calculations placed his readiness somewhere else. The deception catalogue is less a theory about lying and more a theory about how perception shapes the inputs to the other side's forecast.

This produces a contradiction that sits unresolved in the text. If disciplined assessment determines who wins, then the better strategist should always prevail. But Sun Tzu also insists that deception is the normal operating method of warfare, which is how a weaker side beats a stronger one by making the stronger one's assessment wrong. Both claims are true here. Assessment is how you know what to do. Deception is how you stop the other side from doing what they otherwise would. That places the real competition somewhere most readers do not expect. The clash of armies is downstream of the clash of calculations. The general who wins is often not the one with the better position. It is the one whose picture of the battlefield is truer, and whose opponent's picture has been most successfully corrupted.

Go back to the temple. Sun Tzu ends the chapter there. The general who wins a battle, he says, makes many calculations in his temple before the battle is fought. The general who loses makes few. The general who makes none has already lost. That image is not about mental habits or hard work. It points to where the decision actually happens. Outcomes are shaped in the temple rather than on the field. What opens next is the consequence of that insight: once the calculation tells you to move, the question is no longer whether to fight but how fast you can finish before time turns your audit into a bad one.
