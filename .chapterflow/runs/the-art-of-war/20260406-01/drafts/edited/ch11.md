# The Nine Situations

A general is standing at the edge of hostile territory. His army is a day's march inside the border, the home country behind them, no fortified positions yet seized. He knows what this moment is. Not just a place on a map, but a type of positional relationship — one with its own logic, its own failure modes, its own required response. He does not pull out a general principle and apply it. He reads which kind of situation he is in.

Chapter 11 builds the most operationally demanding framework in the book. Nine ground types, each defined not by the physical landscape but by the army's positional relationship to the conflict: how deep it has gone, who controls the exits, whose soldiers are psychologically bound to home, what the supply situation is. Dispersive ground is the commander's own territory — soldiers think of family, scatter easily, fight poorly. Facile ground is the shallow edge of the enemy's territory — momentum is fragile, solidarity not yet formed, the order is to keep moving and not stop. Contentious ground is high-value position that either side could seize, and both know it; the directive is to race for it, not to initiate a pitched engagement before holding it. Open ground gives free movement to both sides; do not attempt to cut off the enemy's path. Intersecting ground forms the highway junction of three neighboring states — seize it and you have most of the strategic picture; the action is to form alliances immediately. Serious ground is deep in the enemy's country, with fortified cities in the rear; soldiers know retreat is unlikely, solidarity is high, the order is to plunder for local supply. Difficult ground is hard terrain — mountains, marshes, passes; keep moving steadily without stopping. Hemmed-in ground has one narrow entrance and one narrow exit; a small enemy force can block a much larger one; the response is stratagem, not force. Desperate ground has no exit except through fighting; the order is to fight without delay.

The logic underneath each rule is not tactical geometry. It is human psychology under pressure. Soldiers on dispersive ground are calculating men who still have options; they do not fight with full commitment because they have not yet lost the option of going home. Soldiers on serious ground are men who know the way back is closed; they fight with a quality of resolve that was not available to them when retreat remained possible. The ground type does not just describe where the army is. It describes what kind of people the situation has made of the soldiers inside it.

This is the principle behind burning the boats. The general who destroys the army's means of retreat is not punishing his soldiers or demonstrating ruthlessness. He is changing their positional relationship to the conflict, deliberately moving them from a ground type with escape built in to one without it. The psychological transformation that follows is not guaranteed — it requires that the general has already established trust and demonstrated field competence before the act, and that the primary path forward is genuinely viable. A commitment device deployed without those prior conditions does not produce resolve. It produces panic and the collapse of whatever command authority existed. But when the conditions are right, the irreversibility of the act is the mechanism. A commitment that can be undone generates a different quality of attention than one that cannot.

The same chapter that teaches the general to deliberately trap his own soldiers also provides the design principle for how a trapped force can respond to attack from any direction. The Shuai-jan serpent of the Ch'ang mountains is Sun Tzu's image: strike its head and the tail responds; strike its tail and the head responds; strike the middle and both respond simultaneously. An army built on this design answers any threat anywhere without requiring that every soldier first receive the signal, deliberate, and choose to cooperate. The coordination is structural. It is built into the mutual support architecture of the force before any engagement begins — in the communication protocols, the named contacts, the shared rapid-response patterns that connect each part of the force to every other. A force that articulates the serpent ideal without building the underlying architecture will coordinate smoothly in training conditions and fragment under real pressure, precisely when the stakes make coordination most necessary.

The chapter closes on the intelligence prerequisite that makes all of it work or fail. Knowing that nine ground types exist is useless without knowing which type the army is currently on. That knowledge requires something prior: verified intelligence about the intentions of neighboring states, the specific conditions of the terrain, the locations of local guides who know the ground in ways no arriving commander can. Without that intelligence, the nine-ground framework produces false confidence — the particularly dangerous kind that comes from applying a rule correctly to a misclassified situation. The general who has misread his position and chosen the appropriate rule for the position he believes he is in will execute with precision and end up in the wrong place. He cannot tell the error from correct performance through internal experience alone. Both feel identical while the wrong action is being taken.

The framework's depth is that it does not permit a single classification and then a fixed response. The ground types are not stable across a campaign. Facile ground at entry becomes serious ground after two weeks of advance. What was open ground becomes hemmed-in ground when the army's line of supply is cut. The general who classifies the position once and stops updating has mistaken a dynamic instrument for a static one. Reading which situation the army is currently in is not the beginning of the chapter's work. It is the continuous work of anyone who commands.
