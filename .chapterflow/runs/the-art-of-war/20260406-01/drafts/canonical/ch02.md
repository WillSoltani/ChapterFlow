# Waging War

Picture a treasurer in a palace counting out a thousand ounces of silver for one day of operations. He does it again the next day. And the day after. The army has not yet won anything. It is simply continuing to exist. Somewhere in another palace, another ruler is making the same calculation about the same kind of army. What Sun Tzu wants the reader to feel at the beginning of this chapter is that the silver does not stop. Every day the campaign runs is a day the state gets poorer, and a day the window in which victory was possible gets smaller.

That feeling is the chapter's whole thesis. Sun Tzu opens with a concrete cost estimate: a thousand chariots, a thousand heavy chariots, a hundred thousand mail-clad soldiers, provisions for a thousand li, entertainment of guests, glue and paint and harness and armour, all totaling one thousand ounces of silver per day. The point of the estimate is not the number. The point is that the expenditure runs for as many days as the campaign does, which means the cost is not a budget line but a hemorrhage.

Once the treasury is bleeding, other things start failing in a specific sequence. The weapons dull. The soldiers' morale flags. The strength of the army drops. The treasury runs lower, which starves the supply chain, which lowers the morale further. And somewhere else, other rulers watch the whole process and start to move. Sun Tzu names them the opportunistic chieftains who spring up to take advantage of your extremity, and he makes a sharp observation about them: once they arrive, no amount of wisdom on the commander's part can undo the situation. The war has taught the whole neighborhood that your state is exhausted. No country, he says plainly, has ever benefited from prolonged warfare.

The operational answer Sun Tzu offers to this problem is counterintuitive and specific. Bring war material with you from home, he writes, but forage on the enemy. Keep your supply lines short. Convert captured chariots, captured supplies, captured prisoners into strength that replaces what home would otherwise have to send. One cartload of the enemy's provisions, he estimates, is worth twenty from your own store, because your own store has already been paid for in silver and sweat before it ever reaches the front. The foraging doctrine is not a moral theory. It is a logistics rule meant to protect the home state from the very campaign it authorized.

There is a contradiction in this chapter that Sun Tzu sets up carefully. He tells commanders to finish quickly, and then he tells them they have never seen cleverness in long delays but have frequently heard of stupid haste in war. Both warnings are true. The difference between stupid haste and decisive finishing is the previous chapter's audit. A commander who has done the five-factor comparison and seen that the window is open can push for a fast finish because he knows what the window is. A commander who has not done the audit but is moving quickly is producing the same motion without the information that justifies it. The speed looks identical from outside. The outcomes are not.

What Sun Tzu does at the end of this chapter is the move that keeps the argument from being a logistics memo. He says the leader of armies is the arbiter of the people's fate, the person on whom the peace or peril of the nation depends. The line reframes everything that came before. The silver is not a bookkeeping number. It is the measure of how much weight the peasantry will carry in the form of heavier taxation, drained stores, and lost sons. The tempo discipline is not a style preference. It is what it looks like for a general to take responsibility for the people whose lives are now tied to the length of the campaign. In war, then, Sun Tzu writes, let your great object be victory rather than lengthy campaigns. The next chapter will ask what the fastest version of victory actually looks like, and the answer will not be what most readers expect.
