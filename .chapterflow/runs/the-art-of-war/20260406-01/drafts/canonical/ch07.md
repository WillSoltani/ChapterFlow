# Maneuvering

A commander starts after his opponent. He takes the road the opponent is not watching. He arrives at the objective first. The opponent, who took the obvious route, spends the whole journey trying to understand what happened.

Sun Tzu calls this the artifice of deviation. The circuitous route becomes the direct one when the opponent's attention is locked onto the short path. The mechanism requires two things operating simultaneously: a lure that draws the opponent away, and a deviation route that is genuinely clear. If either fails, the deviation produces delay without gain.

But speed imposes a precise cost that the deviation does not dissolve. Forcing the march one hundred li loses all leaders. Fifty li loses half the force. Thirty li costs a third. This is not a caution about rushing. It is a specific cost schedule for trading force integrity against distance. A plan that devises the perfect positional advantage but requires a hundred-li march to reach it may arrive at the position without enough force to hold it.

An army without baggage is lost. Without provisions, lost. Without supply bases, lost. These three dependencies are not background conditions. They are active constraints. Movement is not just displacement. It is sustained displacement, and sustaining it requires each link in the logistics chain to hold. Each dependency is also a lever the opponent can sever without engaging the force in direct combat.

Morale runs on a daily cycle. Morning spirit is keenest. By noon it flags. By evening it is spent. The tactical application is direct: attack when the opponent is flagging, wait when they are keen. The broader application extends to stacking advantages. Wait at ease while they toil. Arrive near the goal while they are still far. Be fed while they are hungry. Each advantage compounds the others.

Gongs and drums unify the dispersed force across noise. Banners and flags extend the force's sight. Together they allow a large army to act as one organism in conditions where individual voices and eyes cannot carry far enough.

Seven prohibitions close the chapter. Each names a specific trap that presents as an opportunity. The retreating enemy that is actually baiting pursuit. The keen opponent who is daring you to move first. The surrounded army that becomes most dangerous when pressed. The pattern is the same in every case: the apparent opportunity is the mechanism of the danger. The prohibitions name what to refuse. Knowing when the specific situation is an instance of the trap is the judgment they point toward but cannot provide.
