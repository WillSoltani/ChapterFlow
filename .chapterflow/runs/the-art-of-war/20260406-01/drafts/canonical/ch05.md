# Energy

A soldier holds a crossbow with the bolt locked and the string drawn back to its limit. Nobody watching from outside can see the tension in the bow. When the bolt finally releases, the strike will appear effortless, almost spontaneous. Sun Tzu uses this image to introduce a principle he calls shi, energy or momentum: potential stored before it is needed, released at the moment of maximum effect. The image matters because it separates energy from mere force. Force applied continuously at full effort is expenditure. Energy accumulated and then released at the right moment converts the same effort into a multiplied outcome. The strategic discipline lives in the gap between the two.

Two methods govern how a skilled force operates. The direct method, zheng, holds the engagement. It joins battle, keeps the opponent's attention fixed, maintains contact, and prevents retreat. The indirect method, qi, decides the outcome. It works from an angle the opponent did not model, at a moment the opponent did not anticipate, on terrain the opponent did not prepare for. The distinction is not between attacking and not attacking. Both methods attack. The distinction is between the function of holding the engagement and the function of deciding it. A force that only uses the direct method is fighting a fair contest. The indirect method is what tilts the contest before it is formally decided.

From these two elements, Sun Tzu argues, infinite variations arise. He grounds this in the mathematics of combination: five notes, five primary colors, five flavors, each producing more combinations than can be experienced in a lifetime. The practical claim is that no tactical response can exhaust the indirect options available to a skilled commander. Every defensive response the opponent makes to the direct engagement creates new angles for the indirect. The indirect method has no natural limit because the opponent's responses are themselves inputs to the next iteration.

Simulated disorder demonstrates the energy principle at the level of the whole force. A skilled commander can make a disciplined force appear chaotic, fragmented, overwhelmed. Outside observers see confusion. Inside the force, precise coordination is running exactly as planned. The paradox has an organizational implication: only a force with real internal structure can manufacture convincing disorder. A genuinely disorganized force cannot simulate chaos on purpose, because simulation requires control over the gap between what is projected and what is real.

Combined energy applies the same logic to the system as a whole. Sun Tzu's instruction is to seek combined energy rather than requiring too much from individual soldiers. The rolling-stone metaphor captures the mechanism: a round stone placed on a slope generates momentum from its position, not from any internal quality of the stone. The commander's skill is in constructing the slope, placing soldiers in conditions where the position generates energy, so that the release, when it comes, is the product of accumulated systemic potential rather than exceptional individual effort under pressure.
