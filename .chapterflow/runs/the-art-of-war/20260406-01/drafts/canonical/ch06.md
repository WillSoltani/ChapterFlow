# Weak Points and Strong

An exhausted messenger arrives at the ridge three hours after dawn. The commander who called the meeting has been there since first light: tents pitched, water boiling, ground already known. The messenger is not less capable. He arrived second, and second means starting from conditions someone else chose. Sun Tzu opens the chapter on that single asymmetry. Whoever is first in the field sets the terms, frames the engagement, and occupies the prepared ground. The latecomer arrives depleted and must respond on terrain he did not select.

From that opening, Sun Tzu develops two mechanisms for ensuring you are the one who arrives first in conceptual terms. The first is positive: offer what the opponent must come toward. Give them a reason they cannot decline, and their rational interests will direct them toward your prepared position. The second is negative: threaten what they cannot afford to abandon. Impose a cost on any response other than moving toward your chosen engagement point. A skilled commander does not wait for the contest to begin and then try to respond. He shapes the situation so that the opponent's best available choices all lead toward a position already prepared.

The knowledge requirement behind both mechanisms is stated explicitly. Discover the opponent's dispositions while concealing your own. That asymmetry makes concentration possible. A commander who knows where the opponent is thin, while keeping his own distribution opaque, can bring his whole force against a fraction of theirs. Without that knowledge gap, concentration is guesswork. The active work of gathering intelligence and the active work of maintaining concealment are both necessary to sustain the asymmetry.

Two precise targeting rules follow directly. Attack only positions the opponent has left undefended; that is where concentration wins reliably. Defend only positions that cannot be attacked; that is where commitment is not required. Both rules produce reliable outcomes by restricting engagement to situations where the terms are already favorable before the commitment is made.

Water closes the chapter with an image that carries its own argument. Water has no constant shape but always flows to the low ground, governed by the actual terrain rather than by habit or precedent. Tactics must work the same way. A method that produced a victory in the first engagement has also revealed an approach to every opponent who observed the result. Repeating the method converts a surprise into a signal. The heaven-born captain is defined as the one who modifies methods in relation to the specific opponent and the specific moment, with no fixed form and no repeatable recipe.
