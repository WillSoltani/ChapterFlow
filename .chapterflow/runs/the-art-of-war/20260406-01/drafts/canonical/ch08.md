# Variation in Tactics

The courier arrived before dawn with a sealed order: advance on the northern ford at first light and take the position before the enemy could reinforce it. The general read the order twice, then set it down on the campaign table beside his maps. He had spent three days on the ridge above that ford, watching. The position was too easily held, too openly offered, the ground behind it funneling any attacking force into a narrow approach that the enemy could collapse from two sides simultaneously. The order was not wrong in its ambition; it was simply written by men who had not seen what he had seen. The position was bait. The order did not know this. He did.

Sun Tzu opens this chapter not with a list of rules but with the recognition that rules are insufficient. The nine variations are not nine specific situations to be memorized like a catechism. They are a category: the set of circumstances in which the normal rule produces the wrong result. A road that ordinarily must be taken must sometimes not be taken. An army that ordinarily must be attacked must sometimes be let pass. A position that ordinarily must be contested must sometimes be surrendered. The general's work is not to memorize which roads, which armies, which positions; it is to develop the judgment to recognize when he is standing inside one of those exceptions. That recognition is the whole of the skill.

This is why Sun Tzu insists that a general who knows the terrain but not the nine variations cannot convert his knowledge into advantage. Factual knowledge of the ground, the distances, the water sources, the choke points, is necessary but not sufficient. It becomes useful only when a second layer of understanding is present: the capacity to ask, of any situation, whether the standard rule applies or whether this is one of the cases where following the standard rule is precisely what the enemy is counting on. The terrain tells the general what is possible. The nine variations tell him what to do with what is possible. Without the second layer, the first is merely description.

The chapter's account of dual consideration follows from this. Every situation contains both advantage and disadvantage, not as separate features to be weighed against each other, but as properties folded into the same fact. The ford that makes a quick crossing possible also makes a quick retreat impossible. The fortified position that offers protection also offers confinement. A general who sees only the advantage in a favorable situation will overcommit; he will advance without accounting for what the advantage costs him. A general who sees only the disadvantage in an unfavorable situation will not act; he will hold when holding is the slower form of defeat. The discipline Sun Tzu is describing is the practice of running both assessments at the same time, of holding the advantage and the disadvantage that lives inside it as a single object of thought.

The five faults belong to this framework. Recklessness, cowardice, a quick temper, excessive concern for reputation, over-solicitude for the men: these are not simply character defects that a better-raised officer would not have. They are predictability signatures. An opponent who has observed long enough knows which fault is present and can arrange conditions to trigger it. The reckless commander can be lured into an unsuitable engagement by the appearance of a vulnerable target. The commander protective of his honor can be drawn out by a calculated insult. The fault, once known, becomes a handle by which the enemy steers without ever giving a direct order. Knowing one's own fault is not a matter of self-improvement alone; it is strategic information, because it reveals the most likely mechanism by which an opponent will try to use you against yourself.

The general at the campaign table understood this too. Headquarters had issued an order that reflected no knowledge of the field. To follow it was not loyalty; it was the transfer of decision-making authority to men who lacked the information required to decide. Sun Tzu is explicit: there are commands from the sovereign that must not be obeyed. This is not insubordination. It is the correct operation of delegated authority. The sovereign delegates command to the general precisely because the general will be present at the ground and the sovereign will not. When the general's positional knowledge contradicts the sovereign's instruction, the general's knowledge is authoritative. The delegation only functions if the general uses it.

He set the sealed order aside and issued his own: the ford would not be attacked at dawn. He had three days of observation and one direct order, and only one of them contained the truth of the situation. Knowing which was which was the entire art.
