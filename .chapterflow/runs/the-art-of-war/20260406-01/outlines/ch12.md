# Chapter 12 Outline: The Attack by Fire

---

## Easy Depth — 3 Key Takeaways (point only)

1. Five fire targets define a taxonomy of high-leverage disruption points: the goal is maximum damage at the point of maximum vulnerability, not random destruction. Targeting stores and arsenals disrupts what the enemy's operational capacity depends on; targeting soldiers disrupts only the people prosecuting it.

2. Fire attack requires all four preconditions to be in place before action: the agent must be ready, the season must be dry, the day must be right, and the wind direction must be known. Acting before any condition is met wastes the opportunity and alerts the enemy without achieving the intended effect.

3. War must never be waged from anger. Anger passes; a destroyed kingdom cannot be rebuilt and the dead cannot be restored. Move only when advantage is certain, halt when it is not. The consequences of emotionally reactive decisions outlast the emotion that produced them.

---

## Medium Depth — 5 Key Takeaways (point + moreDetails direction)

**T1 — Five fire targets: a hierarchy of operational disruption**
Point: The five targets — soldiers in camp, stores, baggage trains, arsenals and magazines, and dropping fire on the enemy — address different layers of the enemy's operational capacity. Attacking stores and arsenals disrupts the substrate on which the campaign depends. Attacking soldiers disrupts the people prosecuting it. The hierarchy implies: disrupt logistics and supply before engaging soldiers, because materiel is harder to replace than personnel.
moreDetails direction: The five-target hierarchy reveals that the chapter's real subject is disruption of operational capacity, not destruction per se. Stores and arsenals represent the supply chain that makes sustained combat possible. An army whose stores are destroyed cannot resupply at the same rate it can replace casualties. Frame each target in terms of how long it takes the enemy to reconstitute — the higher the reconstitution cost, the higher the target's strategic leverage.

**T2 — Four preconditions as a decision filter**
Point: The fire agent must be in place, the season must be dry, the day must fall when the moon is in wind-associated constellations, and the wind direction must be known. No precondition can be substituted for another. If any one is unmet, the action should be deferred, not improvised around.
moreDetails direction: The precondition framework functions as a decision gate, not a preference. Improvising around an unmet precondition — using fire in the wrong season, without an agent already in place — produces an action with a lower probability of success while consuming the agent and alerting the enemy. The framework is designed to make deferral the correct response to partial readiness, not a fallback for weak commanders.

**T3 — Response timing: real-time observation, not pre-set plans**
Point: After fire is set inside the enemy camp, observe first. If upheaval follows, coordinate the external attack with the internal collapse. If no upheaval follows, wait and do not attack. The correct window cannot be calculated in advance; it must be read from observable evidence in real time.
moreDetails direction: The failure modes are symmetrical. Attacking too early — before the fire has disrupted the enemy's capacity to respond — produces an assault against a force that has not yet been compromised. Attacking too late — after the enemy has recovered from the chaos — produces an assault against a force that has reconstituted. The correct window is defined by the upheaval, not by the plan, and real-time observation is the only method that can catch it.

**T4 — Fire takes; water reinforces**
Point: Water can reinforce an attack already underway but cannot destroy the conditions for enemy resistance the way fire can. Fire is multiplicative — it eliminates the substrate on which further resistance depends. Water is additive — it strengthens what is already there. The distinction determines which tool is appropriate to the objective.
moreDetails direction: The water/fire distinction is a proxy for a broader analytical category: some actions strengthen an existing dynamic (additive) and some eliminate the preconditions for that dynamic to continue (multiplicative). The most powerful disruptions are multiplicative. An action that appears to destroy something may be only additive if the enemy has substitute resources — the diagnosis requires knowing the enemy's resource structure before selecting the type of intervention.

**T5 — The three-condition rule as a check on anger**
Point: Move only when advantage is certain, victory calculable, and danger avoidable. If any condition fails, do not move. War must not be waged from anger. Anger may change to gladness, but a kingdom destroyed cannot be restored. The three conditions apply at every decision point, not just at campaign opening.
moreDetails direction: The three-condition rule is not a checklist run once before a campaign is committed. It is a continuous evaluation that can reverse a decision at any point before the action becomes irreversible. The anger prohibition is what explains why: anger is the emotion most likely to short-circuit continuous evaluation. A commander who wages war from anger has abandoned the evaluation at the moment it matters most — when emotional pressure makes violation feel justified.

## Hard Depth — Same 5 Takeaways (what hard moreDetails should add)

**T1 hard:** The five targets differ not just in strategic leverage but in the timing of their effects. Disrupting stores and arsenals requires time to produce operational consequence — the enemy army continues to fight from existing supply while the disruption works through the logistics chain. Disrupting soldiers produces immediate tactical effects. The strategic choice is between fast tactical gain and slower but more durable operational collapse. A commander who always prefers the faster effect will systematically underinvest in the higher-leverage targets that take longer to manifest.

**T2 hard:** The precondition framework creates a waiting cost the chapter does not resolve. A commander who waits for all four conditions to be simultaneously met may wait long enough that the political or military context changes and the opportunity disappears entirely. The framework prescribes deferral when conditions are unmet, but does not provide a rule for when deferral itself becomes the wrong response. The framework's silence on the waiting cost is a structural gap: it treats the precondition set as a necessary filter but does not acknowledge that filters have a temporal cost.

**T3 hard:** Real-time observation of whether "upheaval" has occurred requires prior agreement on what counts as upheaval and what counts as recovery. Without explicit criteria defined before the fire is set, two observers watching the same enemy camp will reach different conclusions from the same evidence. One sees upheaval; the other sees organized response. One sees recovery; the other sees a lull before renewed chaos. The operational precision the chapter demands cannot be achieved by observation alone — it requires pre-defined criteria for the signals that will trigger or defer the external attack.

**T4 hard:** The additive/multiplicative distinction requires knowing the enemy's resource structure before the intervention is selected. An action that appears multiplicative — destroying an arsenal — is only additive if the enemy has substitute arsenals or can resupply from allied sources. The diagnosis of what the enemy's actual substrate is requires intelligence that may be unavailable. A commander who assumes the target is multiplicative when the enemy has substitutes has selected the wrong type of intervention. The water/fire distinction presupposes knowledge of what the fire will actually destroy and what remains after it.

**T5 hard:** The three-condition rule contains a self-certification problem. A commander who believes advantage is certain and victory calculable has run the calculation himself — and the calculation is only as valid as the inputs. A commander motivated by anger or urgency will find inputs that support the conclusion he wants to reach. The three-condition rule stops motivated reasoning in theory but not in practice: the conditions can be satisfied on paper by a commander who is already committed to acting. The rule's value depends on whether the evaluation is honest, and the chapter does not specify a structural mechanism for ensuring that honesty — it relies on the commander's character.

## Examples

**Ex1 — Contrast (work)**
Characters: Two project leads, Odette and Stellan, managing competitive responses at the same company.
Concept: Fire vs. water; additive versus multiplicative disruption (T4).
Scene: Odette's team is responding to a competitor's new product by doubling headcount on their own product team — adding force to the same direction the competitor is moving. Stellan's team targets the competitor's supply relationship with a key component manufacturer, working to secure a preferred-partner agreement that the competitor will have to work around. Odette's approach is water: it reinforces what her company is already doing. Stellan's approach is fire: it attacks the substrate the competitor's product development depends on. Two years later, Odette's team has more engineers but the competitor still ships. Stellan's contract has constrained the competitor's production timeline.
Ending type: consequence — the contrast shows that the type of disruption determines whether the competitor's capacity is slowed or eliminated, and that more force in the same direction is not the same as disrupting the conditions that make the competitor's force possible.

**Ex2 — Vignette (school)**
Character: Wren, an undergraduate thesis student at a liberal arts college.
Concept: Preconditions before action — conditions must be met before committing, not improvised around (T2).
Scene: Wren's thesis depends on data from an IRB-approved survey. The survey is approved, the sample is identified, and the deadline for data collection is three weeks out. A professor suggests she run a preliminary analysis on a smaller convenience sample now to get early findings, without waiting for the full sample to be collected. Wren considers it. She recognizes that the convenience sample lacks the properties that make the main survey's data valid — using it would produce results she would have to disclaim in the thesis. The precondition for analysis is the complete, IRB-approved sample. Running the analysis on substitute data is improvising around an unmet condition, not satisfying it. She declines and waits.
Ending type: self_directed_question — Wren's choice raises a question the vignette leaves open: how often do we substitute a condition we have for the condition we need, and call the substitution good enough?

**Ex3 — Before/After (personal)**
Character: Nneka, a parent managing a conflict with a teenage daughter over academic performance.
Concept: The anger prohibition — the consequences of decisions made from reactive emotion outlast the emotion itself (T5).
Before: After seeing a failing grade, Nneka reacts in the moment — she cancels the daughter's extracurricular activities immediately, takes the phone, and delivers a speech she later regrets. Her daughter shuts down. The academic performance does not improve. The relationship degrades for three months.
After: Eighteen months later, facing a similar situation, Nneka pauses before responding. She runs the three-condition check: is the action she is considering likely to improve outcomes (advantage certain)? Can she predict that it will work (victory calculable)? Is the risk of damaging the relationship further avoidable (danger avoidable)? She does not pass all three. She schedules a conversation for the next day instead. The conversation produces a plan the daughter agrees to.
Ending type: reversal — the reversal is not a change of heart but a change of method: deferring from the reactive action to the deliberate one changes the outcome by changing what the conversation is about.

**Ex4 — Decision Point (work)**
Character: Cyprian, a communications director at a regional hospital system.
Concept: Response timing — observe first; if upheaval follows disruption, coordinate the external action; if not, wait (T3).
Scene: A negative investigative news story about a competing hospital system breaks overnight. Cyprian's team has a prepared press strategy — positioning their own system as the alternative — that they have been holding for the right moment. His deputy wants to release it immediately while the competitor is under fire. Cyprian holds the release. He watches how the competitor responds through the morning. By noon, the competitor has issued a credible public statement, activated their patient advocacy program, and redirected news coverage to a positive community initiative. The chaos window has closed. Cyprian re-shelves the press strategy. Releasing it now, against a competitor who has reconstituted their public position, would produce an attack on a prepared adversary rather than a coordinated strike into an active disruption.
Ending type: open_question — the decision point leaves open whether Cyprian would have gained more by moving immediately, or whether the competitor's recovery was predictable and waiting was correct regardless.

**Ex5 — Inner Monologue (school)**
Character: Nikolai, a graduate student preparing to defend a research grant proposal.
Concept: Three-condition rule — advantage certain, victory calculable, danger avoidable; the anger prohibition (T5).
Scene: Nikolai's grant proposal is scheduled for a panel review in three weeks. A committee member he has a difficult history with sends a terse email questioning one of his core methodological assumptions two days before the submission deadline. Nikolai's first instinct is to respond immediately — to defend the methodology in an email, setting up a dispute before the panel has even convened. He drafts a reply. Then he sits with it. He runs the three conditions: does an email debate with this committee member improve his chances before the panel (advantage certain)? Can he predict the exchange will help rather than harm (victory calculable)? Does the response risk creating an adversarial dynamic that will carry into the panel room (danger avoidable)? He fails the third condition. He deletes the draft and asks for a brief phone call instead.
Ending type: bridge — the decision to call rather than write is the bridge between the reactive response and the deliberate one; the same concern, addressed through a different channel, at a different moment, produces a different quality of exchange.

**Ex6 — Postmortem (personal)**
Character: Casimir, a landlord reflecting six months after a contentious tenant dispute.
Concept: Five fire targets — targeting the substrate versus targeting the surface problem; knowing which layer of the problem to address (T1).
Scene: Six months earlier, Casimir responded to a difficult tenant situation by escalating to formal legal proceedings immediately. He had a legitimate grievance, a documented record, and legal standing. The proceedings took four months, cost significantly in legal fees, produced the eviction he sought, and left the property damaged by a tenant who knew they were leaving. In the postmortem, Casimir realizes he targeted the wrong layer. The tenant's behavior was the surface — the equivalent of soldiers in camp. The substrate was the tenant's financial situation, which made the tenancy unviable and was the actual cause of the non-payment. Had he addressed the substrate — offering a structured exit or a repayment arrangement that made compliance in the interest of both parties — he might have avoided the legal proceedings, the fees, and the property damage. He targeted soldiers when the leverage was in the supply chain.
Ending type: broader_principle — the postmortem generalizes: when a conflict has a surface problem and a substrate problem, attacking the surface creates costs the substrate does not; the highest-leverage intervention targets what the surface problem depends on.

## implementationPlan Direction

The implementation plan should center on two practical skills: (1) the precondition check — before committing to any high-stakes irreversible action, identify the conditions that must be true, verify each one specifically, and treat any unmet condition as a reason to defer rather than improvise; and (2) the three-condition rule applied as an emotional-state check — particularly when anger, urgency, or frustration is present, run the advantage/victory/danger evaluation before acting, specifically because those emotions are the conditions under which the evaluation is most likely to be skipped.

The work context should apply to decisions about when to launch a competitive response or escalate a conflict. The school context should apply to decisions about when to push back on institutional decisions or challenge a committee member or evaluator. The personal context should apply to emotionally charged relationship decisions where reactive responses are most tempting.

The twenty-four-hour challenge should be specific: identify one decision currently pending where you are experiencing some urgency or frustration, run the three-condition check explicitly, and write down which condition you are least certain about. The weekly practice should focus on the precondition habit: for one active situation per week, list the conditions that must be true before a specific action is justified, and note which conditions you have verified versus assumed.
