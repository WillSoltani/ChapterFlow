# Chapter 3 Outline — Attack by Stratagem

## Chapter Promise
By the end, the reader should understand that winning without destructive battle is possible, that it requires a hierarchy of methods ranked by outcome quality and cost, and that all of it depends on the relational knowledge the "know yourself and the enemy" formula names.

## Opening Move
Open inside the siege. A commander watching an assault fail after six months of preparation and one-third losses, the town still untaken. Not the best example but the most revealing: it shows what happens when the hierarchy of methods is inverted and a commander reaches for the costliest option first.

## Anchor Allocation

## Paragraph Job Map
- P1: Hook — the siege image. The commander who could not control his irritation, the men launched like swarming ants, the one-third losses, the town still standing. The first image of the chapter is its worst-case outcome to establish what the hierarchy is designed to avoid.
- P2: Frame — the hierarchy of outcomes and methods. Capturing intact beats destroying. Four methods ranked by quality: break plans first, disrupt alliances second, field battle third, siege worst. Supreme excellence consists in breaking resistance without fighting.
- P3: Mechanism — why the hierarchy is not just a preference for caution. Siege takes six months of preparation, then another six for the assault works. The angry general who launches anyway loses a third of his men and may not take the town. The hierarchy is not an aesthetic judgment. It is a cost calculation.
- P4: The orthogonal failure mode — civilian interference. Three ways a ruler ruins his own army: commanding movements while ignorant of conditions, governing an army like a kingdom, employing officers without discrimination. This is a different axis of failure from the method hierarchy; a commander can choose the right method and still lose if the command authority above him is broken.
- P5: Distinction — "breaking resistance without fighting" is the hardest method, not the easiest. It requires the best intelligence, the most accurate relational knowledge, and the most sophisticated positioning. A commander who has not done the Ch1 audit cannot reliably reach for the top method. The hierarchy is not a shortcut to avoiding hard work; it is a measure of what a correctly-prepared commander can achieve.
- P6: Bridge — the formula. "Know the enemy and know yourself, and you need not fear the result of a hundred battles." The formula is the epistemic prerequisite for the hierarchy. A commander who has both kinds of knowledge can choose correctly from the four methods. A commander who knows only one side is operating at half capacity and will win some and lose some. A commander who knows neither will lose every time. End on the live question for Ch4: if knowing yourself is the prerequisite, what does "putting yourself beyond the possibility of defeat" actually require?

## Required Distinction or Mechanism
The distinction: **fighting well is not the highest form of generalship; making destructive fighting unnecessary is.** This runs against the intuitive model of military excellence, which tends to celebrate technical mastery in battle. Sun Tzu is arguing that technical mastery in battle is the third-best option, and the first two options are available only to a commander who has done the relational knowledge work that most commanders skip.

## Hard-Depth Tension
The genuine tension: **is the highest form (breaking resistance without fighting) always available, or is it conditional on having relational knowledge that most commanders lack most of the time?** Sun Tzu treats it as a goal to aim for, but the force-ratio rules suggest he knows commanders will often have to fight. Hard depth must hold the threshold question: when does attempting the highest form become self-defeating because the knowledge to execute it does not exist, and the time spent trying has cost the window for the lower methods?

## Threshold Question
**Does pursuing the highest form of strategy become its own form of delay when the relational knowledge required to execute it is not available?**

## Hard Takeaway Topics
- Takeaway 1: Capture-intact beats destroy — the evaluative frame the hierarchy sits inside.
- Takeaway 2: Four-method hierarchy, ranked by outcome quality and cost.
- Takeaway 3: Siege as the failure case that calibrates the rest of the hierarchy.
- Takeaway 4: Civilian interference as an orthogonal failure mode that the method hierarchy cannot solve.
- Takeaway 5: The highest form requires the best knowledge — it is not a shortcut.
- Takeaway 6 (hard): The threshold question about whether pursuing the highest form can become its own trap.

## Scenario Lesson Map
- Ex1 (dilemma, work): Yusuf deciding whether to compete head-to-head in a market segment or find an uncontested angle. Teaches: the four-method hierarchy applied to competitive strategy.
- Ex2 (dialogue, school): Amara at moot court prep, deciding how to approach the opposing team's likely argument. Teaches: breaking the opponent's plans through preparation rather than out-arguing them during the round.
- Ex3 (decision_point, personal): Nadia deciding between a direct family argument about property and finding the underlying friction point. Teaches: resolving resistance at the source rather than relitigating the surface.
- Ex4 (before_after, work): Petra's sales approach before and after she stops leading with product feature superiority and starts mapping the customer's real constraints. Teaches: capturing the outcome intact (a willing, retained customer) beats winning an argument.
- Ex5 (postmortem, school): Leon reviewing a student council election he won but at high cost to his relationship with the opposing candidate. Teaches: winning is not the same as winning well.
- Ex6 (predict_reveal, personal): Kwame predicting his partnership exit negotiation outcome if he pushes hard, then revealing what a more structured approach produced. Teaches: the hierarchy applied to interpersonal negotiations.

## Genericity Risks
- "Winning without fighting" becoming generic "avoid conflict" self-help.
- "Know yourself and the enemy" becoming a generic self-awareness slogan.
- Siege metaphor becoming a generic "don't siege your employees" management cliché.
- Civilian interference becoming a pure complaint about micromanagement rather than a diagnostic.
