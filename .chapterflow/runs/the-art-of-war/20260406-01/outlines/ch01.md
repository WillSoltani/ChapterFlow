# Chapter 1 Outline — Laying Plans

## Chapter Promise
By the end, the reader should understand that strategic success is forecasted before it is earned, that Sun Tzu's audit of five factors through seven questions is a comparison ritual rather than a checklist, and that deception is the operational method for protecting an assessment that the opponent would otherwise correct.

## Opening Move
Open inside the image of a general walking into the ancestral temple the night before a campaign to do the calculations. Not a thesis about planning. A scene with a person, a map, a lamp, a decision that hasn't been made yet.

## Anchor Allocation
- Anchor 1 — five constant factors: used in paragraphs P2 and P3 (setup and mechanism).
- Anchor 2 — seven comparative questions: used in paragraph P3 (showing the audit in operation).
- Anchor 3 — temple calculations image: used in P1 (opening) and P6 (bridge).
- Anchor 4 — deception catalogue: used in paragraph P4 (the turn from audit to execution).

## Paragraph Job Map
- P1: Hook — the temple scene. Establish that the chapter is about the night before, not the morning of. Ask a live question about what a general is actually doing when they "plan".
- P2: Set the frame that war is a state-survival problem (load-bearing quote #1). Introduce the five constant factors as a system, not a list: social cohesion, time, space, individual judgment, organizational discipline.
- P3: Show the audit in operation. Walk through the seven comparative questions as a comparison ritual. Emphasize that the general is not asking "how good am I?" but "compared to the other side, where do I stand?". The ritual converts uncertainty into a forecast.
- P4: The turn. Introduce the chapter's sharpest move: once assessment is finished, execution is about managing what the opponent perceives. Use the deception catalogue (seeming weak, feigning disorder, attacking the unprepared) as a repertoire, not a slogan. Load-bearing quote #2 lands here.
- P5: Distinction (Deliver). The contradiction at the heart of the chapter: if assessment determines the outcome, why is deception required at all? The implicit answer — deception is how you prevent the opponent from running their own correct audit — is the book's real epistemic claim. Raise this without resolving it cleanly.
- P6: Bridge — return to the temple image. The general who wins makes many calculations; the general who loses makes few; the general who makes none has already lost. Load-bearing quote #3 lands here. End on a live question about Chapter 2's shift to tempo: once the calculation says "move", how fast?

## Required Distinction or Mechanism
The distinction at the analytical core of the chapter: **Sun Tzu's planning is a comparison ritual, not a production of insight in a vacuum.** It forecasts by placing two sides next to each other on five structured dimensions and running seven explicit questions. That makes the chapter fundamentally relational: you do not know if you can win until you have done the comparison, and the comparison is the plan.

## Hard-Depth Tension
The real tension that must appear in hard depth: if disciplined assessment determines the winner, then the best strategist should always win — but the chapter also says deception is the operational method, which is how a weaker side wins against a stronger one by corrupting the stronger side's assessment. Both claims are true in the chapter. Hard depth must hold both up without collapsing one into the other. The resolution is not "assessment + deception = win"; it is "assessment is how you know what to do; deception is how you prevent the other side from doing it first". That makes the chapter about epistemic competition, not tricks.

## Threshold Question
Exact question hard depth must leave alive: **does a disciplined assessment reward the better strategist, or the one who succeeds at making the better strategist's calculations wrong?**

## Takeaway Count Lock
- Easy: 3 (always)
- Medium: 6 (the chapter carries a multi-part framework: state-survival framing, five factors, seven questions, audit as comparison, deception as execution, temple image as synthesis)
- Hard: 6 (same structural count at greater depth)

## Hard Takeaway Topics
- Takeaway 1: Strategy as a state-survival problem, not a contest of bravery.
- Takeaway 2: The five constant factors as a system of social, environmental, human, and organizational variables (not a generic list).
- Takeaway 3: The seven comparative questions turn planning into a relational audit rather than a solo production.
- Takeaway 4: Deception is the operational method for protecting a correct assessment from being corrected by the opponent.
- Takeaway 5: The temple-calculations image binds the two halves — the disciplined auditor and the operational trickster are the same person, in sequence.
- Takeaway 6 (hard only): The epistemic contradiction — assessment rewards the better strategist, but deception rewards the one who corrupts the other side's assessment first.

## Scenario Lesson Map
- Scenario 1 (decision_point, work) — Lena prepares a product-launch pre-mortem under pressure from a VP who wants hype. Teaches: how to convert enthusiasm into a structured comparison before committing budget.
- Scenario 2 (dialogue, school) — Ravi at a history seminar hears a confident classmate assert a claim Ravi's research contradicts; he chooses how to reveal what he knows. Teaches: audit-before-speaking as a form of seven-question discipline in intellectual contests.
- Scenario 3 (dilemma, personal) — Aisha planning a cross-country move and deciding how to compare her city options under family pressure. Teaches: the five factors as a personal life decision framework without importing battlefield paranoia.
- Scenario 4 (inner_monologue, work) — Marcus, an operations director, reviewing a vendor contract late at night and realizing he has been assessing one side only. Teaches: the relational nature of the audit; one-sided planning is not planning.
- Scenario 5 (contrast, school) — Tomás (debate team captain) compares two prep approaches before a regional tournament. Teaches: the difference between "planning" as content review and "planning" as a structured opponent comparison.
- Scenario 6 (vignette, personal) — Devin rehearses a hard conversation with a sibling about shared responsibility for an aging parent. Teaches: when the tool is honesty, strategic assessment does not require deception, and importing it would wreck the relationship.

## Preview Constraint
The preview must set up Chapter 2's question about tempo — **once the audit says move, how fast must you finish?** — without telling the reader what Chapter 2 concludes. Keep it to a single live question the reader can hold in mind as they continue.

## Genericity Risks
- Risk 1: sliding into "planning is good" generic advice.
- Risk 2: turning the five factors into generic business-variable bingo.
- Risk 3: making the deception half of the chapter sound like a self-help permission slip for manipulation.
- Risk 4: opening the chapter with a thesis instead of a scene.
- Risk 5: letting modern examples become uniformly martial in vocabulary across all three tones.

## Forbidden Drift
- Filler move: "At its core, strategy is..."
- Filler move: "In today's fast-paced world..."
- Invented authority: any neuroscience of "fight-or-flight planning", "oxytocin-based leadership".
- Brief-language leak: "five constant factors as a system" (use a rewrite, do not transplant the phrase).
- Seed language leak: "comparison ritual" (use it in thinking, paraphrase in prose).
- Outline leak: "epistemic competition" (think it, do not write it).
