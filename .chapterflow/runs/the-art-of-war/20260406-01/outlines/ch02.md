# Chapter 2 Outline — Waging War

## Chapter Promise
By the end, the reader should understand that tempo is a structural constraint rather than a style preference, that time erodes the very factors Ch1's audit measured, and that Sun Tzu's forage-on-the-enemy doctrine is a logistics rule meant to protect the home state from its own campaign.

## Opening Move
Open inside the silver cost image. A treasurer in a palace counting out a thousand ounces of silver for one day of operations, the next day, the next, the accumulating weight of it. Not a thesis. A picture of an ongoing expense that does not stop.

## Anchor Allocation
- Anchor 1 — thousand-ounces-per-day cost: P1 (scene) and P2 (mechanism).
- Anchor 2 — compounding-cost chain (dulled weapons → damped morale → exhausted treasury → opportunistic rivals): P3.
- Anchor 3 — forage-on-the-enemy doctrine: P4.
- Anchor 4 — general as arbiter of the people's fate: P6 (closing).

## Paragraph Job Map
- P1: Hook — the palace treasurer, silver cost, daily expense that does not stop. Establish tempo as an ongoing cost rather than a one-time decision.
- P2: Build — explain why the thousand-ounces number is not about the number. It is about the fact that the expense runs for as many days as the campaign runs, which means every day the campaign continues is a day the state gets poorer.
- P3: The compounding-cost chain. Dulled weapons become damped morale. Damped morale becomes exhausted strength. Exhausted strength becomes a spent treasury. A spent treasury invites rivals who have been waiting to move. Each step makes the next step more likely. Load-bearing quote 1 lands here ("no country has ever benefited from prolonged warfare").
- P4: The operational answer — forage on the enemy. Keep the home state's supply lines short. Convert captured resources (chariots, supplies, prisoners) into new strength rather than hoarding or destroying them. Load-bearing quote 2 lands here ("bring war material with you from home, but forage on the enemy"). One cartload of the enemy's provisions is equivalent to twenty of one's own — the concrete numeric rule.
- P5: Distinction — the contradiction with the impulse toward stupid haste. Sun Tzu is not saying faster is always better. He is saying that once the audit has authorized action, the cost of delay compounds, and a commander who has not internalized that math will trade decisive finishing for the appearance of careful preparation. The difference between stupid haste and decisive finishing is the Ch1 audit — only the audited commander knows when to move and how long the window is.
- P6: Bridge — the governance reading. The closing line that the general is the arbiter of the people's fate. Connect the logistics discipline back to the state-survival framing from Ch1. Load-bearing quote 3 lands here ("in war, then, let your great object be victory, not lengthy campaigns"). End on a live question about what happens when the tempo constraint meets a situation where fighting itself is the wrong move, which is where Ch3 will take the argument.

## Required Distinction or Mechanism
The analytical distinction: **tempo is not a preference for speed. It is a recognition that the resources the campaign depends on are themselves degrading while the campaign runs.** This is subtly different from generic "faster is better" advice. Sun Tzu is naming a specific thermodynamic-feeling claim about war: the longer you take, the fewer resources you have, which means the standard of what counts as a successful finish keeps getting harder. The audit Ch1 produced is accurate on day 1, slightly less accurate on day 30, and useless by day 180.

## Hard-Depth Tension
The real tension hard depth must hold: **if speed is a fiscal and political duty to the state, is a slow campaign ever justifiable on ethical grounds?** Sun Tzu is writing at a scale where a prolonged war kills peasants through taxation, impoverishment, and conscription. The hard reading has to take that moral weight seriously and ask what happens when strategic patience (the thing a commander might defend as wisdom) is indistinguishable from letting the state die slowly. Hard depth should leave the threshold question alive without resolving it cleanly.

## Threshold Question
**When does strategic patience become a moral failure rather than a virtue?**

## Takeaway Count Lock
- Easy: 3 (always)
- Medium: 6 (thesis, compounding cost chain, forage doctrine, capture-and-convert rule, stupid-haste distinction, governance framing)
- Hard: 6 (same structure, greater depth; the sixth takeaway in hard carries the ethical threshold question)

## Hard Takeaway Topics
- Takeaway 1: Tempo is not a style choice. It is a recognition that time itself degrades the resources the campaign depends on.
- Takeaway 2: The compounding-cost chain. Dulled weapons → damped morale → spent treasury → opportunistic rivals. Each step makes the next more likely.
- Takeaway 3: The forage-on-the-enemy doctrine. Shorten the home state's supply lines by living off the territory the campaign moves through.
- Takeaway 4: Convert captured resources into strength rather than trophies. One cartload of the enemy's supplies is worth twenty from home because home supplies have already been paid for in full.
- Takeaway 5: The difference between stupid haste and decisive finishing. Haste without the Ch1 audit is just faster failure. Decisive finishing is haste inside a forecast that is still accurate.
- Takeaway 6: The governance framing. The general who drags out a campaign has accepted responsibility for the people whose fate is now determined by the length of the war. This is where tempo becomes an ethical question.

## Scenario Lesson Map
- Scenario 1 (dialogue, work) — Naomi, startup founder, having a hard conversation with her CFO about killing a long-running initiative. Teaches: tempo as fiscal discipline in resource-constrained contexts.
- Scenario 2 (decision_point, school) — Mei, AP physics student, deciding between a marathon study week and a shorter sharp pass. Teaches: tempo applied to cognitive resources; the compounding-cost chain of prolonged low-yield study.
- Scenario 3 (postmortem, personal) — Theo, looking back on a family lawsuit that ran for three years. Teaches: the general-as-arbiter framing for personal disputes that outlive their strategic value.
- Scenario 4 (dilemma, work) — Hector, plant manager, deciding whether to shut down a line for a costly repair or run it longer. Teaches: the compounding-cost chain applied to operational maintenance.
- Scenario 5 (before_after, school) — Nia, middle school group project coordinator, rebuilding her team's approach after a multi-week planning stall. Teaches: the stupid-haste vs decisive-finishing distinction at a collaborative level.
- Scenario 6 (predict_reveal, personal) — Rosa, planning a major home renovation, predicting what the scope will look like in month 6 if current decisions hold. Teaches: the forage-vs-bring-it-from-home doctrine applied to personal projects.

## Preview Constraint
The preview must set up Ch3's question: if finishing quickly is the job, what is the fastest version of finishing? Ch3 will argue that the best finish is not finishing by fighting at all. Hold that as a live question without telling the reader the answer.

## Genericity Risks
- Risk 1: sliding into "time management" self-help.
- Risk 2: flattening the foraging doctrine into generic "work smarter not harder".
- Risk 3: turning stupid haste vs decisive finishing into a motivational quote.
- Risk 4: letting the cost estimate become a spreadsheet demonstration instead of an emotional scene.
- Risk 5: having gentle/direct/competitive all default to work-setting martial metaphors.

## Forbidden Drift
- Filler moves: "Time is money", "In today's fast-paced world", "Hustle is the new smart".
- Invented authority: any "science of productivity" neuroscience.
- Brief-language leak: "compounding cost chain" (paraphrase, do not transplant).
- Seed language leak: "arbiter of the people's fate" verbatim except in the one approved quote location.
