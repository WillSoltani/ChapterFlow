# Chapter 3 Brief — Attack by Stratagem

Book: The Art of War / Author: Sunzi / Edition: Giles 1910 / Book ID: the-art-of-war
Chapter Number: 3 / Chapter Title: Attack by Stratagem
Source Sidecar Path: sidecars/source/ch03.source.txt

## Core Claim
The highest form of strategic achievement is to resolve a contest without requiring destructive battle at all. Sun Tzu argues this by ranking four methods of victory — breaking the enemy's plans, disrupting their alliances, engaging their army in the field, and besieging their cities — in declining order of quality and increasing order of cost. The best outcome preserves what you wanted to possess. The worst outcome, the siege, destroys it and costs most of your own force in the process. "Know the enemy and know yourself" closes the chapter as the epistemic prerequisite for everything above it: only a commander who has done the relational audit can rank and choose among the methods with any accuracy.

## What Makes This Chapter Distinct
No other chapter has a hierarchy of methods ranked by outcome quality rather than force type. Ch3 is the only place Sun Tzu explicitly argues that fighting well is not the highest form of generalship; fighting so well that fighting becomes unnecessary is. The chapter is also the source of the book's most famous single formula ("know the enemy and know yourself"). That formula here has a specific function: it is the diagnostic that enables a commander to choose correctly among the four methods. Without the relational knowledge the formula names, the hierarchy is unworkable in practice.

## Author Logic Chain
First Sun Tzu establishes that capturing intact beats destroying (at every scale from country to company).
Then he shows that winning without fighting beats winning by fighting.
Then he ranks methods: breaking enemy plans is highest, disrupting alliances second, field battle third, siege worst.
Then he details why siege is catastrophically costly (six months preparation, one-third losses, town often still untaken).
Then he introduces the civilian-interference disaster as an orthogonal failure mode.
Then he closes with the five essentials for victory and the know-yourself-and-the-enemy formula.

## Required Anchors
- **Capture-intact-beats-destroy hierarchy**: five scales (country, army, regiment, detachment, company). Load-bearing because it establishes the evaluative frame: preserving what you want to possess is strategically superior to shattering it.
- **Four-method hierarchy**: break plans > disrupt alliances > field battle > siege. Load-bearing because it is the chapter's analytical spine.
- **Siege as the worst example**: six months of siege preparation, one-third losses, town still untaken. The angry-general-launching-men-like-swarming-ants image. Load-bearing because it names the failure mode that the hierarchy is designed to avoid.
- **Civilian interference as orthogonal failure**: three ways a ruler ruins his army (commanding retreat/advance while ignorant of conditions, governing an army like a kingdom, employing officers without discrimination). Load-bearing because it adds a different axis: strategic method can be right but still fail if command authority is corrupted from above.
- **Know-yourself-and-the-enemy formula**: closing formula. Load-bearing because it completes the argument: the hierarchy is only usable by someone who has done the relational knowledge work.

## Frameworks or Terms Introduced
- **Capture-intact principle**: preserving what you want to possess is the success criterion.
- **Four-method hierarchy**: ranked by outcome quality and cost.
- **Civilian interference taxonomy**: three specific ways a ruler corrupts his own army's effectiveness.
- **Five essentials for victory**: knowing when to fight/not-fight, handling superior/inferior forces, unified army spirit, prepared-waits-for-unprepared, military capacity without sovereign interference.
- **Know-enemy-and-self formula**: the closing epistemic rule.

## Specific Applications (scenario design)
- Work: choosing to solve a competitive problem through superior positioning or market timing rather than head-to-head price war.
- Work: solving a management conflict at the level of structure or incentives rather than through escalation or punishment.
- School: winning a debate or competition by anticipating the opponent's argument structure and removing its foundations before the round starts.
- Personal: resolving a disagreement with a family member by removing the underlying source of friction rather than relitigating the surface argument.
- Personal/Work: the civilian-interference pattern in organizations where executives override field decisions without understanding operational constraints.

## Common Misreadings
- **"Sun Tzu says avoid conflict."** He says avoid destructive, unnecessary conflict. The force-ratio rules (§8-10) are clear that field battle is sometimes the right choice; the question is whether a less costly alternative exists.
- **"Breaking resistance without fighting means being passive."** Sun Tzu's top-ranked method (breaking the enemy's plans) requires sophisticated prior intelligence and active maneuvering — it is the most demanding approach, not the least.

## Counterarguments or Limits
- In cooperative domains, the whole hierarchy is misapplied. Fixing a relationship is not the same as breaking an opponent's resistance.
- "Know the enemy and know yourself" assumes information quality that may not be available. The formula is the goal; Chapter 13 (spies) is the operational mechanism for getting there.
- Civilian interference is a real constraint that strategic frameworks often underestimate. The chapter acknowledges it but does not fully solve it.

## Previous Chapter Bridge
Chapter 2 argued that the fastest finish is the best finish. Chapter 3 extends this: the fastest and cheapest finish may be one that requires no destructive battle at all.

## Next Chapter Bridge
Chapter 4 (Tactical Dispositions) will show that before any of these methods can be chosen, a commander must first make defeat impossible — which is the security prerequisite for the strategic flexibility Chapter 3 describes.

## Cross-Chapter Tensions
- Ch3's "win without fighting" ideal vs Ch2's "finish quickly": both are compatible when the no-fight option is available. The tension is when no-fight options are unavailable and the daily rate is running.
- Ch3's civilian-interference warning vs Ch10's "fight even if the ruler forbids it": both are about command integrity, one from the top down (civilian interference corrupts), one from the bottom up (field judgment should override political pressure when the situation clearly demands it).

## Moral Complexity
Required framing: "breaking resistance without fighting" sounds humane. The mechanism is often intimidation, misdirection, coalition disruption, and psychological pressure, not benevolence. Frame as strategic awareness (the best outcome for the state, preserved resources, fewer casualties) rather than as a moral position that implies gentleness. The chapter is consequentialist, not pacifist.

## Concept Budget
- target: 4
- rationale: (1) capture-intact principle, (2) four-method hierarchy, (3) civilian-interference orthogonal failure, (4) know-self-and-enemy epistemic prerequisite. Tight and closed.

## Hard-Depth Minimum
Hard must surface the contradiction: if breaking resistance without fighting is the highest form, and knowing yourself-and-the-enemy is the prerequisite, then a commander who lacks full relational knowledge cannot access the highest form — and must fall back to lower methods even though they cost more. Hard depth must leave the threshold question alive: **is the highest form always available, or is it conditional on having the relational knowledge that most commanders lack most of the time?**

## Unsupported Zones
- Do not invent named historical battles or generals from other books.
- Do not claim the "know yourself and the enemy" formula works in situations where information is structurally unavailable (e.g. modern covert operations, imperfect market information) as if Sun Tzu addressed those.
- Do not import the siege description as a metaphor for corporate bureaucracy without flagging the analogical stretch.
