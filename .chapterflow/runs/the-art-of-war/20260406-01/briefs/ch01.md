Book: The Art of War
Author: Sunzi (Sun Tzu, traditional attribution)

Edition / Translation: Project Gutenberg #17405, Lionel Giles 1910 (public domain)
Book ID: the-art-of-war

Chapter Number: 1
Chapter Title: Laying Plans
Position In Book: opening chapter; establishes the master logic of the whole text
Source Sidecar Path: sidecars/source/ch01.source.txt

## Core Claim
Strategic success begins before the first move, not during it. Sun Tzu argues that a disciplined pre-battle audit — five structural factors compared across yourself and the opponent, expressed as seven explicit questions — can forecast who wins and who loses. Execution then runs on deception, because once assessment is finished, the remaining job is to control the opponent's perception of your intent and capability. The chapter fuses two ideas the rest of the book will keep separate: the auditor's discipline and the trickster's misdirection.

## What Makes This Chapter Distinct
No other chapter turns strategy into an explicit questionnaire. Chapter 1 uniquely gives the reader a comparison framework — the five constant factors and seven comparative questions — and then pairs it with the book's strongest single-line statement of execution ("All warfare is based on deception"). The chapter's signature move is the transition from audit to misdirection inside one continuous argument. Every later chapter inherits from either the audit half or the deception half; Ch1 is the only place both halves are co-located.

## Author Logic Chain
First Sun Tzu establishes that war is a state-survival problem (not a side contest or a theater of bravery).
Then he shows that the survival calculation is knowable in advance through five constant factors (Moral Law, Heaven, Earth, Commander, Method and discipline) and seven comparative questions.
Then he concludes that after that assessment, execution belongs to deception — the rest of the book describes how.
The closing image of "calculations in the temple" re-binds the two halves: the disciplined auditor and the operational trickster are the same person, doing two things in sequence.

## Required Anchors
- **The five constant factors**: named list (Moral Law, Heaven, Earth, Commander, Method and discipline). Load-bearing because they are the chapter's audit schema.
- **The seven comparative questions**: the explicit questionnaire (which sovereign, which general, which side has Heaven and Earth, whose discipline, which army stronger, whose officers and men better trained, whose reward-and-punishment more constant). Load-bearing because they convert abstract planning into a concrete comparison ritual.
- **Temple calculations**: the image of the general making "many calculations in his temple ere the battle is fought" versus the loser who makes "but few calculations beforehand". Load-bearing because it states the planning thesis as an image, not just a maxim.
- **Deception list**: the concrete misdirection catalogue (seeming unable when able, inactive when using force, near when far, feigning disorder, irritating the choleric, pretending weakness, attacking the unprepared). Load-bearing because it shows execution is not abstract; it is a repertoire.

## Frameworks or Terms Introduced
- **Five constant factors**: Moral Law (people in complete accord with the ruler), Heaven (conditions of time and weather), Earth (distances and terrain), Commander (wisdom, sincerity, benevolence, courage, strictness), Method and discipline (organization, supply lines, expenditure).
- **Seven comparative questions**: the audit protocol.
- **Temple calculations**: metaphor for pre-battle estimation as a decision ritual.
- **Deception as normal operation**: once assessment is done, execution is about shaping the opponent's perception.

## Specific Applications (for scenario design — not for copying)
- Running a pre-mortem on a product launch by comparing leadership, market timing, team readiness, resources, and customer sentiment before committing budget.
- Planning an exam schedule by comparing course difficulty, personal energy, and competitor-student preparation before starting to study.
- Entering a negotiation with a deliberate plan about which information you reveal and which you hold back.
- Treating a career decision as a structured comparison rather than an emotional choice.
- For debate or moot court: scouting the opponent's structure early and deciding where misdirection or framing will matter.

## Common Misreadings
- **"Sun Tzu says lie to everyone."** The deception half of the chapter follows the audit half. Execution without assessment is incompetence, not strategy.
- **"The five factors are a generic list of business variables."** They are connected: Moral Law is social cohesion, Heaven is time, Earth is space, Commander is individual judgment, Method is organizational discipline. The list is a system, not a checklist.
- **"Planning means planning more."** Sun Tzu is not recommending more meetings. He is recommending a disciplined comparison ritual that converts uncertainty into a forecast.

## Counterarguments or Limits
- Clausewitzian objection: friction, chance, and the fog of war make assessment look more reliable in the temple than on the ground. Sun Tzu's confidence in pre-battle forecasting can look overconfident once fighting begins.
- Xunzi-style Confucian objection: building a system on deception corrodes trust and moral order. In cooperative domains (teams, families, long institutions), Sun Tzu's framework underestimates the cost of being seen as manipulative.
- Modern organizational objection: high-trust institutions often outperform paranoid ones long-term. Importing Sun Tzu into a domain where transparency and reciprocity are the higher-value strategies is a misapplication.
- Assessment depends on information quality. Ch1 does not say where the information comes from; Ch13 answers that question 12 chapters later. The gap is structural.

## Next Chapter Bridge
Chapter 2 (Waging War) shifts from abstract audit to the economics of campaigning: time is the enemy, prolonged wars destroy the state that waged them. The bridge is tempo: once you have done the temple calculations and decided to move, how quickly must you finish?

## Cross-Chapter Tensions
- **Audit vs action**: Ch1 says calculate before moving; Ch11 says "rapidity is the essence of war". Both true — the contradiction resolves at the boundary between planning and execution. Hard depth should surface this.
- **Deception vs honest intelligence**: Ch1 anchors the book in deception; Ch13 anchors it in foreknowledge through human sources. The book's internal counterweight is that deception only works when your own information is real.

## Moral Complexity
Required framing: present deception as strategic awareness, not endorsement. The chapter rewards disciplined assessment and uses deception as its operational method, but the framing should not slide into "lie more to get ahead". Modern readers should see deception here as shaping perception inside an adversarial contest that has already been correctly diagnosed, not as a universal style.

## Concept Budget
- target concept count: 4
- rationale: The five factors collapse into "assessment schema" conceptually; the seven questions are the same concept operationalized. That leaves four real concepts: (1) state-survival framing, (2) the audit schema, (3) deception as operational method, (4) the temple-calculations synthesis.

## Hard-Depth Minimum
The hard depth must preserve the contradiction that Sun Tzu himself tries to finesse: **if assessment determines the outcome, why is deception required at all?** The answer the chapter implies — that deception is how you prevent the opponent from performing their own correct assessment — is a real epistemic point, not a slogan. Hard depth must leave this threshold question alive: does disciplined planning reward the better strategist, or the one who succeeds at making the better strategist's calculations wrong?
