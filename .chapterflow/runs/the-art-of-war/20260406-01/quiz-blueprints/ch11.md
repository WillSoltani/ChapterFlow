## Core concepts under test

- concept A: Nine ground types as positional diagnostic — each type's rule follows from soldier psychology and supply logic at that position — §1-12
- concept B: Desperate ground rule — fight immediately; hesitation is more dangerous than action — §10, 12, 21-26
- concept C: Burning boats as a commitment device — irreversibility is the mechanism — §36-37
- concept D: Serpent principle — coordinated response is structural, not voluntary — §27-32
- concept E: Intelligence prerequisite — misclassification is worse than no framework — §39

**q01** — Bloom's: Remember | Depth: Easy | Concept: A (nine ground types and rules)

Topic: Identifying the correct behavioral rule for hemmed-in ground.

Prompt direction: Ask the reader what Sun Tzu prescribes when an army finds itself on hemmed-in ground — ground that can only be entered and exited through narrow passages, where a small enemy force can block a much larger one.

Correct answer: On hemmed-in ground, use stratagem. Because direct force cannot overcome the positional disadvantage of a narrow entry and exit, the correct response is indirect action, deception, or outmaneuvering the constraint rather than pushing against it.

Wrong answer 1 (tests misconception: hemmed-in ground calls for fighting, like desperate ground): Fight immediately, since the constriction of options is similar to desperate ground and the same urgency applies.
Wrong answer 2 (tests misconception: hemmed-in ground calls for retreating before the exit closes): Retreat as quickly as possible, since the narrow exit means the risk of being trapped is the primary danger.
Wrong answer 3 (tests misconception: hemmed-in ground calls for holding position until reinforcements arrive): Hold the position and consolidate, using the narrow entrance as a defensive barrier while waiting for support.

**q02** — Bloom's: Understand | Depth: Easy | Concept: B (desperate ground logic)

Topic: Understanding why Sun Tzu prescribes immediate fighting on desperate ground rather than hesitation or retreat.

Prompt direction: Ask what changes about a soldier's behavior when there is no available retreat — and why Sun Tzu says fighting without delay is the correct response on desperate ground rather than attempting to find an exit.

Correct answer: Soldiers who cannot retreat lose the calculation that makes them cautious. With no exit, their attention sharpens and resolve increases — they fight with a quality of commitment they would not bring to a situation where escape remained possible. Hesitation on desperate ground does not open new options; it only delays the action that the position actually requires.

Wrong answer 1 (tests misconception: desperate ground logic is about tactical speed, not psychology): Fighting immediately is correct because speed is always the essence of war; psychological transformation is not Sun Tzu's reasoning here.
Wrong answer 2 (tests misconception: desperate ground is a retreat trigger): Desperate ground signals that the army's position is untenable and that an orderly retreat, even under fire, is preferable to a fight with no strategic upside.
Wrong answer 3 (tests misconception: desperate ground logic only applies when the army is already resolved; a demoralized army should wait): If the army is demoralized, Sun Tzu would counsel holding position until morale recovers, since fighting without resolve produces poor outcomes regardless of the ground type.

**q03** — Bloom's: Apply | Depth: Medium | Concept: A + C (ground classification + commitment device)

Topic: Applying the nine-ground framework to a non-military situation where a team leader must decide whether to eliminate a fallback option.

Prompt direction: A startup founder has been running two product directions simultaneously, telling investors and team members that the company will pivot to whichever one gains traction first. The team's energy is diffuse; no single direction has reached critical commitment. The founder is considering shutting down the secondary product direction entirely to force focus. What does the nine-ground framework say about whether removing the fallback option is advisable — and what condition must be true for the move to generate resolve rather than panic?

Correct answer: Removing the fallback moves the team from dispersive or open ground toward serious or desperate ground. The move can generate resolve — but only if the primary direction has a genuine path to success. A commitment device that locks a force into a position with no viable path forward produces panic, not resolve. The framework requires the general to verify that the primary path is actually viable before burning the secondary one.

Wrong answer 1 (tests misconception: any removal of options increases focus and therefore resolve): Shutting down the secondary direction will always improve commitment because it removes the psychological option of partial success; the condition of the primary path is not relevant to whether the move generates resolve.
Wrong answer 2 (tests misconception: preserving both options is always strategically superior): Sun Tzu would preserve both directions because maintaining multiple paths forward is always safer than committing to one; the framework does not support eliminating fallbacks voluntarily.
Wrong answer 3 (tests misconception: commitment devices are only valid on desperate ground, not serious ground): The burning-boats commitment device is only appropriate when total destruction is the alternative — using it in a less urgent context like a product pivot misapplies the principle.

**q04** — Bloom's: Apply | Depth: Medium | Concept: D (serpent principle) | Named character: Chioma (executive director, nonprofit)

Topic: Applying the serpent principle to an organizational coordination failure.

Prompt direction: Chioma runs a nonprofit with three program teams and a communications office. During a fast-moving funding crisis, each team acted independently: the programs team reached out to funders directly, the communications office sent a different message to the press, and the finance team prepared a contingency that contradicted the public communication. The teams were all working hard and all cared about the outcome. The coordination failed because each team was responsive to its own part of the problem, not to the whole. Chioma must decide how to prevent this in the future. What does the serpent principle say about why the coordination failed and what the structural remedy is?

Correct answer: The coordination failed because the teams were not structurally connected to each other's responses. The serpent principle says that mutual response must be built into the architecture before the crisis arrives — not managed voluntarily during it. The remedy is a designed mutual support structure: named cross-team contacts, shared rapid-response protocols, and communication sequencing that makes each team's response contingent on what the others are doing.

Wrong answer 1 (tests misconception: coordination failure is a culture or motivation problem): The failure reflects a culture in which teams do not naturally prioritize organizational coherence over functional performance. The remedy is values training and leadership modeling, not structural design.
Wrong answer 2 (tests misconception: serpent coordination is achieved through centralized command during crises): The solution is for Chioma to be the central decision point in all future crises — routing every external message through her office before release — so that coordination is imposed from above rather than designed into the structure.
Wrong answer 3 (tests misconception: the serpent principle only applies to military-scale organizations with formal command hierarchies): The coordination architecture Sun Tzu describes is specific to armies; a small nonprofit's coordination problems are best solved through informal relationship-building rather than formal protocol design.

**q05** — Bloom's: Apply | Depth: Medium | Concept: B + C (desperate ground + commitment device) | Named character: Lars (PhD student, thesis defense)

Topic: Applying the desperate ground and commitment device logic to an academic context where deferring a fixed deadline reopens an exit.

Prompt direction: Lars is one week from his dissertation defense. His committee has offered a three-month deferral to strengthen a chapter on methodology. A fellow student advises taking the deferral because the work could always be stronger. Lars declines. He has noticed that the fixed defense date is producing a quality and consistency of work he has not maintained at any other point in the program. He suspects that accepting the deferral would reopen the exit path — converting his current position into something with more room for adjustment — and change the nature of his working state. Is Lars's reasoning consistent with how the nine-ground framework treats fixed positions?

Correct answer: Yes. The fixed defense date functions as a commitment device that places Lars on serious or desperate ground, where retreat is unlikely and the quality of engagement sharpens accordingly. Accepting the deferral would move him back to a position with an open exit, which historically has not produced the same quality of output. His reasoning correctly identifies that the position is generating the performance, and that changing the position changes the performance — not just the schedule.

Wrong answer 1 (tests misconception: a fixed deadline is only motivating if the person consciously decides it is): Lars's improved output reflects a personal motivational choice that would persist even with the deferral. The deadline is a surface cause; the actual driver is his internal decision to commit, which a deferral would not remove.
Wrong answer 2 (tests misconception: taking available time is always strategically superior because quality compounds): Accepting the deferral is the superior strategic choice because stronger work always produces better long-term outcomes, and the framework never recommends limiting the time available for preparation.
Wrong answer 3 (tests misconception: the desperate ground rule applies only when destruction is the alternative; a dissertation deferral is not desperate ground): The ground typology only applies when the stakes include genuine defeat. A dissertation deferral is a low-stakes professional decision and does not meet the conditions under which the framework's logic applies.

**q06** — Bloom's: Apply | Depth: Medium | Concept: E (intelligence prerequisite + misclassification) | Named character: Beatrix (small business owner, lease negotiation)

Topic: Applying the intelligence prerequisite to a situation where a negotiator misclassifies her own position.

Prompt direction: Beatrix entered a lease negotiation believing she had two alternative locations available, which she thought gave her open ground — free movement, no urgency to press. She made moderate concessions and accepted mid-range terms. Six months after signing, she learned that both alternative locations had been leased to other tenants three weeks before her negotiation concluded. She had been on hemmed-in ground the entire time. What does the nine-ground framework say is the structural source of her error — and what would have prevented it?

Correct answer: The error is a misclassification produced by an intelligence gap. Beatrix classified her position as open ground based on what she believed to be true (alternatives exist) rather than what she had verified (alternatives are actually available). The framework requires knowing the true state of the exit paths before the classification is made. The structural prevention is verification of position-defining facts — specifically, confirming that the alternatives she was relying on were genuinely available — before entering the negotiation.

Wrong answer 1 (tests misconception: the error is a negotiation skill failure, not an intelligence failure): Beatrix lost value because she lacked assertiveness or negotiation technique. Better skills would have secured better terms regardless of whether she correctly classified her positional situation.
Wrong answer 2 (tests misconception: misclassification is not a structural risk because any framework produces better outcomes than none): Even with a misclassification, using a framework like the nine-ground typology produces better outcomes than no framework, so the error is a minor calibration issue rather than a structural failure.
Wrong answer 3 (tests misconception: the framework only applies when the stakes are existential; a lease negotiation does not qualify): The nine-ground typology applies to military engagements with existential stakes. A lease negotiation involves commercial terms, not survival, and the misclassification consequence is not comparable.

**q07** — Bloom's: Analyze | Depth: Hard | Concept: F (commitment device prior conditions)

Topic: Analyzing the conditions the commitment device requires that the chapter does not make explicit.

Prompt direction: Sun Tzu describes burning boats and breaking cooking pots as a method for transforming a calculating army into a do-or-die one. But the chapter does not specify what conditions must be in place before the general can deploy this device effectively. What prior conditions does the commitment device actually require — and what happens when a leader uses it without having established them?

Correct answer: The commitment device requires two prior conditions: the general must have established genuine trust and field competence with the soldiers before the device is deployed, and the force must have a viable path to victory that fighting can actually unlock. A general who burns boats without prior trust does not generate resolve — he generates panic and the collapse of command authority. The irreversibility of the act means there is no recovery from deploying it in the wrong conditions. The chapter implies these prerequisites through its broader argument but never states them as conditions, which is the gap.

Wrong answer 1 (tests misconception: the commitment device works regardless of prior conditions because the psychology of no-exit is universal): The psychological effect of removing retreat options is universal — all soldiers respond to eliminated exits with increased resolve regardless of their relationship with the general or the viability of the path forward.
Wrong answer 2 (tests misconception: the prior condition is only timing — deploying the device before soldiers can resist it): The only condition that matters is deploying the commitment before soldiers have time to object. Once the boats are burned, the psychology shifts regardless of the relational history.
Wrong answer 3 (tests misconception: prior conditions are irrelevant because the chapter explicitly says to deploy the device on serious and desperate ground, not as a conditional recommendation): Sun Tzu gives the instruction without conditions deliberately — the nine-ground framework already provides the situational filter, and the general who applies it correctly has already met any necessary prior conditions by virtue of being in the right ground type.

**q08** — Bloom's: Analyze | Depth: Hard | Concept: D (serpent principle communication architecture)

Topic: Analyzing what the serpent principle requires structurally and where it fails in practice.

Prompt direction: The Shuai-jan serpent is Sun Tzu's model for organizational coordination: any part attacked, the rest responds as a unified whole. Two readers draw different conclusions about what the principle requires. Reader A says the serpent principle is primarily a culture and mindset principle — when team members genuinely understand that they are part of a single organism, they will respond appropriately to threat anywhere. Reader B says the serpent principle is an architecture principle — without designed communication protocols that connect each part to the others before the attack arrives, the mindset alone produces no reliable coordination under real stress. Which account is more consistent with the chapter's logic?

Correct answer: Reader B's account is more consistent. The chapter's example — enemies from Wu and Yueh cooperating instinctively during a storm in the same boat — is invoked to show that structural situation (same boat, shared danger) produces cooperation even between enemies. Mindset and culture require deliberate choice under pressure; architecture makes the response automatic. A force that relies on mindset alone for serpent coordination will find it degrades precisely when coordination matters most — when stress is highest and deliberate choice is hardest.

Wrong answer 1 (tests misconception: Reader A's culture account is more consistent because the chapter's example emphasizes shared purpose overcoming enmity): The Wu and Yueh example proves that shared purpose — not structure — is what drives coordination. The principle is primarily cultural; architecture can support it but is not the primary mechanism.
Wrong answer 2 (tests misconception: both accounts are equally valid because the chapter does not distinguish between structure and culture): Sun Tzu does not draw the distinction between culture and structure, so both interpretations are equally supported by the text. Neither is more consistent.
Wrong answer 3 (tests misconception: neither account is fully correct because the chapter intends the serpent principle only for armies with established unit hierarchy, not general organizations): The serpent principle applies specifically to military command structures and cannot be directly translated to organizations without formal command hierarchy. Both readers are over-extending the principle.

**q09** — Bloom's: Evaluate | Depth: Hard | Concept: G + Ch10 connection (ground-type transition)

Topic: Evaluating the ground-type stability problem and how it relates to Ch10's terrain categories.

Prompt direction: Chapter 10 classifies six types of terrain and gives rules for each. Those terrain types are physically stable during a given engagement — mountain is mountain, narrow pass is narrow pass. Chapter 11's nine ground types are positionally defined and change as the campaign progresses: facile ground becomes serious ground as the army advances deeper. Does this difference in stability create a problem the nine-ground framework does not resolve? And does a general who treats the nine ground types as stable categories — the way Ch10's terrain types are stable — make a structurally different kind of error?

Correct answer: Yes, a structural difference exists. Ch10's terrain categories are physically fixed and can be classified once at the start of an engagement. Ch11's ground types are dynamic — they are positions in a relationship between the army and the conflict, and they shift as the campaign evolves. A general who classifies the ground at entry and then stops re-classifying will find that the rule he applied correctly at the outset becomes wrong as the position transitions. The error is not a single misclassification; it is the failure to treat positional classification as a continuous task.

Wrong answer 1 (tests misconception: the nine ground types are as stable as terrain types once the army is committed to a campaign direction): Once a campaign begins, the ground type is determined by the initial conditions — the army entered on facile ground and its subsequent advance does not reclassify it as serious ground until the campaign formally enters a new phase. The classification is updated episodically, not continuously.
Wrong answer 2 (tests misconception: the transition problem proves the nine-ground framework is less reliable than Ch10's terrain framework): Because Ch11's ground types are unstable and Ch10's terrain types are stable, the terrain framework is the more operationally reliable guide during an active engagement. The nine-ground framework's value is primarily strategic and pre-campaign.
Wrong answer 3 (tests misconception: the two frameworks address the same problem at different scales and should not be compared structurally): Ch10 and Ch11 are entirely separate classification systems with no relationship; comparing their stability properties is a category error. Each chapter applies to a different aspect of command.

**q10** — Bloom's: Evaluate | Depth: Hard | Concept: H (recursive epistemological problem in intelligence prerequisite)

Topic: Evaluating the recursive intelligence problem — the general who cannot know what he does not know.

Prompt direction: The chapter states that a commander must know the intentions of neighboring states, the terrain, and how to use local guides before the nine-ground framework can be applied correctly. Two readers assess the severity of this intelligence requirement. Reader A says: the intelligence requirement is a high but achievable standard; a general who works hard at intelligence-gathering will meet it well enough. Reader B says: the intelligence requirement contains a recursive problem — a general who does not know the intelligence he is missing cannot know which classifications he has gotten wrong, and the framework generates confidence from correct rule application that is indistinguishable from the confidence produced by correctly applying a rule to a misclassified situation. Which assessment identifies the deeper structural problem?

Correct answer: Reader B identifies the deeper problem. The framework's value is its precision, but that precision generates confidence regardless of whether the underlying classification is accurate. A general who has applied the wrong rule to the wrong ground type has done so with the confidence of correct procedure. He cannot distinguish his error from correct performance through internal experience alone — both feel like correct application of the framework. Reader A's account describes a resolvable operational challenge; Reader B's describes a structural limit of the framework itself.

Wrong answer 1 (tests misconception: Reader A's account is more useful because it points toward a practical improvement path): Reader A is more consistent with Sun Tzu's overall approach, which emphasizes practical mastery rather than philosophical skepticism. Reader B's concern is theoretically interesting but produces paralysis rather than better practice.
Wrong answer 2 (tests misconception: the two readers are describing the same problem at different levels of resolution): Both readers are pointing at the same intelligence gap; Reader B's framing adds rhetorical weight but does not identify a structurally different problem. The practical implication is the same: gather better intelligence.
Wrong answer 3 (tests misconception: the recursive problem is solved by using multiple intelligence sources): If the general uses multiple independent intelligence sources, the recursive problem is resolved because the sources will cross-check each other's blind spots. Reader A's approach, applied rigorously, eliminates the concern Reader B raises.

correctIndex: 1
