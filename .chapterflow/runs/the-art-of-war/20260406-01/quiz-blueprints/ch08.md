## Questions

**q01** — remember / easy
Topic: The nine variations — what they are and what they name.
Concept tested: Basic recall of the five enumerated exception categories (roads, armies, towns, positions, sovereign orders).
Wrong-answer misconceptions to test:
- (A) The nine variations are nine battle formations to be memorized.
- (B) The nine variations describe nine types of terrain (confusing with Ch10).
- (C) The nine variations are a list of tactical attacks, one per enemy size class.
Correct answer direction: The variations are categories of situations in which standard rules are suspended — not formations, not terrain types, not attack modes.

**q02** — understand / easy
Topic: What it means that a general who does not know the variations "cannot use the five advantages."
Concept tested: The relationship between variation-recognition and payoff — the advantages are unreachable without the recognitional capacity.
Wrong-answer misconceptions to test:
- (A) A general unfamiliar with the variations will simply be slower to act.
- (B) Knowing the terrain compensates for not knowing the variations.
- (C) The five advantages are a separate checklist; they don't depend on variation-recognition.
Correct answer direction: Variation-recognition is the precondition for using the five advantages — terrain knowledge without it is incomplete; the general runs the wrong computation on correct data.

**q03** — apply / medium
Topic: Dual consideration — holding advantage and disadvantage simultaneously.
Concept tested: Application of the two-sided audit to a non-military setting.
Scenario: A team identifies a fast-mover opportunity that no competitor has spotted yet. What does dual consideration require them to do before committing?
Wrong-answer misconceptions to test:
- (A) They should move immediately before doubting themselves (dual consideration only applies after commitment).
- (B) They should list every possible risk before acting (confusing dual consideration with comprehensive risk analysis).
- (C) They should split the team — half optimize for the opportunity, half plan for failure.
Correct answer direction: Dual consideration is a specific cognitive discipline — while holding the advantage clearly, ask what is difficult or dangerous about this exact advantage; while noting the difficulty, ask what opening it conceals. It is not comprehensive risk analysis and not a splitting of attention.

**q04** — apply / medium (named character: Rashida, startup ops director)
Topic: The five faults — identifying which fault is active in a specific situation.
Concept tested: Matching a character's behavior pattern to the correct fault and its exploit.
Scenario: Rashida's startup is facing a hostile negotiation from a larger acquirer. The acquirer's rep makes a pointed comment about Rashida's team's inexperience in front of the whole room. Rashida immediately escalates the terms and walks away from a deal she had been prepared to accept. Her co-founder later tells her the comment was deliberate provocation.
Question: Which of Sun Tzu's five faults was activated — and how did the situation (not just the opponent) create the leverage?
Wrong-answer misconceptions to test:
- (A) Rashida showed recklessness — she acted without calculating consequences (confusing recklessness with reactive anger).
- (B) Rashida showed over-solicitude — she was too worried about her team's reputation.
- (C) Rashida showed cowardice — she retreated from a good deal.
Correct answer direction: Hasty temper — susceptibility to provocation by insults. The exploit was deliberate; the opponent applied force at exactly the fault's leverage point. The situation (public setting, comparative claim about the team) maximized the fault's activation.

**q05** — apply / medium (named character: Finn, university department chair)
Topic: Sovereign orders not to be obeyed — when local situational knowledge overrides chain-of-command.
Concept tested: Distinguishing principled situational override from the recklessness or delicacy-of-honor faults.
Scenario: Finn's dean has directed all department chairs to freeze discretionary hiring for the semester. Finn has just been offered a visiting scholar — a leading researcher in the department's core area — available only this semester at an unusually low cost. Finn believes the freeze was issued without knowledge of this specific opportunity.
Question: What does Ch8's framework say about the conditions under which Finn's override would be justified — and what would make the same override a fault?
Wrong-answer misconceptions to test:
- (A) The dean's order is absolute; situational knowledge never justifies override in a hierarchy.
- (B) Finn should override because the opportunity is objectively good and good opportunities always override rules.
- (C) Finn should follow the rule because breaking it would damage his relationships with the dean (importing over-solicitude as the reason for compliance).
Correct answer direction: The override is justified when Finn has genuine situational intelligence the dean lacked when issuing the order, and when the order was not issued for this type of situation. The override becomes a fault if it is driven by recklessness (acting before verifying the opportunity's quality), delicacy of honor (ego about being corrected), or hasty temper (frustration with the freeze policy). The chapter's test is whether the override is grounded in superior local knowledge, not in character pressure.

**q06** — apply / medium (named character: Yara, independent consultant)
Topic: The five faults — over-solicitude and its activation by situational pressure without deliberate enemy action.
Concept tested: The relational vs. dispositional nature of the faults — situational pressure can activate a fault without a deliberate opponent probe.
Scenario: Yara is advising a family business on a restructuring. The founding family members are visibly distressed during the engagement. Yara finds herself softening her recommendations — removing the most disruptive changes — not because the analysis has changed, but because she does not want to add to the family's visible distress. The engagement closes with an incomplete restructuring that fails to address the core problem.
Question: Which fault is active — and what is notable about how it was activated?
Wrong-answer misconceptions to test:
- (A) Yara showed cowardice — she was afraid to deliver hard news.
- (B) Yara showed recklessness — she moved forward with an incomplete recommendation.
- (C) The fault only counts if an opponent deliberately exploited it; Yara's situation involved no adversary.
Correct answer direction: Over-solicitude — excessive concern for the wellbeing of those in her care, making her susceptible to worry and to pulling punches. Critically, no deliberate manipulation occurred: the family's distress was real and not staged. The situation type (high emotional salience, visible suffering, close advisory relationship) activated the fault without anyone applying deliberate pressure. This is the chapter's deeper point about the faults being situation-resonant, not only opponent-exploited.

**q07** — analyze / hard
Topic: The ambiguity between principled exception-recognition and fault-driven override.
Concept tested: The structural gap in Ch8 — the framework that justifies deviation is identical in surface form to several of the five faults.
Question: Sun Tzu says some sovereign orders should not be obeyed, and some roads should not be followed. He also says recklessness and delicacy of honor are dangerous faults. How does a general — or anyone — distinguish in real time between a justified situational exception and a fault-driven deviation that feels like an exception?
Wrong-answer misconceptions to test:
- (A) Justified exceptions always feel uncomfortable; faults feel comfortable — discomfort is the signal.
- (B) If the outcome is good, it was an exception; if bad, it was a fault — results determine the category.
- (C) The chapter resolves this by requiring the general to check against the five constant factors before deviating.
Correct answer direction: The chapter does not resolve this ambiguity cleanly — this is the structural incompleteness in the framework. The reader should name the gap: an override grounded in genuine situational intelligence that the rule-issuer lacked is an exception; an override grounded in character pressure (ego, anger, fear, excessive concern) is a fault. But from the inside, in real time, both can feel like situational mastery. The chapter's prescription — know the variations — cannot, by itself, prevent the fault-ridden general from misidentifying their own state.

**q08** — analyze / hard
Topic: The relationship between Ch7's prohibitions and Ch8's generalized exception logic.
Concept tested: How Ch7's specific do-not rules are a local instance of Ch8's general framework.
Question: Ch7 closed with a list of prohibitions: do not pursue simulated flight, do not attack keen temper, do not press a desperate foe. Ch8 opens with a different set of exceptions: roads not to be followed, armies not to be attacked. What is the structural relationship between these two sets of rules — and what problem does Ch8 reveal that Ch7 leaves unresolved?
Wrong-answer misconceptions to test:
- (A) Ch8 contradicts Ch7 — Ch7 says attack is often wrong, but Ch8 says the road must sometimes be taken.
- (B) The two chapters are about different subjects (movement vs. character); they don't bear on each other.
- (C) Ch8's exceptions replace Ch7's prohibitions with a more complete list.
Correct answer direction: Ch7's prohibitions are themselves exceptions to the normal attack/pursuit impulse — they are local instances of the variation logic. Ch8 generalizes: there is no fixed rule that covers all situations, and the common-case rule is precisely what an intelligent opponent exploits. The problem Ch7 left open: its prohibitions name the traps but do not say how a commander recognizes when they are active. Ch8 names that recognition capacity as the core competence — and reveals it cannot itself be reduced to another rule.

**q09** — evaluate / hard
Topic: The dual-consideration discipline as a planning-phase vs. execution-phase tool.
Concept tested: Evaluating the limits of dual consideration — when importing it into execution produces the cowardice fault.
Question: Sun Tzu prescribes holding advantage and disadvantage simultaneously. A general preparing an attack uses dual consideration during planning and identifies a serious risk — but then continues dual-considering during the execution phase, hesitating at each step. Is this what the chapter recommends? What has gone wrong?
Wrong-answer misconceptions to test:
- (A) Nothing has gone wrong — more consideration is always better than less.
- (B) The general should stop dual-considering and commit; the chapter recommends decisiveness over analysis.
- (C) The general should delegate the hesitation problem to a subordinate.
Correct answer direction: Dual consideration is a planning-phase discipline. Importing it into execution produces hesitation, which the chapter classifies under cowardice (or hasty-temper's inverse — sluggishness under pressure). The chapter's five faults operate at the execution phase; dual consideration operates at the assessment phase. The general who cannot switch phases has imported a planning tool into an execution context, which collapses into a fault. Sun Tzu's framework is phase-sensitive in a way the dual-consideration maxim alone does not make explicit.

**q10** — evaluate / hard
Topic: Evaluating the five-faults framework as a complete account of general failure.
Concept tested: Assessing whether the five faults exhaust the failure modes, and what Ch9 (Army on the March) must address.
Question: The five faults are framed as character defects that enemy pressure can exploit. Given what Ch8 has established about variation-recognition and dual consideration, is character the right level of analysis for why generals fail — or does the chapter point toward a different kind of failure that the fault list does not capture?
Wrong-answer misconceptions to test:
- (A) The five faults are exhaustive — every general failure reduces to one of the five.
- (B) The faults are irrelevant to modern contexts where organizational roles replace individual character.
- (C) The real failure is poor terrain knowledge — fixing information solves the fault problem.
Correct answer direction: The five faults capture character-driven failure under pressure. But Ch8 also describes a separate failure mode: the variation-blind general — one with no character faults who simply cannot recognize which situation they are in. This is a recognitional failure, not a character failure. Ch9 (Army on the March) likely addresses the observation discipline that feeds recognitional competence — reading terrain signs, enemy behavior, environmental signals. The fault list and the variation framework point at two different levels of failure; the chapter holds both without fully integrating them.
