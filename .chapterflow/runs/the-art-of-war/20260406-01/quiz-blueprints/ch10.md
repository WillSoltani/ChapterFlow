**q01** — Bloom's: Remember | Depth: Easy | Concept: A (terrain type vocabulary)

Topic: Identifying what Sun Tzu calls temporizing ground and the decision rule it carries.

Prompt direction: Ask the reader what Sun Tzu means by temporizing ground and what the correct action is when you are on it.

Correct answer: Temporizing ground is a position where neither side gains from moving first. The correct action is not to advance even if the enemy offers an attractive target; instead, withdraw to entice the enemy out, then strike when part of his force has come forward.

Wrong answer 1 (tests misconception: temporizing means occupying and holding): Temporizing ground is a position you should occupy and hold strongly, garrisoning it against the enemy's approach.
Wrong answer 2 (tests misconception: temporizing ground requires direct engagement when the enemy baits): When the enemy offers an attractive target on temporizing ground, you should advance and take the offered advantage before the enemy can consolidate.
Wrong answer 3 (tests misconception: temporizing means the same as inaccessible): Temporizing ground is ground that neither side can occupy or traverse; both sides must find an alternate route.

**q02** — Bloom's: Understand | Depth: Easy | Concept: C + E (knowing vs. using; humaneness-discipline pairing)

Topic: Understanding what Sun Tzu means when he says a general who is "indulgent but unable to make his authority felt" has soldiers like spoilt children.

Prompt direction: Ask why Sun Tzu says that caring for soldiers without being able to enforce commands produces an army that is "useless for any practical purpose."

Correct answer: Humaneness without discipline removes the general's ability to direct the army under stress. Soldiers who have been treated warmly but face no consistent enforcement will not follow orders when following is costly. Care and authority are both required; care without authority produces attachment that does not translate into reliable action.

Wrong answer 1 (tests misconception: the problem is that soldiers become too emotionally dependent): The issue is that excessive care makes soldiers emotionally fragile — they cannot function under the hardship of battle because they have been protected too much.
Wrong answer 2 (tests misconception: indulgence is the opposite of discipline and they cannot coexist): Sun Tzu is saying a commander must choose between being caring and being disciplined; the chapter argues for discipline over humaneness when the two conflict.
Wrong answer 3 (tests misconception: the problem is about fairness rather than authority): Soldiers treated indulgently come to expect favored treatment and resist any standard they perceive as unfair, which is why discipline becomes impossible.

**q03** — Bloom's: Apply | Depth: Medium | Concept: B (six calamities, command failures)

Topic: Applying the calamity taxonomy to a non-military organizational situation.

Prompt direction: Ask the reader to apply the calamity of ruin to an organization. Sun Tzu defines ruin as what results when higher officers are angry and insubordinate and engage the enemy on their own account before the commander-in-chief is ready. A marketing director at a company publicly commits to a major partnership in a press release without executive approval, acting on her own judgment that the opportunity is too good to pass up. What calamity does this map onto, and what structural failure does it reveal?

Correct answer: Ruin. The calamity is not that the decision was wrong — it may have been a good partnership — but that the marketing director acted from her own account before the organization was positioned to support the commitment. The structural failure is that the command relationship allowed a senior officer to commit the organization independently, which may force the executive to fight in a position he did not choose and is not prepared for.

Wrong answer 1 (tests misconception: this is disorganization, not ruin): This maps to disorganization — the executive's authority is weak and his orders are unclear, which is why the marketing director felt she could act unilaterally.
Wrong answer 2 (tests misconception: this is not a calamity, it is a judgment call): Sun Tzu's calamities apply only to military engagements; organizational situations involving good intentions and senior judgment do not fall under this analysis.
Wrong answer 3 (tests misconception: the calamity is rout because the organization cannot estimate the partner's strength): The situation maps to rout, because the company committed to the partnership without accurately evaluating whether it had the internal capacity to fulfill the terms.

**q04** — Bloom's: Apply | Depth: Medium | Concept: A + C | Named character: Preethi (head of product, B2B software company)

Topic: Applying the terrain classification framework to a product decision where the type of ground determines the available moves.

Prompt direction: Preethi is evaluating whether to build a deep integration with a single enterprise customer's proprietary internal system. The customer has offered to co-fund development and provide access to data. The integration would be technically complex but achievable in six months. However, the resulting product would be tightly coupled to that customer's architecture and nearly impossible to generalize to other customers without significant rebuilding. Preethi has to decide whether to proceed. What terrain type does this situation most closely resemble, and what does the corresponding decision rule say?

Correct answer: Entangling terrain. The situation is easy to enter — the customer is offering favorable terms and co-funding — but hard to exit once the deep integration is built. The decision rule for entangling terrain is to advance only if the enemy is unprepared: here, only if there is a strong reason to believe the integration will produce durable value that cannot be reversed by the customer withdrawing. If that condition is uncertain, the favorable entry terms should not override the irreversibility of the commitment.

Wrong answer 1 (tests misconception: this is accessible terrain because both sides can benefit): The terrain is accessible because both Preethi's company and the customer can move freely — the customer can adopt the integration and Preethi's company can build it. Accessible terrain favors the first mover, so Preethi should proceed quickly.
Wrong answer 2 (tests misconception: this is temporizing terrain because neither side should move first without more information): The situation is temporizing ground — neither party benefits from moving first until more due diligence is complete. Preethi should withdraw temporarily, gather more data, and re-engage when the conditions are clearer.
Wrong answer 3 (tests misconception: terrain classification does not apply to product strategy): The six terrain types are military concepts that do not translate to product management decisions, which require a different framework focused on market opportunity and technical feasibility.

**q05** — Bloom's: Apply | Depth: Medium | Concept: D (general's duty vs. sovereign's command) | Named character: Olu (middle school vice principal)

Topic: Applying the positional knowledge principle to a situation where the person with field knowledge must decide whether to act against the explicit instruction of a superior.

Prompt direction: Olu's principal has directed that all parent communication about a student disciplinary matter go through the principal's office only, and that Olu not contact parents directly. Olu then receives information from a classroom teacher that the same student has been making statements suggesting she may be at risk of self-harm. The principal is traveling and unreachable for four hours. Olu has to decide whether to follow the standing instruction or contact the parent directly. What does the chapter's principle about the general's duty say about Olu's decision?

Correct answer: Olu's positional knowledge — the specific, time-sensitive fact on the ground — is something the principal cannot have from a distance. The standing instruction was issued without knowledge of this condition. Following it literally in these circumstances would not serve the purpose the instruction was designed to serve. The chapter's principle licenses acting on field conditions that the superior cannot observe, provided the divergence is genuinely factual and field-bound, not a preference about how to handle the matter generally.

Wrong answer 1 (tests misconception: following the explicit instruction is always correct unless given a direct counter-order): The standing instruction takes precedence. Olu should continue attempting to reach the principal and document the attempt, rather than take independent action that the principal explicitly prohibited.
Wrong answer 2 (tests misconception: the principle only applies when the superior is permanently unavailable, not temporarily): The chapter's override principle requires that the superior be genuinely unable to exercise command, not merely temporarily unavailable. A four-hour delay does not constitute the kind of absence that licenses independent action.
Wrong answer 3 (tests misconception: Olu should defer to the school counselor as a way of acting without technically violating the instruction): The chapter's principle does not endorse routing around authority through intermediaries. If the action is warranted, Olu should take it directly and account for it later, rather than obscure the decision-making through delegation.

**q06** — Bloom's: Apply | Depth: Medium | Concept: E (humaneness and discipline paired requirement) | Named character: Sven (architect, small design firm)

Topic: Applying the humaneness-discipline pairing to a professional relationship where only one of the two elements is present.

Prompt direction: Sven manages a small team of three junior architects. He is genuinely invested in their development — he reviews their work carefully, explains his reasoning, checks in on their workload, and adjusts project assignments when someone is under pressure. But when a junior architect submits work with recurring errors Sven has addressed before, Sven lets it pass rather than having a direct conversation about the pattern. He tells himself he does not want to damage the trust he has built. Over six months, the errors continue. A client raises a concern. What does the chapter's paired requirement say about Sven's approach?

Correct answer: Sven has the care in place but has withheld the discipline that would make the care functional. The chapter is explicit: indulgence without authority produces an army that is useless for practical purposes. Sven's reluctance to enforce the standard is not an extension of his care — it is the failure to complete the paired requirement. The trust he has built is exactly what makes enforcement possible without destroying the relationship, but only if he uses it.

Wrong answer 1 (tests misconception: Sven's approach is correct because trust comes before standards): Sven is following the correct sequence — humaneness first — and should continue building trust before introducing performance expectations. The client concern is a signal to increase support, not to introduce enforcement.
Wrong answer 2 (tests misconception: the problem is that Sven's standards are too high for junior architects): The recurring errors suggest Sven's expectations are misaligned with the juniors' current level. The fix is recalibrating standards, not introducing more enforcement.
Wrong answer 3 (tests misconception: the chapter's pairing means finding a balance between the two, not using both fully): The chapter prescribes a point of balance between humaneness and discipline — Sven has moved too far toward care and should rebalance by reducing personal investment and becoming more task-focused.

**q07** — Bloom's: Analyze | Depth: Hard | Concept: G (classification meta-problem)

Topic: Analyzing what makes the terrain classification problem harder than knowing the decision rules.

Prompt direction: Sun Tzu provides a decision rule for each of the six terrain types. A commander who knows all six rules still faces a prior problem: correctly identifying which type of terrain they are on. Analyze the source of difficulty in that classification step, and what the consequences are when a commander misclassifies the terrain.

Correct answer: The classification problem is harder than the rule problem because the six categories are cleanly distinct in the text but frequently ambiguous in practice. A position that combines features of entangling and temporizing terrain requires identifying which conditions are actually dominant before any rule applies. Misclassification produces worse outcomes than having no rule: a commander who treats entangling terrain as accessible commits to a position he cannot exit; one who treats accessible terrain as temporizing leaves a first-mover advantage unclaimed. The rules are conditional on correct classification, and the classification requires field experience that the rules themselves do not provide.

Wrong answer 1 (tests misconception: the rules are self-applying once the terrain is observed): The six terrain types map directly to observable physical features — a narrow defile is a narrow pass, steep hills are precipitous heights — so classification is straightforward for any experienced observer. The difficulty is in correctly executing the rule, not in identifying the terrain type.
Wrong answer 2 (tests misconception: misclassification is less dangerous than wrong rule application): Misclassifying the terrain is a less serious error than applying the wrong decision rule to correctly classified terrain, because terrain types are visible and the classification can be corrected before the decision is made.
Wrong answer 3 (tests misconception: the classification problem is solved by more thorough reconnaissance): Sufficient reconnaissance always resolves terrain ambiguity. The classification problem is not a structural feature of the six-type schema but a temporary information deficit that additional scouting eliminates.

**q08** — Bloom's: Analyze | Depth: Hard | Concept: D (recursive problem in override authority)

Topic: Analyzing the recursive problem in the general's authority to override the sovereign's command.

Prompt direction: Sun Tzu says the general must fight if victory is certain even if the ruler forbids it, and must not fight if victory is uncertain even if the ruler commands it. The justification is that the general has positional knowledge the ruler lacks. Analyze the recursive problem this creates: if the general has override authority when the ruler is ignorant of field conditions, who determines when the ruler is ignorant?

Correct answer: The general cannot self-certify the condition that licenses the override, because any general who disagrees with a command can claim field conditions justify divergence. The principle is structurally legitimate only when the divergence is about factual conditions that the ruler literally cannot observe at a distance — force ratios, terrain features, enemy disposition at a specific moment. It does not license override based on strategic disagreement, political judgment, or general preference for a different approach. The boundary is the factual-versus-preference distinction: field facts are observable from the field; strategic preferences are available to both parties, and the sovereign retains authority over them.

Wrong answer 1 (tests misconception: the principle licenses any judgment the general believes is superior): Sun Tzu's point is that the general's judgment is superior to the ruler's in all military matters, because the general is the professional. The override authority is therefore broad and applies to strategic as well as tactical disagreements.
Wrong answer 2 (tests misconception: the recursive problem dissolves because results determine who was right): The circular problem is resolved by outcomes: if the general's override produced victory, the override was legitimate; if it produced defeat, it was not. Sun Tzu's text implies that legitimacy is determined retrospectively by success.
Wrong answer 3 (tests misconception: the principle requires prior formal agreement between the ruler and the general about the scope of override authority): The chapter's override principle only applies when the ruler and the general have agreed in advance on the conditions that license independent command decisions, making it a contractual rather than a positional authority.

**q09** — Bloom's: Evaluate | Depth: Hard | Concept: C + Ch9 connection

Topic: Evaluating the relationship between Ch10's terrain-classification framework and Ch9's observational method.

Prompt direction: Ch9 argues that field competence is fundamentally observational: judgment is built from accumulated direct observation, not from applying rules. Ch10 provides six terrain types with specific decision rules. Do these two claims support each other or create tension? Can a commander who has mastered Ch10's classification rules still lack what Ch9 says is the foundational competence?

Correct answer: They create tension as well as mutual support. Ch10's terrain rules provide the categories for what to look for, which is the map Ch9 says you need before observational accuracy is possible. But Ch9's claim is that accurate classification of terrain requires the kind of perceptual attunement that only direct field experience provides — you cannot correctly classify entangling from accessible terrain by knowing the definitions alone. A commander who has mastered Ch10's rules without Ch9's observational foundation may apply the correct rule to a misclassified situation. The two chapters operate in sequence: Ch10 gives the schema; Ch9 builds the perceptual accuracy to apply it correctly.

Wrong answer 1 (tests misconception: Ch10's rules are sufficient and Ch9's observational method is additive but not necessary): A commander who has memorized Ch10's six terrain types and their decision rules is fully equipped to classify terrain correctly, because the categories are defined by observable features. Ch9's observational discipline is a supplement that accelerates learning but is not required for applying the rules.
Wrong answer 2 (tests misconception: Ch9 and Ch10 address different problems with no meaningful relationship): Ch9 is about reading the battlefield in motion; Ch10 is about classifying terrain before battle. They address different timescales and different problems, so the tension between them is more apparent than real.
Wrong answer 3 (tests misconception: Ch10 supersedes Ch9 because it provides explicit rules where Ch9 only provides method): Ch10's explicit classification schema replaces the more ambiguous observational method of Ch9 for terrain-related decisions. Rules are more reliable than accumulated observation because they are transferable and do not depend on individual experience.

**q10** — Bloom's: Evaluate | Depth: Hard | Concept: E + cross-chapter (Ch10 to prior chapters)

Topic: Evaluating which account of the humaneness-discipline relationship is more consistent with Ch10's own framing, and what that implies about how to read the calamities catalogue.

Prompt direction: The chapter says caring for soldiers like children makes them follow into the deepest valleys, but indulgence without authority makes them useless. Two readers interpret this differently. Reader A says: the chapter is prescribing a balance — be warm enough to earn loyalty, strict enough to maintain order. Reader B says: the chapter is prescribing a sequence and a paired requirement — care is the continuous foundation, and enforcement operates within it, not instead of it. Evaluate which reading is more consistent with the chapter's calamities catalogue, and what that consistency implies.

Correct answer: Reader B is more consistent. The calamities catalogue does not describe commanders who failed to find the right balance point; it describes commanders whose command structures were structurally broken in specific ways. Ruin is not "too much" or "too little" of either quality — it is senior officers acting outside the command relationship. Disorganization is not a balance failure — it is a structural absence of clear authority, defined roles, and consistent enforcement. The calamities are structural diagnoses, not calibration problems. Reader B's reading — care as continuous foundation, enforcement within it — is consistent with the catalogue's logic: the problem is always in the structure of the command relationship, not in the amount of warmth or severity applied.

Wrong answer 1 (tests misconception: Reader A's balance account is more consistent with the calamities): Reader A is more consistent because each of the six calamities describes a commander who is too strong or too weak in one dimension — officers too strong, soldiers too weak; or the reverse — which implies the problem is a balance failure that the right calibration would correct.
Wrong answer 2 (tests misconception: the chapter does not prescribe a sequence because it is silent on the order of care and enforcement): The chapter does not specify a temporal sequence between humaneness and discipline; it requires both simultaneously. Reader A and Reader B are both reading a sequencing claim into a text that only specifies that both are required.
Wrong answer 3 (tests misconception: the calamities catalogue is unrelated to the humaneness-discipline discussion): The calamities catalogue and the humaneness-discipline passages address different problems — the first is about force composition and command structure, the second is about the commander's relationship with individual soldiers. They are analytically separate and should not be used to evaluate each other.
