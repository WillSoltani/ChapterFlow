# Quiz Blueprint: Chapter 12 — The Attack by Fire

**correctIndex plan:** 0=3-4, 1=3-4, 2=3-4
**Forbidden shapes:** no "best applies", no "best reflects", no chapter-title-in-quotes

---

## Core concepts under test

- concept A: Five fire targets — a hierarchy of disruption from surface (soldiers) to substrate (stores, arsenals) — §1-4
- concept B: Four preconditions as a necessary decision gate — all must be met; improvising around one degrades the action — §5-8
- concept C: Response timing after fire — observe first; coordinate external attack with internal upheaval; wait if no upheaval — §9-12
- concept D: Fire vs. water — multiplicative (destroys the substrate) vs. additive (reinforces what is already there) — §13-14
- concept E: Anger prohibition and three-condition rule — advantage certain, victory calculable, danger avoidable; anger passes, consequences do not — §15-20
- concept F: Waiting cost — precondition deferral has a temporal cost the framework does not resolve — hard-depth extension
- concept G: Self-certification problem — the three conditions can be met on paper by a motivated commander; character, not structure, is the only guard — hard-depth extension
- concept H: Additive/multiplicative distinction requires enemy resource intelligence — if enemy has substitute resources, the supposedly multiplicative action is only additive — hard-depth extension

**q01** — Bloom's: Remember | Depth: Easy | Concept: A (five fire targets)

Topic: Identifying the five targets for fire attack and their relationship to operational capacity.

Prompt direction: Ask the reader to identify which of the five fire targets Sun Tzu names would have the most durable strategic effect on an enemy's capacity to continue a campaign — and why.

Correct answer: Arsenals and magazines represent the most durable strategic disruption. They contain the materiel the enemy's entire military capacity depends on. Destroying them degrades not just the current engagement but the enemy's ability to resupply and sustain future operations. Soldiers in camp, by contrast, can be replaced by recruitment; lost materiel requires time, resources, and supply chains the enemy may not have available.

Wrong answer 1 (tests misconception: soldiers in camp are the highest-value target because eliminating the enemy's fighting force ends the campaign immediately): Attacking soldiers in camp produces the most immediate effect and ends the campaign fastest by eliminating the people who do the fighting. Materiel without soldiers is useless, so stores and arsenals are secondary targets.
Wrong answer 2 (tests misconception: baggage trains are the highest-value target because they contain ongoing operational supply): Baggage trains are the highest-value target because they carry the supplies the army is actively using. Destroying them disrupts the immediate campaign more directly than destroying fixed arsenals, which may be replenished from distant sources.
Wrong answer 3 (tests misconception: dropping fire on the enemy directly is the most effective because it combines fire with direct engagement): Dropping fire directly on the enemy force is the most powerful method because it combines disruption and destruction in a single action, making it more effective than attacking any fixed logistical target.

**q02** — Bloom's: Understand | Depth: Easy | Concept: B (four preconditions)

Topic: Understanding why all four preconditions must be met before fire attack is justified.

Prompt direction: Ask what happens when a commander proceeds with fire attack before all four preconditions are in place — specifically, what is the cost of improvising around an unmet precondition rather than deferring until conditions are satisfied.

Correct answer: Proceeding without all four preconditions produces a lower-probability action. The fire agent may not be positioned to achieve full effect. The wrong season reduces the dryness that fire depends on. The wrong day means wind conditions may not carry the fire where it needs to go. Any one of these deficits reduces the action's likelihood of success while consuming the agent and alerting the enemy. Deferral is the correct response to partial readiness, not a fallback — the framework is designed to make waiting the right answer when conditions are incomplete.

Wrong answer 1 (tests misconception: proceeding with three of four conditions is acceptable because the fourth condition is a marginal improvement): Missing one of the four preconditions only slightly reduces effectiveness. A commander who has three conditions in place and a strategic window that may close should proceed — the marginal cost of an unmet precondition is smaller than the cost of a missed opportunity.
Wrong answer 2 (tests misconception: the preconditions are guidelines rather than strict requirements; a skilled commander adapts): A skilled general uses the preconditions as guidelines but can adapt to unmet conditions through superior preparation and execution. The precondition framework applies to average commanders; an exceptional one can compensate for missing conditions through skill.
Wrong answer 3 (tests misconception: improvising around an unmet precondition is preferable to waiting because waiting cedes initiative to the enemy): Seizing the initiative matters more than satisfying all preconditions. A commander who waits for perfect conditions allows the enemy to prepare. Partial-condition fire attack may cost some effectiveness but preserves the initiative that waiting forfeits.

**q03** — Bloom's: Apply | Depth: Medium | Concept: C + D (response timing + additive/multiplicative)

Topic: Applying the observation-first doctrine and the fire/water distinction to a non-military competitive situation.

Prompt direction: A startup has just learned that a major competitor is experiencing a significant internal crisis — a product recall that has generated negative press and is consuming the competitor's leadership attention. The startup's team wants to immediately launch a major marketing campaign positioning themselves as the safe alternative. Their co-founder argues for waiting and observing first — watching whether the competitor's crisis produces actual customer defection or whether the competitor manages to contain the narrative quickly. Which response is more consistent with the chapter's doctrine — and what does the distinction between fire and water reveal about which type of competitive action the marketing campaign actually is?

Correct answer: The co-founder's wait-and-observe approach is more consistent with the chapter's doctrine. The response timing principle says to coordinate the external action with the internal upheaval — not to launch the external action while the outcome is still uncertain. A marketing campaign launched before customer defection has actually occurred is water: it reinforces an existing dynamic (the startup's positioning) rather than exploiting the competitor's disrupted substrate. If the competitor contains the crisis, the campaign hits a recovered adversary. Waiting to confirm that upheaval is real before launching the campaign is the correct sequencing.

Wrong answer 1 (tests misconception: launching immediately is correct because the window of competitive opportunity is narrow and waiting forfeits it): Moving immediately is correct because competitive windows close quickly. A competitor in crisis is most vulnerable in the first twenty-four hours; waiting for confirmed customer defection allows the competitor time to recover. The correct response to an opponent's disruption is immediate external pressure.
Wrong answer 2 (tests misconception: the marketing campaign is fire rather than water because it attacks the competitor directly): The marketing campaign is fire because it targets the competitor — it attacks the competitor's brand and market position, which is equivalent to attacking soldiers in camp. Water would be a defensive move; any offensive action against the competitor qualifies as fire.
Wrong answer 3 (tests misconception: the fire/water distinction is irrelevant to competitive marketing decisions because the categories only apply to military logistics): The fire and water distinction applies only to physical resource destruction and reinforcement. Marketing campaigns operate in a different domain and do not map onto the chapter's distinction between multiplicative and additive disruption.

**q04** — Bloom's: Apply | Depth: Medium | Concept: E (anger prohibition + three-condition rule) | Named character: Fatou (senior manager, investment firm)

Topic: Applying the three-condition rule in a high-stakes professional context where emotion is present.

Prompt direction: Fatou is a senior manager at an investment firm. A junior analyst on her team publicly contradicted her recommendation during a client meeting, causing visible confusion and undermining the client's confidence in the firm's position. Fatou is furious. After the meeting, she is considering documenting a formal performance warning and removing the analyst from all client-facing responsibilities immediately. She believes this is justified — the analyst's behavior was unprofessional and harmful. Before acting, she runs the three-condition check. Apply each condition: is the advantage of her proposed action certain? Is the outcome calculable? Is the danger to other goals avoidable?

Correct answer: The three-condition check reveals that the proposed action fails at least the third condition. The danger to other goals — specifically, the risk of being seen by her team as retaliating against dissent rather than managing performance — is not clearly avoidable. Acting immediately while visibly angry compounds this risk. The advantage of formal documentation is also less certain than it appears: it may be correct in its content but undermined by the timing and the emotional state in which it was produced. The chapter's instruction is to halt when any condition is not met. Fatou should delay the formal response until her evaluation of all three conditions passes.

Wrong answer 1 (tests misconception: all three conditions are met because Fatou has legitimate grounds for the action, making anger irrelevant to the evaluation): Fatou's evaluation passes all three conditions. She has legitimate grounds, the outcome is predictable given her authority, and the danger is acceptable given the seriousness of the analyst's behavior. The anger is irrelevant to whether the action is correct — correctness is determined by the merits, not the emotion accompanying them.
Wrong answer 2 (tests misconception: the three-condition rule only applies to major strategic decisions, not to personnel management): The three-condition rule applies only to decisions with existential stakes — launching a campaign, going to war. Personnel decisions are ordinary managerial actions that should be based on performance standards, not subject to Sun Tzu's pre-action evaluation framework.
Wrong answer 3 (tests misconception: the anger prohibition means Fatou should not act until the anger has fully passed, regardless of whether the action is otherwise justified): The anger prohibition means Fatou must wait until she feels no anger before acting, because any decision made while angry is by definition compromised. Once the anger passes completely, she can proceed with the same action she intended to take today.

**q05** — Bloom's: Apply | Depth: Medium | Concept: B + E (preconditions + three-condition rule) | Named character: Nikolai (graduate student, research grant)

Topic: Applying the precondition framework and three-condition rule to an academic challenge situation.

Prompt direction: Nikolai is a graduate student preparing to defend a research grant proposal before a departmental panel. Two days before submission, a panel member sends a critical email questioning his core methodological assumption. Nikolai's first instinct is to reply immediately with a detailed defense of the methodology. He drafts the reply. Before sending it, he asks whether the preconditions for this action are in place: does he know what the panel member's actual concern is, or just how it appeared in the email? Does he know whether the email represents the member's settled view or an opening for dialogue? Does he know whether other panel members share the concern? He realizes he cannot verify any of these. What does the chapter's precondition logic suggest Nikolai should do?

Correct answer: The preconditions for an email defense are not in place. Nikolai does not know the shape of the concern well enough to address it precisely — the email gives him the concern's surface, not its substance. An email defense sent without that knowledge is a fire attack launched without the agent properly positioned: it consumes the response, risks escalating the concern, and does not resolve what it is aimed at. The precondition logic suggests deferral: request a brief conversation to understand the concern before responding in writing. The conditions for a well-targeted response are met by the conversation, not before it.

Wrong answer 1 (tests misconception: sending the detailed defense is correct because any delay signals uncertainty or weakness in the methodology): Responding immediately with a thorough defense is correct because delay signals that Nikolai is uncertain about his methodology. The panelist's concern is best addressed by demonstrating that the methodology is defensible, which requires engaging directly and promptly.
Wrong answer 2 (tests misconception: Nikolai should not respond at all and let the panel evaluation speak for itself): The best response to a pre-submission challenge is silence. Engaging the panelist before the review creates a relationship dynamic that may distort the panel's evaluation. Nikolai should let the proposal stand on its own merits.
Wrong answer 3 (tests misconception: the precondition framework only applies to irreversible physical actions and does not translate to academic or professional communication decisions): The precondition framework applies to military operations only. Nikolai's decision about whether to send an email is a routine professional judgment and should be made based on professional norms, not military tactical doctrine.

**q06** — Bloom's: Apply | Depth: Medium | Concept: A + D (target hierarchy + additive/multiplicative) | Named character: Juniper (freelancer, retainer negotiation)

Topic: Applying the fire-target hierarchy and the additive/multiplicative distinction to a negotiation context.

Prompt direction: Juniper is a freelancer negotiating a long-term retainer with a potential corporate client. The client has been slow to respond and is dragging the negotiation over several small contract terms. Juniper is considering two approaches to accelerate the negotiation. Approach A: send a detailed message making her case for each disputed term individually, demonstrating the value of her work on each point. Approach B: contact the client's head of marketing — the internal champion who brought her name to procurement — to ask whether the contract priorities have shifted internally. Which approach addresses the substrate of the slow negotiation rather than its surface?

Correct answer: Approach B addresses the substrate. The slow negotiation is likely not caused by the disputed terms themselves — those are surface. The substrate is whether the internal priority for the project has changed, or whether the procurement process has encountered a bureaucratic obstacle the head of marketing can remove. Approach A is additive: it reinforces Juniper's position on the contested points without addressing what is actually causing the delay. Approach B is multiplicative: it targets the condition that is generating the delay, which, if resolved, eliminates the problem rather than managing it one term at a time.

Wrong answer 1 (tests misconception: Approach A is more appropriate because it demonstrates professional thoroughness and respects the procurement process): Approach A is correct because it addresses the specific disputed terms through proper channels. Contacting the head of marketing bypasses the procurement process and risks damaging the client relationship by appearing to circumvent the people managing the contract.
Wrong answer 2 (tests misconception: both approaches are equivalent because both are designed to move the negotiation forward): Both approaches serve the same purpose and are roughly equivalent in strategic value. The choice between them is a matter of preference and communication style, not a strategic decision with different leverage implications.
Wrong answer 3 (tests misconception: neither approach addresses the real problem, which is Juniper's failure to set a deadline for the negotiation): Neither approach addresses the substrate. The correct action is for Juniper to set a clear deadline for the negotiation to conclude, which changes the dynamics by introducing time pressure that makes the client's slow pace costly to them.

**q07** — Bloom's: Analyze | Depth: Hard | Concept: F (waiting cost of the precondition framework)

Topic: Analyzing the structural gap in the precondition framework — the cost of deferral that the chapter does not resolve.

Prompt direction: The chapter instructs that fire attack should be deferred when any of the four preconditions is unmet. The precondition framework produces a clear rule: wait. But a commander who waits for all four conditions to be simultaneously met may wait long enough that the opportunity the fire attack was designed to exploit no longer exists. Two analysts consider this problem. Analyst A says: the precondition framework is complete — if the opportunity disappears while waiting for the right conditions, the opportunity was not viable under the framework, and deferral was the correct response. Analyst B says: the precondition framework prescribes deferral but provides no rule for when deferral itself becomes the wrong choice — specifically, when the cost of not acting while waiting exceeds the cost of acting under imperfect conditions. Which analyst identifies the deeper structural problem?

Correct answer: Analyst B identifies the deeper problem. The precondition framework treats readiness as a binary gate: conditions met, proceed; conditions unmet, defer. But deferral has a temporal cost that is not addressed anywhere in the framework. An opportunity is not static — it degrades as the enemy moves, as political contexts shift, as the agent's position becomes less viable. The framework prescribes deferral without providing a rule for the point at which the cost of further deferral exceeds the cost of proceeding with imperfect conditions. That gap makes the framework incomplete: it resolves the question of whether to act when fully ready, but not the question of when partial readiness is better than continued waiting.

Wrong answer 1 (tests misconception: Analyst A is correct because the framework's completeness means any opportunity lost to waiting was not a viable opportunity by the framework's standards): Analyst A is correct. The precondition framework defines what counts as a viable opportunity — one where all conditions are met. An opportunity that disappears before conditions are met was never viable under the framework's own standard. The framework does not have a gap; it has a strict definition of readiness that excludes premature action by design.
Wrong answer 2 (tests misconception: both analysts identify the same problem at different levels of analysis, and the practical resolution is to try to accelerate condition-satisfaction rather than acting before conditions are met): Both analysts describe the same tension between readiness and opportunity. The practical resolution — accelerating the satisfaction of the unmet condition — is implied by the framework. Neither analyst identifies a structural gap; they identify a practical challenge the commander must address through preparation.
Wrong answer 3 (tests misconception: the waiting cost problem is solved by the three-condition rule, which provides the framework for when to act under conditions of uncertainty): The three-condition rule — advantage certain, victory calculable, danger avoidable — resolves the waiting-cost problem. When a commander can satisfy the three conditions despite an unmet precondition, the three-condition rule authorizes action. The two frameworks work together to fill the gap Analyst B identifies.

**q08** — Bloom's: Analyze | Depth: Hard | Concept: G (self-certification problem in the three-condition rule)

Topic: Analyzing the self-certification problem — whether the three-condition rule can stop motivated reasoning.

Prompt direction: The chapter's three-condition rule — move only when advantage is certain, victory calculable, and danger avoidable — is designed to prevent a commander from acting from anger or urgency. But the evaluation of all three conditions is performed by the same commander who may be motivated to proceed. Two readers consider whether the rule works in practice. Reader A says: the rule works because it requires the commander to state explicit criteria — advantage, calculability, avoidable danger — and meeting those criteria is a factual standard that the commander's emotional state cannot change. Reader B says: the rule does not reliably stop motivated reasoning because the inputs to the evaluation — what counts as certain advantage, what constitutes calculable victory, what qualifies as avoidable danger — are all defined by the evaluating commander. A commander who wants to act can define the inputs to support that conclusion without violating the rule's form. Which reader's account identifies the more significant structural problem?

Correct answer: Reader B identifies the more significant problem. The three conditions are evaluative categories, not factual ones. "Advantage is certain" requires a judgment about certainty. "Victory is calculable" requires a judgment about the reliability of the calculation. "Danger is avoidable" requires a judgment about risk. All three judgments are made by the commander who may be motivated to reach a particular conclusion. A commander who begins the evaluation wanting to act will define each category in a way that allows a positive result. The rule's form — three explicit conditions — creates the appearance of discipline without providing a structural guard against the bias it is designed to prevent. The chapter relies on the commander's character, not on a structural check.

Wrong answer 1 (tests misconception: Reader A is correct because the three conditions create explicit criteria that must be met, making motivated deviation detectable): Reader A identifies the more reliable mechanism. The three conditions create a formal structure that requires the commander to produce specific justifications for each criterion. That formality makes it harder to proceed on purely emotional grounds, because the commander must articulate reasons that can be examined by others.
Wrong answer 2 (tests misconception: both readers describe the same structural tension, and the resolution is to have a second evaluator review the three-condition analysis before the commander acts): Both readers identify the same problem. The practical solution — requiring a second evaluator — is implied by the chapter's broader argument about the counsel of competent advisors. Neither reader identifies a different structural problem; they identify the same gap at different levels of abstraction.
Wrong answer 3 (tests misconception: the self-certification problem is irrelevant because the chapter's anger prohibition operates independently of the three-condition rule): The anger prohibition is a separate and sufficient rule: it tells the commander not to act from anger, full stop. The three-condition rule is supplementary. Even if the three conditions can be manipulated by a motivated commander, the prior prohibition on anger-driven action provides an independent check that the three-condition self-certification problem does not undermine.

**q09** — Bloom's: Evaluate | Depth: Hard | Concept: H + Ch11 connection (additive/multiplicative distinction requires enemy resource intelligence)

Topic: Evaluating the additive/multiplicative distinction and its intelligence prerequisite — connecting Ch12's fire/water logic to Ch11's classification accuracy problem.

Prompt direction: The chapter claims fire is multiplicative — it destroys the substrate of resistance — while water is additive. But whether an action is truly multiplicative depends on whether the enemy has substitute resources. An arsenal that is destroyed may be multiplicative if the enemy has no other source of supply; it is additive if the enemy can resupply from allied arsenals. Two readers assess the implications. Reader A says: the fire/water distinction is a reliable analytical category — destroying a supply depot is always multiplicative because physical destruction of material reduces the enemy's total capacity. Reader B says: the fire/water distinction is a diagnostic that requires prior intelligence about the enemy's resource structure; without that intelligence, a commander cannot know whether the destroyed target is truly the substrate or merely one of several substitutable instances. Which reader's account is more consistent with the chapter's analytical framework — and what does this imply about the intelligence requirement that fire attack shares with Ch11's ground-type classification?

Correct answer: Reader B's account is more consistent. The chapter's analytical force depends on fire being multiplicative — destroying the conditions for resistance, not just a particular instance of it. But that force only holds if the target is genuinely the substrate rather than one of multiple available sources. Destroying one of three arsenals leaves the enemy with two; the action is additive, not multiplicative. The intelligence prerequisite that fire attack shares with Ch11's ground-type framework is the same in structure: both require verified knowledge of the enemy's actual resource and positional situation, not assumed knowledge. A commander who assumes a target is multiplicative without verifying the enemy's substitute capacity may execute fire attack with precision while achieving only additive effect.

Wrong answer 1 (tests misconception: Reader A is correct because physical destruction of materiel is objectively multiplicative regardless of the enemy's resource structure): Reader A is correct. Physical destruction reduces the enemy's total capacity by the amount destroyed, regardless of whether substitutes exist. The distinction between additive and multiplicative is about the type of action, not about the enemy's response to it. Destroying an arsenal is multiplicative by definition.
Wrong answer 2 (tests misconception: the fire/water distinction and Ch11's ground-type classification address entirely different problems and share no common intelligence requirement): The two frameworks address different problems — Ch12 addresses weapons selection; Ch11 addresses positional classification. They operate in different domains and share no structural requirement. Applying Ch11's intelligence prerequisite to Ch12's fire/water distinction conflates frameworks that were designed for different purposes.
Wrong answer 3 (tests misconception: the enemy's substitute resources are relevant only after the fire attack has been conducted, as part of post-action assessment, not as a pre-action intelligence requirement): Whether the enemy has substitute resources is relevant only during the post-action phase, when the commander assesses whether the attack achieved its intended effect. The pre-action intelligence requirement is satisfied by correctly identifying the target, not by mapping the enemy's substitute supply structure.

**q10** — Bloom's: Evaluate | Depth: Hard | Concept: F + G + Ch1 connection (temporal cost + self-certification + the opening calculus of Ch1)

Topic: Evaluating the relationship between Ch12's three-condition rule and Ch1's pre-campaign advantage calculation — whether Ch12's rule adds a structural check or restates Ch1's opening logic.

Prompt direction: Chapter 1 establishes that every campaign begins with a calculation of comparative advantage — five factors, seven considerations, and a systematic comparison of the two sides before commitment. Chapter 12's three-condition rule — advantage certain, victory calculable, danger avoidable — appears to impose the same logic again at the moment of fire attack. Two readers evaluate whether Ch12's rule adds something new. Reader A says: Ch12's three-condition rule is a terminal application of Ch1's logic — the same pre-commitment calculation applied at the point of irreversible action rather than at campaign opening. Ch12 adds no new principle; it reinforces Ch1's calculus in a specific high-stakes context. Reader B says: Ch12's three-condition rule adds something Ch1 does not contain — a specific structural check against emotional motivation, designed to operate precisely when the calculus is most likely to be distorted by urgency, anger, or desire for revenge. Ch1's calculation is performed before commitment; Ch12's check is designed to operate in conditions where emotion has already entered. Which account identifies what Ch12 adds to Ch1 that is not already present?

Correct answer: Reader B identifies the additional element. Ch1's calculation is pre-commitment and relatively cold: it is performed before the campaign has begun, before casualties have occurred, before personal grievance or emotional stakes have accumulated. Ch12's three-condition rule is designed to operate under conditions of emotional pressure — specifically, when a commander is angry or otherwise motivated to act. The structural addition is the explicit prohibition on emotional motivation as a basis for action, applied to a decision made at a moment of high emotional intensity. Ch1 does not address the emotional state of the evaluator; it assumes the calculation is performed without bias. Ch12 closes that assumption by naming the distortion — anger — and explicitly prohibiting it as a basis for commitment.

Wrong answer 1 (tests misconception: Reader A is correct because the two rules are logically equivalent and Ch12 adds no new principle): Reader A is correct. The three-condition rule and Ch1's calculus are logically equivalent — both require a positive advantage calculation before commitment. Ch12 does not add a new principle; it applies an existing one to a specific context. Recognizing the connection shows that Ch1 and Ch12 form a coherent whole.
Wrong answer 2 (tests misconception: neither reader is correct because the two rules operate in different domains and cannot be directly compared): Both readers attempt an invalid comparison. Ch1 applies to campaign-level decisions; Ch12 applies to tactical-level weapons choices. The two rules operate at different levels of strategy and cannot be evaluated against each other for what each adds.
Wrong answer 3 (tests misconception: the key difference is that Ch12's rule is more restrictive — it requires certainty, while Ch1 requires only favorable probability): The additional element Ch12 contributes is greater evidentiary strictness: Ch1 requires a favorable comparison of factors; Ch12 requires certainty. The new principle is a higher evidentiary standard for action, not an emotional-state check. Ch12 raises the bar for what justifies commitment, not the domain in which the bar applies.
