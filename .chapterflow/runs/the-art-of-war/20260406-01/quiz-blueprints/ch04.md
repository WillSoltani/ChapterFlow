# Chapter 4 Quiz Blueprint — Tactical Dispositions

Chapter: Tactical Dispositions (Ch 4)

## Core concepts to test
- concept 1: Defense-offense asymmetry — defense (making defeat impossible) is in your own hands; offense (the opportunity to win) is provided by the opponent's error
- concept 2: No-reputation excellence — the highest form of winning produces victories that look obvious, not spectacular; absence of drama is the signal of quality
- concept 3: No mistakes as the mechanism — victory is decided by eliminating error during preparation, not by superior performance during the fight
- concept 4: Five-step cognitive chain — Measurement → Estimation of quantity → Calculation → Balancing of chances → Victory, each step depending on the quality of the preceding one
- concept 5: Accumulated advantage and strategic deadlock — the pent-up-waters threshold, and the limit case where both sides execute perfect defense

## Bloom ceiling: evaluate (level 5)

## Question plan

### q01: basic recall — what the defense-offense asymmetry states
Test whether the reader can accurately state the asymmetric claim in §1-2: that securing yourself against defeat is in your own hands, while the opportunity to defeat the enemy is provided by the enemy. Wrong answers should include the inversion (opportunity to win is also in your hands) and the symmetry error (both halves are equally controllable).

### q02: recall — what the five-step chain produces at each stage
Test whether the reader can identify the correct order and direction of dependency: Measurement → Estimation → Calculation → Balancing → Victory, and that each step's quality limits what the next step can produce. Wrong answers should scramble the order or claim the chain can compensate for bad step-one inputs.

### q03: concept application — apply defense-first to a non-military setting
Present a scenario outside the military domain (e.g., an engineer asked to ship a new feature before the risk analysis is complete) and ask which move is most consistent with the defense-first principle. Wrong answers should include "ship now and iterate" (offensive-first) and "avoid the feature entirely" (confusion of defense-first with passivity).

### q04: named-scenario (work) — Tariq's startup defensive positioning
Tariq's startup has enough runway for six months. A competitor just entered his target market. Tariq's team is building a defensible core product but is not ready to compete directly. His co-founder Preet wants to launch a partial product now to stake a claim. What does the defense-first principle say about this choice?

The question should be analytical, not recall. Wrong answers should include: launch now to establish presence (prioritizing offense over defense), wait indefinitely (passivity misreading), launch only in a weaker adjacent market (avoidance rather than preparation). The correct direction: use the remaining preparation time to make the core product competition-proof before entering the contested segment.

### q05: analysis — why no reputation is the marker of excellence
Ask the reader to explain why victories that earn no reputation for wisdom or courage are, in Sun Tzu's framing, the highest form of excellence — rather than evidence of a weak opponent. The question should require distinguishing between preparation-driven ease and opposition-weakness ease.

Wrong answers should include: "no reputation means the opponent was weak" and "Sun Tzu recommends humility as a virtue." The correct direction: if the victory looks easy, it is because the preparation eliminated all scenarios where it would be close. The ease is the product of the work, not evidence of a lack of challenge.

### q06: named-scenario (personal) — Mira's conflict decision
Mira's colleague Jerome publicly took credit for a project she led. The attribution error happened in a message sent the day before a performance review cycle. Mira has evidence but not the full documentation she would need to make a clean case. She is deciding whether to raise the issue now or wait and build the complete record.

What does the defense-first principle say about her timing decision? Wrong answers should include: raise it immediately because delay is passive, raise it only after the review is complete to avoid seeming difficult, ignore it entirely. The correct direction: the question is not when to act but whether the position (the evidence) is strong enough to act from. Acting from an incomplete position is offensive-first strategy; building the position first is defense-first.

### q07: analysis — apply five-step chain to a decision with faulty step-1 data
Present a scenario in which a team runs a complete five-step chain (all steps present, careful calculation) but the initial Measurement was based on a contested assumption about the competition field. Ask what the chain's dependency structure predicts about the final output.

The question should distinguish between "the chain didn't work" (wrong) and "the chain worked precisely — it amplified the error from step one through all subsequent steps" (correct). Wrong answers should include: "the calculation step would have caught the error" and "balancing of chances is where errors typically appear."

### q08: limit — the strategic deadlock question
Ask directly: if both competing teams execute the defense-first principle perfectly — both make defeat impossible, both wait for the opponent's error — what does the chapter's logic predict will happen?

Wrong answers: "one side will eventually make a mistake" (assumes asymmetry the question removes), "the better-prepared team wins" (same assumption), "Sun Tzu says this cannot happen." The correct direction requires the reader to sit with the chapter's actual limit: the principle does not resolve symmetric cases. If both sides are truly invincible, neither can provide the opportunity the other needs. The chapter does not have an answer for this. Whether that is a failure of the argument or the argument's honest conclusion about deterrence is the hard-depth live question.

### q09: synthesis — relationship between Ch4 defense-first and Ch3 preserve-intact
Ch3 argued that the highest form of achievement resolves a contest without destructive battle. Ch4 argues that a commander must first make defeat impossible. Ask the reader to identify the relationship between these two principles — specifically, which one is the prerequisite for the other and why.

Wrong answers should include: "they contradict each other" and "Ch3's approach makes Ch4's unnecessary." The correct direction: Ch3's methods (breaking plans, disrupting alliances) require the freedom to maneuver that only comes from security against defeat. A commander who has not made defeat impossible cannot afford to wait for the opening that the highest methods require. Ch4 is the prerequisite for Ch3.

### q10: predict — what Ch5 will address given Ch4's emphasis on accumulated force
Ask the reader to predict, based on Ch4's closing images (pent-up waters, pound vs grain), what question the next chapter is most likely to address. The prediction should be grounded in the logic of the text, not chapter titles.

Wrong answers: "Ch5 will address how to defend against counterattacks" and "Ch5 will explain why siege is sometimes necessary." The correct direction: Ch4's images establish accumulated advantage poised to be released. The natural next question is how the release is structured and timed — specifically, what the relationship is between direct force (which joins battle) and indirect force (which wins it). That is the Energy chapter's concern.

## Distractor notes
- Tempting wrong type 1: confusion of defense-first with passivity or avoidance. Many readers will interpret "make defeat impossible" as "avoid conflict." Questions q03, q06, and q08 should all have a distractor of this type.
- Tempting wrong type 2: the no-reputation excellence misread as "be humble" or "the opponent was weak." Questions q05 should have both distractors available.
- Tempting wrong type 3: the chain-as-guarantee error — the belief that a complete five-step chain produces a reliable output regardless of step-one quality. Question q07 should make this the primary tempting wrong answer.
- Tempting wrong type 4: the symmetry error in q08 — the assumption that the chapter's principle resolves symmetric cases by declaring one side the winner.
