# Chapter 9 Outline — The Courage to Do the Right Thing

Chapter Promise:
By the end, the reader should understand that courage in leadership means accepting personal cost in order to protect people, truth, and standards when pressure makes the easier option attractive.

Opening Move:
Open in a pressured room where everyone is silently waiting to see who will take the first hit instead of pushing it downward.

Anchor Allocation:
- Johnny Bravo weather letdown used in P2 and P3
- empathy / "they would have done it for me" logic used in P3 and P5
- leaders eat last sacrifice-before-privilege image used in P4 and P6

Paragraph Job Map:
- P1: live pressure scene and chapter question
- P2: Johnny Bravo as the clearest on-disk case of doing the costly right thing without waiting for permission
- P3: explain courage as empathy plus obligation, not macho performance
- P4: generalize through leaders eat last and the direction of cost
- P5: translate into organizational reality: truth, standards, and protection under pressure
- P6: hard edge and bridge: courage must stay costly and situational, then point toward the conditions that enable or suppress it

Required Distinction or Mechanism:
Courage is not generic virtue. It is the moment when leadership turns authority or role into burden-bearing action for others.

Hard-Depth Tension:
Preserve the boundary between courage and reckless self-display. The right thing has to be costly, but the cost must still serve protection, standards, or group survival rather than vanity.

Takeaway Count Lock:
- Easy: 3
- Medium: 5
- Hard: 5

Hard Takeaway Topics:
- Takeaway 1: courage tests the leadership definition from Chapter 8
- Takeaway 2: Johnny Bravo and the right-thing standard
- Takeaway 3: empathy as the source of courage
- Takeaway 4: sacrifice-before-privilege and the direction of cost
- Takeaway 5: the limit condition that separates courage from recklessness or self-dramatization

Scenario Lesson Map:
- Scenario 1 (decision_point, work): teaches taking first pressure so the truth can surface cleanly
- Scenario 2 (postmortem, school): teaches how avoidance of short-term discomfort damages trust later
- Scenario 3 (dialogue, personal): teaches that care sometimes requires a hard honest conversation
- Scenario 4 (predict_reveal, work): teaches that convenient wins can quietly tax trust and standards
- Scenario 5 (dilemma, school): teaches that protecting fairness may require conflict with peers
- Scenario 6 (before_after, personal): teaches how courage changes a relationship when one person finally accepts discomfort instead of dodging it

Next-Chapter Bridge:
If courage depends on more than personality, what happens when the environment itself is a bad fit for healthy action?

Genericity Risks:
- turning courage into a speech about values
- treating "right thing" as obvious in every case
- writing a hero chapter instead of a cost chapter

Forbidden Drift:
- motivational-warrior language
- moral slogan paragraphs with no pressure or tradeoff
- invented battlefield or boardroom details

Fresh Ticket Notes:
- Keep the chapter tied to cost, empathy, and protection.
- The bridge to Chapter 10 should raise the environment question without answering it yet.
