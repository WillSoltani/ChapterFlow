# Chapter 24 — Edited Draft

When abstraction is normal early, people do not only work inside it. They are formed by it. That is the safest way into `The Abstract Generation`. Chapter 23 argued that systems can hide who pays for success. Chapter 24 most likely asks what happens when a generation grows up in environments where distance, mediation, and abstract forms of recognition already feel normal. The answer should not be stereotype. It should be formation.

That only works if the chapter keeps its limit. This is not a complaint that one age group is morally weaker than another, and it is not nostalgia for a supposedly purer past. The chapter's safer claim is that systems shape expectations. If belonging, progress, approval, and consequence are often experienced through mediated or abstract channels, people can carry those habits into school, work, friendship, and leadership without having chosen them deliberately.

That is why the revised title matters. `The Abstract Generation` says the problem is no longer only leaders using abstraction badly. It is also people entering adulthood already trained by it. They may expect recognition without much embodied relationship, authority without much trust, or progress without much patience because the surrounding systems have taught them to experience human life through more distance.

The trust framing gives the chapter its stakes. Healthy groups foster trust and cooperation because people feel trusted and valued inside a Circle of Safety. But those conditions are harder to build when people have been formed in settings where approval is thinner, contact is more mediated, and consequence is more abstract. The issue is not moral defect. It is that concrete human trust can feel slower, stranger, and less intuitive than abstract feedback.

That is why Chapter 12 matters here. Generational conflict is often formation conflict in disguise. Leaders who respond with contempt miss the point because the chapter is not assigning essence to a cohort. It is asking what kind of expectations a system produces. If the system trains thinner forms of contact, then cynicism, self-protection, and impatience can emerge without anybody deciding to be shallow on purpose.

The chapter stays honest only if it keeps one final boundary. The generation is not the problem by itself, and abstraction is not destiny. But `The Abstract Generation` says leaders have to rebuild more concrete habits of trust if they want people formed under abstraction to recover richer forms of belonging. Once that is true, the next chapter can turn naturally toward admission, recovery, and the disciplined work of beginning again.
