# Chapter 10 — Canonical Draft

The numbers are bad, the person looks wrong for the job, and the room rushes toward the easiest verdict: maybe they were never good enough. `Snowmobile in the Desert` most likely interrupts that reflex. The title itself suggests a cleaner question. Before we condemn the machine, should we ask whether we dropped it into the wrong terrain?

That metaphor does a lot of work without needing extra decoration. A snowmobile can be excellent at the thing it was built to do and still look useless in the desert. The failure would be real, but the diagnosis would change. The problem would not mainly be the machine. It would be the mismatch between design and environment. That is the safest way to read this chapter's core move.

The frozen Simon FAQ strengthens that reading. It says the book examines the disconnect between how our bodies were designed to function and how they function today. That pushes the metaphor beyond equipment and into human systems. Some poor outcomes may come from asking people to operate under conditions that distort judgment, trust, cooperation, or energy before we ever get to character.

That matters because the book keeps returning to environment. The frozen materials talk about healthy and safe conditions, about the Circle of Safety, and about leaders creating the trust and collaboration that let groups work well. If that is true, then leadership is not only about judging people accurately. It is also about designing terrain well enough that people can actually show what they are capable of.

This makes the chapter practical. A struggling person may still be responsible for their choices, but leaders fail when they treat underperformance as personal weakness without checking role fit, incentives, psychological safety, or the basic conditions of the work. A wrong environment can make a strong person look weak, just as a good environment can reveal strength that bad terrain had hidden.

The hard edge is that the chapter cannot become a blanket excuse. Some failures are personal. Some people really are in the wrong role because they lack discipline, skill, or honesty. But the title warns against making that the first explanation every time. Leaders should inspect the desert before declaring the snowmobile broken. Once that becomes the habit, the next question follows naturally: how did we build so many deserts in the first place?
