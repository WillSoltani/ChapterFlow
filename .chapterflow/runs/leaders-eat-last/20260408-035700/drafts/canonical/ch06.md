# Chapter 6 — Canonical Draft

The sprint board is full, the numbers are green, and nobody in the room looks safer. People are energized, but it is the thin kind of energy that makes everyone stare at their own lane. A title like `E.D.S.O.` suggests the book is finally naming the internal rewards underneath that pattern. Chapter 5 asked when “more” stops serving the mission. Chapter 6 seems to answer by moving one layer deeper: some forms of pursuit feel good fast, and leaders ignore that at their own risk.

The frozen source bundle gives a narrow but usable way to read the acronym. Simon Sinek’s official book FAQ says *Leaders Eat Last* explores the influence of neurochemicals on how people think, feel, and behave. The same FAQ tells readers to understand their biological chemicals and adapt. It also distinguishes serotonin and oxytocin as selfless chemicals and contrasts them with endorphins and dopamine, which it says can be released alone. That makes `E.D.S.O.` the strongest filesystem-backed expansion available: endorphins, dopamine, serotonin, and oxytocin grouped together before the next chapter turns to the remaining fifth force.

Grouped that way, the chapter does not need a lab lecture to do its job. It only needs a leadership distinction. Some rewards are easier to trigger through chase, completion, relief, or individual success. Others depend more on other people, shared safety, and reciprocal benefit. If a workplace learns how to flood the first set and neglect the second, people can look productive while becoming more isolated. The room stays active, but the social fabric gets thinner.

That is why this chapter belongs right after `When Enough Is Enough`. The problem is not only that people want too much. It is that some systems make narrow rewards feel urgent, rational, and professional. A leader who only knows how to stimulate the fast hit will keep building contests, dashboards, and short-cycle incentives. A leader who understands the relational side of motivation has to ask a harder question: what in this environment teaches people to trust, help, and stay loyal when no metric is flashing?

Seen this way, `E.D.S.O.` is less a chemistry lecture than an environment-design chapter. The frozen takeaways for the book say readers should understand their biological chemicals and adapt, and should try to create a healthy and safe environment. Those ideas fit together. You do not manage people well by pretending they are pure logic. You manage them by noticing which rewards your culture makes cheap and which rewards it makes rare. If only the solo wins feel immediate, collaboration starts sounding sentimental. If people reliably feel seen, protected, and connected, different behavior becomes easier to sustain.

The chapter still needs restraint. The source freeze does not support a detailed neurochemical map, and the title alone cannot carry one. But it does support the core move: leadership works on bodies as well as beliefs. `E.D.S.O.` likely sets the four named chemicals so the next chapter can isolate the remaining force. Its practical lesson is severe and useful. Good leadership is not only telling people to behave better. It is building conditions where the better behavior is rewarded deeply enough to last.
