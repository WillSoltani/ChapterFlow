# Chapter 11 — Canonical Draft

The system is humming, the numbers look strong, and almost nobody in the room feels any urgency to ask what the success is training people to become. That is the safest way into `The Boom Before the Bust`. The title does not sound like a complaint about failure first. It sounds like a warning that failure may have been prepared during a period everyone was still calling progress.

The frozen material gives only a narrow way to build that claim, but it is enough. In the authorized preview, factory employees stand at their machines waiting for bells to tell them when to start, rest, and eat. That image matters because it shows a system built for rhythm, scale, and control. It may have worked well enough for the problem it was trying to solve. But a system can feel orderly and productive long before anyone asks what kind of human habits it is normalizing.

The same frozen excerpt sharpens the point with its language about linear hierarchies and recognition from above. That is more than a description of office politics. It suggests a work design in which people learn to measure success through approval, reward, and signals from the top. During a boom, that may look efficient. When growth is strong, people often tolerate structures that would feel brittle or dehumanizing under strain because the outward results keep telling them the system must be fine.

That is where Chapter 10's terrain question turns historical. If the book says today there is a disconnect between how we were designed to function and how we function now, then Chapter 11 most likely asks when that disconnect began to harden. The safest answer is not a date or a theory of the whole economy. It is a pattern. Earlier conditions may have rewarded systems built for output, pace, and hierarchy so successfully that later generations inherited those systems as common sense. What once looked like the engine of the boom may have quietly planted the logic of the bust.

This makes the chapter more uncomfortable than a simple anti-growth argument. A boom is not fake just because it comes first. People may really have benefited. Order, scale, and clarity can solve real coordination problems. But success can also hide cost, especially human cost, because the system is not being judged under pressure yet. The same rules that help an organization expand can later weaken trust, flatten judgment, and reduce people to parts once the environment changes or the scale becomes too large for human signals to travel cleanly.

So the chapter's real move is cautionary. Do not ask only whether a system once worked. Ask what it taught people to normalize, what kind of leadership it rewarded, and what kind of human strain it hid while outcomes stayed high. `The Boom Before the Bust` is less a history lecture than a delayed-causation warning. It says the present did not simply go wrong one day. It may be living inside the aftereffects of earlier success. And if that is true, the next question follows naturally: who came of age inside those rules and carried them forward?
