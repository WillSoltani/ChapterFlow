# Chapter 7 Edited Draft: Overcome Key Organizational Hurdles

A strategy can pass the commercial test and still go nowhere. The move may offer real buyer value, workable economics, and a plausible adoption path, yet the organization remains stuck. People do not see why the strategy matters, resources stay tied to older priorities, key contributors never move, and internal resistance quietly slows everything down. A strong strategy can therefore fail not because the move is weak, but because the institution around it never shifts.

Kim and Mauborgne answer that problem by refusing vague execution language. Broad appeals to culture, alignment, or change readiness are too soft for this chapter. The issue is not that organizations resist in some general way. The issue is that a few specific hurdles keep motion from starting. When those hurdles stay unnamed, leadership spreads effort too widely and gets little back for it.

The chapter's governing idea is tipping point leadership. The point is not to reform everything at once. It is to concentrate effort where a small move can unlock wider change. That makes execution strategic rather than ceremonial. Instead of treating resistance as an atmosphere, the chapter treats it as a set of bottlenecks that can be attacked directly.

Those bottlenecks take four forms. Cognitive hurdles appear when people do not yet grasp the need for the move or still treat the strategy as abstract. Resource hurdles appear when scarce means stay tied to familiar routines instead of high-leverage priorities. Motivational hurdles appear when the people whose movement matters most remain untouched. Political hurdles appear when internal blockers, threatened interests, or hidden opposition make progress costly. The chapter matters because it keeps those hurdles distinct instead of flattening them into one morale problem.

That distinction changes how leadership behaves. If the barrier is cognitive, the task is to make strategic reality visible enough that people stop hiding inside old assumptions. If the barrier is resources, the task is to concentrate scarce means where they can move the whole system rather than spread them thinly for symbolic fairness. If the barrier is motivation, the task is to move the few people or units whose behavior shifts everyone else's sense of what is real. If the barrier is political, the task is to surface and confront the blockers early instead of pretending execution is neutral.

The chapter therefore rejects diffuse reform. It does not promise that inspiration, budget growth, or broad participation alone will make execution happen. It argues that leadership has to find the few leverage points strong enough to move the rest. That is why the chapter follows the strategic sequence so cleanly. Once the move deserves to live commercially, the next question is how to move the institution through the barriers still holding it back.

This also prepares the next chapter without collapsing into it. Once the organization can move through its key hurdles, leadership still has to build execution into strategy so commitment holds. Overcoming the hurdles creates motion. The next step is to make that motion feel fair, understood, and durable enough to last.
