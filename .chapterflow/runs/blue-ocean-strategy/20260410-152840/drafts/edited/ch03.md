# Chapter 3 Edited Draft: Reconstruct Market Boundaries

Teams usually search for growth where they already know how to look. They benchmark direct rivals, compare features, follow the same buyer definitions, and call that disciplined strategy. But this search starts inside the industry's accepted borders, so even energetic thinking can stay trapped in the same box. A room can generate dozens of ideas and still never question whether the market itself has been defined too narrowly.

Kim and Mauborgne answer that trap with a harder move: reconstruct market boundaries instead of treating them as fixed. The point is not abstract creativity. It is lower search risk. If the current rivals, buyer group, and usage frame are inherited without challenge, then strategy has already accepted too much of the market's logic before the work begins.

The public tool for that work is the six paths framework. Its value is discipline, not novelty theater. It tells a team to look across alternative industries, strategic groups, buyer groups, complementary products and services, the functional-emotional orientation of an industry, and time. That list matters because it interrupts the habit of searching only against direct competitors. It widens the field without collapsing into random brainstorming.

Each path presses on a different assumption about what the market really is. One path asks whether buyers compare alternatives from different industries rather than from one category alone. Another asks whether the real move sits between strategic groups instead of inside one of them. Another asks whether the purchaser, user, or influencer has been treated as the obvious buyer when that assumption should be challenged. The complementary-offering path widens the lens to what happens before, during, and after use. The functional-emotional path questions whether an industry competes mainly on utility or feeling. The time path asks whether a team is freezing today's pattern instead of seeing the direction of change early enough to act.

The official examples help only in a narrow way, but that narrow support is enough. Bloomberg is publicly tied to a buyer-group shift. Canon is useful as a reminder that the practical user can matter more than the conventional purchaser. Viagra is publicly tied to the functional-emotional path. The lesson is not case memorization. It is that blue oceans often appear when value is recombined across boundaries the market had started treating as natural.

This chapter is not a permission slip for wild ideation. A team can mention all six paths and still stay trapped if every option remains a variation on the same competitive frame. The real test is whether boundary reconstruction produces a commercially clearer way to create buyer value, not whether the workshop felt inventive. Once a team has several boundary-shifting options, it still has to step back, see the strategic picture, and choose with discipline. That is where the book moves next.
