# Chapter 9 Edited Draft: Align Value, Profit, and People Propositions

A blue-ocean move can look compelling to buyers and still fail to hold. The offer may seem attractive, demand may appear reachable, and the strategy may sound persuasive in the room. Yet the move begins to wobble when the economic logic underneath it is weak or when the people who have to carry it do not have incentives, support, and alignment strong enough to execute it well. Chapter 9 matters because it argues that buyer value alone is not enough. Successful strategy has to align the value proposition, profit proposition, and people proposition.

Kim and Mauborgne start with the value proposition because that is what buyers experience first. A strategy has to offer a leap in utility strong enough to attract demand. But the chapter refuses to stop there. A buyer-winning move that cannot sustain itself economically is unstable. The chapter therefore adds the profit proposition as the condition that makes the move viable for the firm rather than merely attractive in concept.

Even that pairing is not sufficient. The chapter then adds the people proposition. This is the part that keeps the strategy from flattening into a buyer-and-economics story alone. The people proposition is about whether employees, partners, and relevant participants have aligned incentives and support strong enough to carry the move in practice. If the people side is weak, the strategy may look strong on paper while execution keeps slipping around it.

That is the chapter's central alignment logic. The three propositions are not independent boxes to check. They have to reinforce one another. A strong value proposition with a weak profit proposition creates strain. A strong value and profit proposition with a weak people proposition creates drag in execution. The move becomes sustainable only when the buyer-facing promise, the economic logic, and the people-side support fit together instead of pulling apart.

The expanded-edition warning is sharp here. Official chapter support allows a narrow Tata Nano caution: a move can combine a compelling value proposition and a viable profit proposition yet still suffer in execution when the people proposition is mishandled. The chapter uses that kind of warning to show why alignment has to be strategic rather than decorative. Leaders cannot treat people-side execution as a secondary clean-up problem after value and profit are already set.

This also explains why Chapter 9 follows fair process but does not duplicate it. Chapter 8 focused on building trust into execution through engagement, explanation, and expectation clarity. Chapter 9 widens the frame. It asks whether the full strategic system is aligned across buyers, economics, and people. Fair process can help execution hold, but a misaligned proposition set can still weaken the move. Sustainable strategy needs both execution legitimacy and proposition alignment.

The chapter therefore prepares the book's final turn toward renewal. Once leaders understand that successful blue oceans require the value, profit, and people propositions to reinforce one another, they are in a better position to judge which moves deserve to be renewed over time. Alignment is what keeps a move from looking impressive only at launch. It is what gives the strategy a chance to last.
