# Chapter 5 Edited Draft: Reach Beyond Existing Demand

Growth teams often stay loyal to the people already buying. They refine segments, personalize offers, and study current customers more closely, assuming better strategy means understanding the existing pool in finer detail. That work can help, but it can also trap demand search inside the market's present borders. A team may become more precise about today's buyers while missing the larger demand sitting just outside the category.

Kim and Mauborgne push against that trap by shifting attention from existing customers to noncustomers. The point is not to dismiss current buyers. It is to see that real demand expansion often comes from people the market underserves, repels, or never considered relevant in the first place. If a team studies only current customers, it may learn how to compete better inside the same pool while never asking who else the market has left outside.

The public framework for that move is the three tiers of noncustomers. One tier sits at the edge of the market and is about to leave or is only weakly attached. A second tier actively refuses the industry's offer because its tradeoffs do not feel worth accepting. A third tier remains unexplored because the market has never treated those people as realistic demand. The framework matters because it widens the search for demand without collapsing into vague expansion talk.

Each tier points to a different way demand stays locked out. Soon-to-be noncustomers show where the current market barely holds people in. Refusing noncustomers show where accepted tradeoffs push people away. Unexplored noncustomers show where the market's imagination has been too narrow from the start. The value of the framework is not endless sorting. It is seeing why demand remains outside.

That is why the chapter pushes commonality harder than finer segmentation. A blue-ocean move does not come from slicing today's customer base into smaller groups forever. It comes from noticing shared barriers that keep many outsiders away and redesigning the offer around those barriers. The goal is not a more precise version of the same pool. It is a wider value logic that makes the market newly relevant to people who had been standing outside it.

This chapter also points naturally into strategic sequence. If a team sees real demand among noncustomers, the next question is whether the offer can actually work commercially. Buyer value still has to line up with price, cost, and adoption. Reaching beyond existing demand is not the finish line. It is the point where a bigger opportunity becomes visible enough to test seriously.
