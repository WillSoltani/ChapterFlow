# Chapter 5 Draft: Reach Beyond Existing Demand

Growth teams often stay loyal to the people already buying. They refine segments, personalize offers, and study loyal customers more closely, assuming better strategy means understanding the current customer pool in finer detail. That work can be useful, but it can also trap demand search inside the market's existing borders. A team may become more precise about today's buyers while missing the larger demand that sits just outside the category.

Kim and Mauborgne push against that trap by shifting attention from existing customers to noncustomers. The point is not to ignore current buyers. The point is to see that real demand expansion often comes from people the market underserves, repels, or never considered relevant in the first place. If a team studies only current customers, it may learn how to compete better inside the same pool while never asking who else the market has left outside.

The public framework for that move is the three tiers of noncustomers. One tier sits at the edge of the market and is about to leave or is only weakly attached. A second tier actively refuses the industry's offer because its tradeoffs do not feel worth accepting. A third tier remains unexplored because the market has never treated those people as realistic demand. The framework matters because it widens the search for demand without turning the chapter into vague expansion talk.

Each tier points to a different way demand stays locked out. Soon-to-be noncustomers show where the current market barely holds people in. Refusing noncustomers show where the market's accepted tradeoffs push people away on purpose. Unexplored noncustomers show where the market's imagination has been too narrow from the start. The chapter's value is not in sorting people endlessly. It is in seeing why demand is staying outside.

That is why the chapter pushes commonality harder than finer segmentation. A blue-ocean move does not come from slicing today's customer base into smaller and smaller groups forever. It comes from noticing shared barriers that keep many outsiders away and then redesigning the offer around those barriers. The goal is not more precise targeting of the same pool. It is a wider logic of value that makes the market newly relevant to people who had been standing outside it.

This is also why the chapter naturally leads into strategic sequence. If a team sees real demand among noncustomers, the next question is whether the offer can actually work commercially. Buyer value still has to line up with price, cost, and adoption. Reaching beyond existing demand is therefore not the finish line. It is the point where a bigger opportunity becomes visible enough to test seriously.
