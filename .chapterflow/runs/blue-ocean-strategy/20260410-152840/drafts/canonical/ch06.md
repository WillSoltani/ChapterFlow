# Chapter 6 Draft: Get the Strategic Sequence Right

It is easy to fall in love with an opportunity before asking whether it can survive reality. A team sees strong buyer interest, a fresh strategic curve, or a larger demand pool beyond current customers and starts acting as if the hard part is over. But promising demand is not the same as a viable move. A blue ocean only holds if the idea can survive a tougher sequence of commercial pressure.

Kim and Mauborgne answer that risk by insisting on sequence. The question is not simply whether the idea sounds exciting. The question is whether buyer utility comes first, whether the offering can be priced to attract broad demand, whether the cost structure can support that move, and whether adoption hurdles will still block execution after everything else looks attractive. The chapter matters because it turns strategic excitement into an ordered test.

Buyer utility comes first because novelty without real value does not create a blue ocean. An idea can be original, polished, and energetically presented while still failing to solve a problem buyers actually care about. If utility is weak, later discussions about price or cost become a distraction. The move fails at the start.

Price comes next because value that only works for a narrow elite does not create broad new demand. Cost follows because the offer must be supportable, not just desirable in theory. Adoption then becomes the final pressure. Even a strong value proposition can collapse if the obstacles to adoption remain too high. The sequence matters because each step protects the team from declaring victory too early.

This chapter is not a loose checklist of business criteria. Its force comes from alignment. Strong buyer utility cannot rescue an impossible cost structure forever. Attractive price cannot rescue a move buyers do not truly value. A workable cost model cannot rescue a move adoption barriers will choke. The sequence is valuable because it shows where a promising strategy breaks when one pressure fails to support the others.

That is why the chapter leads naturally into execution barriers. Once a move survives buyer utility, price, cost, and adoption pressure, the next question is not whether the strategy is good on paper. The next question is what in the organization will still resist it. Sequence discipline does not replace execution. It prepares the ground for it by filtering out moves that were never commercially sound to begin with.
