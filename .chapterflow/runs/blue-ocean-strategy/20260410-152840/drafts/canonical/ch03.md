# Chapter 3 Draft: Reconstruct Market Boundaries

Teams usually search for growth where they already know how to look. They benchmark direct rivals, compare features, follow the same buyer definitions, and call that disciplined strategy. The problem is that this kind of search begins inside the industry's accepted borders, so even energetic thinking can stay trapped in the same box. A room can generate dozens of ideas and still never question whether the market has been defined too narrowly from the start.

Kim and Mauborgne answer that trap with a sharper move: reconstruct market boundaries instead of treating them as fixed. The point is not to get more creative in the abstract. The point is to reduce search risk by looking where the industry has stopped looking. If the current rivals, buyer group, and usage frame are all inherited without challenge, then the strategy search has already surrendered too much of its freedom.

The official tool for that work is the six paths framework. Its value is discipline, not novelty theater. It tells a team to look across alternative industries, across strategic groups, across buyer groups, across complementary products and services, across the functional-emotional orientation of an industry, and across time. That list matters because it interrupts the lazy habit of searching only against direct competitors. It widens the field without turning strategy into random brainstorming.

Each path presses on a different assumption about what the market really is. One path asks whether buyers compare alternatives from different industries rather than from one category alone. Another asks whether the real move sits between strategic groups instead of inside one of them. Another asks whether the purchaser, user, or influencer has been treated as the obvious buyer when that assumption should be challenged. The complementary-offering path widens the lens to what happens before, during, and after use. The functional-emotional path questions whether an industry competes mainly on utility or feeling. The time path asks whether a team is freezing today's pattern instead of seeing the direction of change early enough to act on it.

The official examples matter only in a narrow way, but even that narrow support is useful. Bloomberg is tied publicly to a buyer-group shift. Canon is useful as a reminder that the practical user can matter more than the conventional purchaser. Viagra is publicly tied to the functional-emotional path. The lesson is not to memorize cases. The lesson is that blue oceans often appear when a team recombines value across boundaries the market had been treating as natural.

That is why this chapter should not be read as a permission slip for wild ideation. A team can mention all six paths and still stay trapped if every option remains a variation on the same competitive frame. The real test is whether boundary reconstruction produces a commercially clearer way to create buyer value, not whether the workshop felt inventive. Once a team has several boundary-shifting options, it still has to step back, see the strategic picture, and choose with discipline. That is where the book moves next.
