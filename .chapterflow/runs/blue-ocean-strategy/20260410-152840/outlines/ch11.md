Chapter Promise:
Readers will understand how competition-first habits pull organizations back into red oceans and why avoiding those traps means resisting benchmarking, imitation, and fixation on existing demand.

Opening Move:
Open on a team that knows the language of blue oceans but has quietly slipped back into measuring itself mainly against rivals.

Anchor Allocation:
- anchor 1 used in paragraph(s): P1-P2 for red-ocean logic and relapse framing
- anchor 2 used in paragraph(s): P3-P4 for benchmarking and imitation traps
- anchor 3 used in paragraph(s): P5-P6 for existing-demand fixation and the closing warning

Paragraph Job Map:
- P1: re-establish what red-ocean logic means at the end of the book
- P2: frame trap avoidance as the final discipline
- P3: show why trying to beat competitors recreates imitation
- P4: show why benchmarking narrows strategic imagination
- P5: show why fighting over existing customers weakens demand creation
- P6: close the book by tying trap avoidance back to buyer value and new demand

Required Distinction or Mechanism:
The chapter must distinguish operational awareness of rivals from strategy being defined by rivals.

Hard-Depth Tension:
Readers should feel how easy it is to relapse into red-ocean thinking precisely because benchmarking and current-demand focus feel responsible in the short run.

Threshold Question:
What would prove that a team is using competitors as reference points so heavily that it has slipped back into red-ocean logic?

Takeaway Count Lock:
- Easy: 3 (always)
- Medium: 5
- Hard: 5

Hard Takeaway Topics:
- what red-ocean relapse means late in the book
- why beating rivals leads toward imitation
- why benchmarking narrows strategy
- why fighting over existing demand weakens blue-ocean logic
- why trap avoidance closes the book as an ongoing discipline

Scenario Lesson Map:
- Scenario 1 (decision_point, work): leader notices the strategy review is built around competitor columns instead of buyer value
- Scenario 2 (postmortem, school): team realizes its project copied rival moves and lost originality
- Scenario 3 (dialogue, personal): siblings debate whether chasing the same limited audience is shrinking their options
- Scenario 4 (predict_reveal, work): manager expects benchmarking to sharpen strategy until it reveals imitation drift
- Scenario 5 (dilemma, school): seminar team must choose between serving current demand better and exploring noncustomers
- Scenario 6 (before_after, personal): organizer stops tracking rivals obsessively and starts designing for unmet value

Scenario Vividness Targets:
At least four scenarios should include comparison tables, rival dashboards, benchmark decks, customer-segment boards, or demand maps.

Preview Constraint:
As the closing chapter, the preview should resolve toward disciplined vigilance rather than open a new framework.

Genericity Risks:
- generic anti-competition slogans
- claiming rivals should never be watched at all
- inventing an official six-trap checklist
- flattening the chapter into a full-book recap

Forbidden Drift:
- fabricated company relapse case study
- unsupported numbered trap taxonomy
- vague motivational language about thinking differently
- brief-language leakage
