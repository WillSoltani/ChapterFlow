Core Claim:
Activation energy is the model that explains why starting is often disproportionately hard. Systems, habits, and projects may require a threshold of energy before they become easier to sustain. Strong judgment therefore treats ignition as a design problem: lower the barrier, improve the trigger, or supply the initial push instead of misreading failure to start as proof that the goal is wrong.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
This chapter is about startup thresholds and ignition cost, not leverage's force multiplication. It should feel impossible to confuse with Chapter 7 because the core move is not finding a multiplier but clearing the barrier required to begin at all.

Author Logic Chain:
First the author establishes that starting often costs more than continuing.
Then the author shows how underpricing the threshold blocks reactions and habits before they can sustain themselves.
Then the author concludes that wise action reduces startup barriers or designs better triggers for ignition.

Frameworks or Terms Introduced:
- Activation energy: the initial threshold required to start.
- Ignition barrier: the cost of getting from rest into motion or reaction.
- Trigger design: arranging cues or conditions that make starting easier.
- Startup subsidy: extra help supplied at the beginning so continuation becomes possible.

Specific Applications:
- designing better starts for personal habits
- helping teams get past kickoff friction in shared projects
- recognizing when a project needs startup support rather than more judgment

Common Misreadings:
- treating failure to start as proof of weak commitment
- assuming good plans should feel easy from the first step

Counterarguments or Limits:
Lowering the threshold does not eliminate the need for real follow-through. Some reactions start easily and still fail later. The chapter should not treat ignition as the whole story.
