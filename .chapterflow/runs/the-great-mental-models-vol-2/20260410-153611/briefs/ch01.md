Core Claim:
Relativity is a judgment model about vantage point, framing, and timescale. The chapter argues that a view can be partial without being worthless, and disagreement often comes from position rather than stupidity. Better judgment comes from asking what the world looks like from another seat, then extending the timeline so short-term appearances do not dominate interpretation. The chapter is not asking the reader to flatten truth into "all views are equal." It is asking for a wider frame before committing to certainty.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
This chapter is about perspective discipline, not empathy as a virtue and not speed of action. It should feel impossible to confuse with later chapters because its core move is to recast the same event through multiple frames and time horizons.

Author Logic Chain:
First the author establishes that every observer sees from a limited position.
Then the author shows that many confusing actions become legible when you reconstruct the other person's frame.
Then the author concludes that stronger judgment comes from changing vantage point and extending the timeline before deciding what something means.

Required Anchors:
- A disagreement that looks irrational until the observer reconstructs the other person's constraints and information.
- A report, article, or plan whose framing hides the interests or position of the speaker.
- A short-term reading of an event that changes once the timeline is extended.

Frameworks or Terms Introduced:
- Relativity: different positions create different partial views of the same event.
- Vantage point: what a person can see, know, and feel from their location.
- Framing: the shape information takes when it is presented through someone's interests or role.
- Time-horizon shift: changing interpretation by looking farther ahead or farther back.

Specific Applications:
- reading a proposal without mistaking its frame for neutral truth
- interpreting conflict without collapsing into personal contempt
- slowing judgment long enough to compare short-term and long-term readings

Common Misreadings:
- thinking the chapter says every perspective is equally valid
- mistaking perspective-taking for passivity or indecision

Counterarguments or Limits:
Perspective-taking can become endless hesitation if it never reaches a decision. Some frames are distorted or self-serving, so understanding them does not mean endorsing them. Relativity widens judgment, but it does not replace standards of evidence.
