Core Claim:
Replication is the model for understanding how systems persist by copying, reproducing, or transmitting what allows them to continue beyond a single instance. Strong judgment asks what can be reliably carried forward, what degrades in transmission, and how replication differs from mere repetition.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
Chapter 15 focused on protecting current survival. Chapter 16 should shift from defending one existing system to understanding how a system extends itself into future instances. The distinct move is from preservation of the current form to transmission of the form.

Author Logic Chain:
First the author shows that survival of one instance is not the same as persistence across time.
Then the author explains that replication depends on what can be copied or transmitted reliably.
Then the author concludes that wise action distinguishes durable replication from shallow repetition.

Required Anchors:
- A setting where something valuable persists because it can be copied or taught reliably.
- A case where transmission degrades because the system is not replicable enough.
- A case where repetition is mistaken for real replication.

Frameworks or Terms Introduced:
- Replication: copying or transmitting what allows a system to persist.
- Transferability: how well a pattern or function carries into a new instance.
- Fidelity: how accurately something survives transmission.
- Repetition trap: doing something again without making it replicable.

Specific Applications:
- organizations building repeatable processes
- school or club practices that can be taught to others
- personal routines that remain stable because they can be repeated under changing circumstances

Common Misreadings:
- treating repetition as proof of replication
- assuming what works once can automatically be transferred

Counterarguments or Limits:
The chapter should not imply that exact copying is always possible. Replication may require adaptation to context while preserving core structure.
