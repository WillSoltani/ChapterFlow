Core Claim:
Cooperation is the model for understanding how multiple actors coordinate action toward outcomes they cannot reach as effectively alone. Strong judgment asks what makes cooperation stable, what shared gains it produces, and where coordination breaks under mistrust, misaligned incentives, or weak structure.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
Chapter 16 focused on persistence through transmission across instances. Chapter 17 should move from patterns surviving across people to people actively coordinating with one another. The distinct move is from replicated pattern to coordinated action.

Author Logic Chain:
First the author shows that multiple actors can create outcomes through aligned effort.
Then the author explains that cooperation depends on structure, trust, and shared gain.
Then the author concludes that wise action builds conditions where coordination is worth sustaining.

Required Anchors:
- A setting where coordination produces something no single actor could achieve as well alone.
- A case where mistrust or weak structure causes cooperation to break down.
- A system where short-term self-protection weakens shared gain.

Frameworks or Terms Introduced:
- Cooperation: coordinated action among actors pursuing shared gain.
- Coordination cost: the effort required to align multiple contributors.
- Shared gain: value created through joint action.
- Defection pressure: the force pulling actors away from cooperation toward narrower self-interest.

Specific Applications:
- teams aligning across functions
- school groups coordinating roles and timing
- personal life through shared household or relationship effort

Common Misreadings:
- treating cooperation as automatic harmony
- assuming shared goals remove the need for structure

Counterarguments or Limits:
The chapter should not romanticize teamwork. Cooperation can fail when costs, incentives, or trust are badly arranged.
