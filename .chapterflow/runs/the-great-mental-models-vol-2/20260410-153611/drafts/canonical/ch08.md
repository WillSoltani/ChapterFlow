# Activation Energy

Maya had rewritten her plan three times and still had not started the essay. The topic was clear enough. The writing itself was manageable once she got going. But the blank document kept blinking, the outline stayed folded in her backpack, and each night she somehow spent more energy circling the start than she would have spent writing the first paragraph. That is the pressure activation energy adds to judgment. Starting often costs more than continuing.

Activation energy is the threshold required to get a reaction going. In practical life, that means habits, projects, and team efforts often fail not because the path is wrong, but because the barrier to ignition is higher than people expected. A run may be easy after the shoes are on and the door is closed behind you. A project may move well after kickoff once ownership is clear. The hard part is crossing the initial threshold.

That is why this chapter is not generic just-start advice. It is about ignition design. Colin may think he lacks discipline because his workout keeps dying before it begins, when the real barrier is that the bag is unpacked, the clothes are missing, and the first step still feels expensive. Rina may think her team lacks urgency because the project keeps stalling, when the real blocker is that nobody owns the first action on the kickoff agenda. The chapter keeps asking what the actual startup barrier is.

The model gets practical when it introduces trigger design and startup subsidy. Lower the barrier. Improve the cue. Supply extra energy at the beginning so the reaction can cross the threshold and then run more easily. Felix may leave the outline open on the desk instead of folded in the bag. Tori may assign the first action before the meeting ends instead of hoping momentum appears later. Small changes at the point of ignition can matter more than repeated frustration afterward.

This is the warning the chapter adds: people misread hard starts all the time. They treat failure to begin as evidence that the goal is weak or that the person does not care enough. Sometimes that is true. Often the better diagnosis is that the ignition barrier is too high. A system can be well designed for continuation and badly designed for starting.

The chapter also keeps an important limit alive. Lowering the threshold is not the whole story. Some things start easily and still fail later. Startup help is not the same thing as carrying the system forever. A project can get a strong kickoff and still drift without follow-through. A habit can begin tonight and still disappear next week. Activation energy is about crossing the first barrier, not pretending that every later barrier vanishes with it.

That is what makes the chapter a clean follow from leverage. Chapter 7 asked where force should land to create outsized effect. Chapter 8 asks what it costs to get the reaction moving in the first place. A well-placed push may still fail if it does not clear the startup threshold.

The practical lesson is exacting. Diagnose the first barrier before judging the whole plan. Lower the threshold, strengthen the trigger, or supply extra help at the beginning. Then watch whether the system can continue once it is actually underway. Activation energy matters because it keeps judgment from confusing a hard start with a bad goal.
