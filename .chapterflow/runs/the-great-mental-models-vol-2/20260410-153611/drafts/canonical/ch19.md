# Incentives

Priya noticed that her team kept rushing small tasks to completion while the more important maintenance work sat untouched. The dashboard celebrated quantity, the weekly check-in praised visible throughput, and the bonus conversation leaned on whatever could be counted quickly. Nobody had been told to ignore the deeper work. Still, the system kept pulling attention toward what it visibly rewarded. The chapter on incentives begins there. Behavior follows consequences more faithfully than slogans.

Incentives are the model for how systems steer behavior through reward, penalty, and practical consequence. The chapter matters because people rarely respond only to official values or stated goals. They respond to the signals built into the environment around them. A structure can say it wants one thing and still produce another if the rewards point elsewhere.

Lucy sees the same logic in school when participation points are meant to reward engagement but end up rewarding speed of response more than depth of thought. Students adapt quickly to the real signal. The system may say it cares about understanding, yet the behavior it is paying for is fast visible contribution. Incentives matter because they reveal what the system is actually selecting.

The model becomes sharper when it names mismatch directly. Mateo may be told to focus on quality, yet if his review is driven mainly by volume, the practical message is clear. Hannah may want balanced discussion in her club, but if points go to whoever speaks most often, the system quietly favors airtime over substance. A reward structure can distort outcomes by rewarding a narrow slice of the desired result.

Adaptation is not a flaw in the people inside the system. It is often a rational response to the system itself. Victor may choose the metric that keeps him safe, not because he misunderstands the goal, but because he understands the consequences. Incentives work because they shape the cost of one choice relative to another.

The chapter also resists a narrow reading of incentives as money alone. Praise, visibility, grades, convenience, friction, and avoided penalties can all act as incentives. What matters is not the form. What matters is the direction of behavior the structure makes easier, safer, or more rewarding.

The practical rule is to ask what behavior is being rewarded in practice, what stated goal that reward is supposed to serve, where the signal is mismatched, and how people are likely to adapt if the structure stays unchanged. Incentives sharpen judgment because they force attention onto what a system is selecting rather than what it says it prefers.

Chapter 18 showed how larger systems organize people across levels and roles. Chapter 19 adds that those systems still have to direct behavior inside the structure they create. That leads naturally to the next question: once behavior is being steered, why do systems often drift toward the lower-effort path unless conditions change?
