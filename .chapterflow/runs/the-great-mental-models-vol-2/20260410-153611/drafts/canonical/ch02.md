# Reciprocity

Nora saw the late-night Slack message and felt the heat rise before she finished reading it. Caleb had pushed her work back into the queue with a clipped note that sounded like a public correction. Her first instinct was simple: answer sharply, copy the manager, and make sure he felt the same sting she felt. The move would have been satisfying for about five minutes. It also would have become the opening line in the next round between them. That is the point reciprocity forces into view. Your action does not end with your action. It invites a return move, and that return move starts shaping the environment you have to live in next.

That idea sounds obvious until you remember how often people act as though each move stands alone. We send the curt message and think only about release. We ignore a small favor and think only about convenience. We respond to a slight and think only about justice. Reciprocity says that is a bad map for repeated interaction. In any setting where the same people keep meeting, today's tone becomes part of tomorrow's reality. A cold move can teach the other person to harden. A generous move can create room they did not expect to give back. A small insult can compound into watchfulness. A small favor can compound into trust. Life is iterative faster than most people want to admit.

That is why the chapter is not about being nice in the soft, slogan sense. It is about understanding loops. A first move sends information. It tells the other person what kind of game you are playing, what kind of future you expect, and how costly you intend the next exchange to be. If you begin with contempt, you make contempt easier to return. If you begin with measured openness, you create a different set of likely replies. That does not guarantee a good outcome. It changes the probabilities. The chapter's practical claim is that better judgment considers the reaction your action is likely to trigger before you commit to the first move.

This becomes clearer in ordinary relationships because the loop is rarely dramatic at first. Tessa forgets to send notes from class once, and June shrugs it off. Then it happens again, and the shrug gets thinner. Malik borrows equipment after practice and brings it back late, so Coach Rivera stops trusting him with small responsibilities. A colleague takes credit for one helpful suggestion and does not notice the social bill arriving until people stop volunteering ideas around him. Repeated interaction compounds tone. You are not only dealing with the current exchange. You are training the next one.

The chapter also argues that positive first moves can be strategic, not sentimental. Going positive first is not the same as pretending everyone is good or harmless. It means you sometimes create a better loop by making cooperation easier than suspicion in the opening round. A thoughtful introduction, a fair reading of a mistake, a useful favor given before leverage is demanded, or a reset after a small misunderstanding can change the quality of what comes back. People often rise to the interaction they are invited into. Not always. Often enough that the first move matters more than cynical people like to admit.

Forgiveness fits here for the same reason. In a repeated interaction, score-settling can become its own trap. If every slight must be answered in full, the loop hardens and the cost of repair keeps rising. Sometimes the stronger move is to absorb a small miss, clarify the boundary, and reopen the chance for a better next round. That is not weakness. It is an attempt to preserve value across time instead of winning one moment and poisoning five more. The chapter's version of forgiveness is practical. It assumes no malice first when the evidence is thin, because that assumption often keeps the loop from degrading too early.

But reciprocity has a boundary, and the boundary matters. A positive first move is wise only when there is still a real chance the other side will answer in kind. Some actors take generosity as free extraction. Some repeated partners keep defecting because the cost is always paid by someone else. In those cases, more patience does not improve the loop. It trains the wrong lesson. Reciprocity is not a command to cooperate with bad-faith behavior forever. It is a model for reading how your move will change the next move, including when the right change is a harder boundary or an exit.

That tension is what keeps the chapter honest. If you ignore reciprocity, you act as though your moves disappear once expressed. If you oversimplify reciprocity, you turn it into naive kindness and get used. The harder version is narrower and more useful: choose first moves that make the next round better when better is still possible, and stop feeding the loop when the return move keeps proving otherwise. In that sense, reciprocity is less about morality than about sequence. It asks you to think one move further than your impulse.

Nora eventually rewrote the message. She answered Caleb directly, named the missing context behind her work, and suggested ten minutes the next morning to sort the queue before either of them pulled in the manager. The note was not soft. It was cleaner. Caleb came back sharper than she hoped, but not sharper than before. That mattered. The loop had not improved yet, but it had not deteriorated either. Sometimes reciprocity begins there. You choose the move that keeps the next move from getting worse.

That is the chapter's practical promise. Your actions teach people what to send back to you. In repeated settings, small moves do not stay small. They accumulate into trust, resistance, generosity, suspicion, help, or retaliation. The next chapter changes the pressure again by moving from relationship loops to the question of how systems hold order, spend energy, and slide toward disorder if no force keeps them together.
