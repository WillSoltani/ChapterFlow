# Relativity

Maya slid the printout across the conference table and asked for approval before lunch. Owen frowned almost immediately. The plan looked reckless to him: slower hiring, a longer testing window, and a short-term dip in sales targets. From his seat, it felt timid. Maya did not argue at first. She turned the page and showed him the supplier timeline he had not seen, the customer complaints she had been fielding, and the compliance deadline sitting six weeks ahead. What looked weak from one seat looked responsible from another. The facts had not changed. The view had.

That is the opening discipline of relativity as a mental model. The chapter is not asking you to give up on truth or to pretend every interpretation deserves the same weight. It is asking you to stop mistaking your position for the whole picture. You are always standing somewhere. Your role, incentives, time pressure, and recent experience shape what feels obvious to you. So do the things you cannot see. When someone else's decision looks irrational, the useful first question is not "what is wrong with them?" It is "what would the world have to look like for that move to make sense from where they stand?"

That question changes the quality of judgment because it interrupts one of the easiest mistakes people make. We often treat disagreement as proof of defect. Someone must be careless, selfish, naive, or slow. Sometimes that is true. Often it is not. Often the disagreement comes from different constraints, different information, or a different clock. A founder looks at the next financing round. An engineer looks at failure risk. A teacher looks at the whole classroom while a student feels one moment of embarrassment. A parent sees a ten-year consequence while a teenager sees tonight. The same event can carry different meanings because the observers are not standing in the same place.

The model becomes even more useful when you apply it to information, not just people. A report is never only a report. An article is never only a set of facts. A proposal is never only a plan. Each one arrives already framed by a position. Someone chose what to count, what to leave out, what to place first, and what to call urgent. If you forget that, you start reacting to the frame as though it were neutral reality. That is how a narrow dashboard becomes a full strategy, or a loud headline becomes a confident belief. The smarter move is to ask who is inside the frame, who is outside it, and what problem the frame is quietly trying to solve.

Time creates another form of relativity. A bad quarter can look like collapse if you stand too close to it. A delayed answer can look like indifference if you judge it in the first hour. A strict teacher can look unfair in September and wise in May. Extending the timeline does not guarantee a kinder judgment, but it often produces a truer one. Some events only make sense when you watch what they do next. Others only become dangerous when you stop at the short-term relief they provide. Perspective is not only about other people. It is also about where you place yourself on the clock.

This is why the chapter matters as an opening move for the book. Before you can reason well about leverage, incentives, or cooperation, you need a better habit for reading what is in front of you. Relativity trains that habit. It asks you to reconstruct the other seat, inspect the frame, and lengthen the timeline before you lock in your verdict. Those moves do not remove the need for standards. A self-serving explanation can still be self-serving after you understand it. A misleading report remains misleading even after you see how it was built. But your judgment improves because you are no longer confusing your first view with the full event.

There is a limit built into the model, and it matters. Perspective-taking can become an elegant form of avoidance. You can keep widening the frame until every decision feels premature. You can keep honoring complexity until clarity dissolves. That is not what the chapter is after. The point is not endless sympathy or endless analysis. The point is disciplined interpretation before commitment. Widen the view enough to stop being fooled by your first angle, then choose.

Once you start using relativity this way, ordinary situations stop feeling so flat. A tense email becomes evidence of a rushed manager, a defensive colleague, a bad metric, or a hidden fear, not just an insult. A student's resistance might point to laziness, or it might point to shame, confusion, or a classroom signal you missed. A proposal that seems foolish may be protecting against a risk you have not priced in. The model does not make you softer. It makes you less easy to fool, especially by your own certainty.

That is the chapter's practical promise. Better judgment begins when you remember that your view is always partial, information is always framed, and time changes what things mean. The next chapter adds pressure to that insight. Once you see events more clearly, you also need to remember that your own actions push back on the world and invite a response.
