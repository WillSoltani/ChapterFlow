# Relativity

Maya slid the printout across the conference table and asked for approval before lunch. Owen frowned almost at once. The plan looked weak to him: slower hiring, a longer testing window, and a short-term drop in sales targets. From his seat, it sounded like retreat. Maya turned the page and showed him the supplier delays he had not seen, the customer complaints she had been tracking, and the compliance deadline sitting six weeks ahead. What looked timid from one seat looked responsible from another. The facts had not changed. The view had.

That is the discipline behind relativity as a mental model. The chapter is not saying truth disappears because people disagree. It is saying that your first interpretation is shaped by where you stand. Role, information, incentives, and time pressure all influence what feels obvious. So do the blind spots you do not notice while you are inside them. When another person's move looks irrational, the useful first question is not "what is wrong with them?" It is "what would the world have to look like for that choice to make sense from where they stand?"

That question sharpens judgment because it interrupts an easy mistake. We often treat disagreement as proof of defect. Someone must be careless, selfish, or slow. Sometimes that verdict is deserved. Often it is just premature. The difference comes from constraints, information, or timescale. A founder reads the next financing round. An engineer reads failure risk. A teacher watches the whole room while a student feels one public moment. A parent sees a ten-year consequence while a teenager sees tonight. The event is the same. The meaning shifts because the observers are not standing in the same place.

Relativity also helps when you are reading information instead of people. A report is shaped by the person who wrote it. An article is shaped by what it chooses to foreground. A plan is shaped by the risk its author is trying to avoid. If you forget that, you start reacting to the frame as if it were neutral reality. A narrow metric becomes a complete strategy. A loud headline becomes a durable belief. The better move is to inspect the frame. Who chose these facts? What got left out? What position or pressure does this arrangement quietly reveal?

Time creates another kind of relativity. A bad quarter can look like collapse if you judge it from too short a distance. A delayed reply can look like contempt if you freeze the story in the first hour. A hard teacher can look unfair in September and disciplined in May. Extending the timeline does not make every decision look better. It makes the interpretation less cramped. Some actions only reveal their value later. Others look harmless until their long tail comes due. If you want a truer reading, you often need a wider clock.

That is why this chapter works as the opening move of the book. Before you can reason well about leverage, incentives, or cooperation, you need a more disciplined way to read what is in front of you. Relativity trains that habit. Reconstruct the other seat. Inspect the frame. Stretch the timeline. Then decide. Those moves do not abolish standards. A manipulative explanation remains manipulative after you understand it. A distorted report is still distorted after you see how it was assembled. But your judgment gets better because you are no longer confusing your angle with the whole event.

The model has a limit, and the limit matters. Perspective-taking can turn into polished hesitation. You can keep widening the frame until every decision feels unfinished. You can keep honoring complexity until nothing gets chosen. The chapter does not ask for that. It asks for disciplined interpretation before commitment. Widen the frame enough to stop being fooled by your first reading, then accept the burden of judgment.

Once you start using relativity this way, ordinary conflicts stop looking so flat. A tense email might signal disrespect, or it might expose a rushed manager, a hidden fear, or a metric that is distorting behavior. A student's resistance might be laziness, or it might be shame, confusion, or a classroom signal you missed. A proposal that sounds foolish may be guarding against a risk you have not priced in. The model does not make you vague. It makes you harder to mislead, especially by your own certainty.

That is the chapter's practical promise. Better judgment begins when you remember that your view is partial, information comes framed, and time changes what events mean. The next chapter adds a second pressure: once you start acting inside the world, the world answers back.
