False causality appears when the mind sees one event followed by another and decides that the first one caused the second. Timing feels persuasive, and repetition feels even more persuasive.

That is the trap. Two things can occur together without one producing the other. A result can follow an action because of coincidence, background conditions, or some third factor that is doing the real work. But the mind dislikes loose ends, so it quickly turns sequence into cause.

This shows up everywhere. A team changes a process and sales rise, so the change gets credit before anyone checks what else shifted. A student uses a ritual before an exam and then performs well, so the ritual starts looking powerful. A person takes a supplement, feels better, and assumes the supplement must be the reason even though rest, timing, or another factor may be involved.

The chapter does not deny that causes exist. It warns that pattern alone is weak proof. Repeated association can still mislead, and a before-after story can still be false if alternative explanations have not been tested.

The practical correction is to ask what evidence would show real causal force. What else changed? What other explanation fits? Would the same outcome appear without the supposed cause? Those questions do not make judgment paralyzed. They stop a convenient story from becoming false certainty.

Better judgment keeps the gap clear between something happening with an outcome and something truly causing it.
