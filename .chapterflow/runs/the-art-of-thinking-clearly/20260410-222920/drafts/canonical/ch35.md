Winning can feel like proof that your judgment was right. In competitive settings, however, the win itself can sometimes be the warning.

That is the winner's curse. If many people are estimating the value of something, the person who wins may be the one who judged it most aggressively. The victory can mean not that the target was great, but that your estimate ran higher than everyone else's.

This is easy to miss because winning feels validating. The bid succeeds, the offer is accepted, the contest is over, and the mind wants to treat that outcome as confirmation. But the chapter points to a narrower possibility: the win may contain negative information about the price, the estimate, or the terms you accepted.

The trouble starts when victory hides overpayment. A company wins the deal because it valued the target too richly. A buyer gets the item because everyone else saw the price as too high. A person secures the opportunity because they offered more than the situation really justified. The problem is not that every win is bad. The problem is that winning can itself be evidence that your judgment overshot.

The practical correction is to ask what the win implies. If I won, what does that suggest about how my estimate compared with everyone else's? That question turns the outcome from a celebration cue into a diagnostic clue.

The chapter does not ask for fear of all competition. It asks for cleaner interpretation. A win is not automatically proof of value if the very act of winning may mean you were the most optimistic person in the room.
