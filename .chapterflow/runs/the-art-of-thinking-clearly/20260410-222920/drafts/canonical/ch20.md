People often judge a decision by how it ended. If the result was good, the decision looks smart. If the result was bad, the decision looks foolish. The visible outcome takes over the verdict.

That is outcome bias. The quality of the decision process and the quality of the outcome get blurred together. A result can be flattering or punishing because luck, timing, and uncertainty still have a say, but people often treat the final payoff as if it settled the question completely.

This is tempting because outcomes are vivid and easy to remember. Process quality is harder to see. Good reasoning, careful tradeoffs, and limited information at the time are less dramatic than a big win or an obvious loss. The result feels like the cleanest summary of the choice.

The trouble begins when weak decisions get praised for lucky wins and sound decisions get condemned for unlucky losses. An investment can pay off even if the reasoning was sloppy. A careful strategy can fail even if it was well judged under the information available. The problem is not that outcomes are irrelevant. The problem is letting outcomes overrule process too completely.

The practical correction is to inspect the decision before inspecting the verdict. What information was available? What alternatives were considered? What risks and tradeoffs were understood at the time? Those questions help separate decision quality from result quality.

The chapter does not excuse bad results. It asks for a cleaner standard: the outcome is evidence, but it is not the whole verdict on whether the decision was wise. Better judgment reviews the process before letting the payoff write the story.
