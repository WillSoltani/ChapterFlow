An early win can feel like proof that you naturally understand what you are doing. You win your first round, make money fast, get praise on the first attempt, or succeed in a new setting, and the mind quickly turns the result into a story about lasting ability.

That is beginner's luck. A good early outcome arrives before enough evidence exists to tell whether the performance came from repeatable skill, favorable conditions, or simple chance.

The danger is not the success itself. The danger is what gets built on top of it. One win can create confidence, identity, and appetite for bigger bets long before the person has faced repetition, tougher conditions, or bad variance. Luck gets promoted into competence too quickly.

This happens in games, investing, work, school, and hobbies. A beginner wins once and feels talented. An early trade works and feels like insight. A first presentation goes well and feels like permanent ease. Sometimes real ability is present. But one result cannot prove stability on its own.

The chapter does not say early success is meaningless. It can be encouraging. It can reveal potential. But it is weaker evidence than people want it to be.

The correction is to ask what would still hold under repetition, pressure, and less favorable conditions. Better judgment treats the first win as an outcome, not yet as reliable proof of durable skill.
