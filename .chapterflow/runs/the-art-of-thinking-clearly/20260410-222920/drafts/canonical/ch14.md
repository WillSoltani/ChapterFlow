After an outcome happens, people often start talking as if it had been obvious all along. A project failure looks inevitable. A hiring mistake looks easy to spot. A market move looks predictable. Once the answer is known, uncertainty begins to disappear from memory.

That is hindsight bias. It is the tendency to see events as having been more predictable after the fact than they really were beforehand. Knowing the ending changes how the earlier scene is remembered. The mind starts drawing a cleaner line from the past to the result than anyone could honestly see while the situation was still open.

This bias is attractive because known outcomes are tidy. They remove tension. Once the result is fixed, the earlier facts can be reorganized around it. Details that fit the ending become more vivid. Alternatives that were once live begin to fade. The event starts looking less like one uncertain path among many and more like the path that was always coming.

That is why postmortems, exam reviews, and personal judgments can become unfair so quickly. A team can say the warning signs were obvious once the launch fails. A student can feel the correct answer should have been clear once the answer key appears. A person can say they knew a relationship or purchase would end badly once it actually does. The result may teach something, but it can also distort memory of what was knowable beforehand.

The practical correction is to reconstruct the decision before the outcome arrived. What information was genuinely available then? What alternatives were still plausible? What did the situation look like before the ending turned uncertainty into certainty? Those questions help separate learning from memory distortion.

The chapter does not say outcomes are useless. It says that knowing the result now is not the same as having known it in advance. Better judgment resists the temptation to let the ending rewrite how open the situation really was.
