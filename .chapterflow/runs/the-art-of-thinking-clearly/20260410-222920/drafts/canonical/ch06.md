Someone gives you a small gift, useful advice, a free sample, or an unexpected favor, and the room changes. The later request may be modest or substantial, but it no longer feels like a clean request. Refusing begins to feel rude. The original gesture starts creating pressure behind the next decision.

That is the reciprocity effect in judgment. Human beings are built to return what they receive. Often that is healthy. Social life would be colder and thinner without some willingness to answer kindness with kindness. The mistake begins when repayment pressure becomes stronger than independent evaluation of what the return response actually deserves.

The mechanism is quiet. A person who has done you a favor no longer feels like a neutral actor. A concession in negotiation, a helpful introduction, a favor at work, a classmate sharing notes, or a small gift from a salesperson can all create a feeling of debt. The debt may not be spoken, but it can still steer behavior. People begin saying yes because saying no now feels ungrateful rather than because yes is the best choice.

You can see this in work, school, sales, and ordinary relationships. A manager may grant flexibility and then receive easier agreement later. A student may feel pushed to support a group decision because someone helped them first. A customer may treat a free sample like a reason to buy. A friend may offer a favor that quietly narrows your freedom to refuse a later ask. In each case, gratitude may be real. But gratitude is not the same thing as obligation to comply.

That is the practical distinction. A favor can deserve appreciation without buying authority over your judgment. The right question is whether you would still agree to the request if the earlier gesture had not happened. If the answer changes only because of the favor, reciprocity may be doing hidden work that the merits of the situation cannot do on their own.

The chapter does not say people should become suspicious of all kindness. Reciprocity helps bind trust, cooperation, and everyday generosity. The problem is not return itself. The problem is when return pressure outranks what is fair, wise, or wanted in the present moment.

Reciprocity matters because it makes obligation feel like character. People want to seem decent, fair, and appreciative, so they often obey the pressure before they notice it. Better judgment separates gratitude from compliance and lets a favor remain a favor instead of letting it quietly write the next decision.
