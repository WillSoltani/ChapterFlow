Sometimes people stay calm while things are clearly getting worse because they tell themselves that improvement always feels bad at first. A failing rollout, a chaotic class method, a painful routine, or a draining change process gets defended with the same comforting idea: this is just the hard middle, so the badness must be a sign that progress has started.

That is the it'll-get-worse-before-it-gets-better fallacy. It mistakes temporary pain, confusion, or deterioration for evidence of future improvement. The key mistake is not noticing that difficulty can accompany progress without proving it. Some changes are painful because they are working. Others are painful because they are poorly designed, misdirected, or simply failing.

This fallacy is attractive because struggle feels meaningful. If something costs time, energy, comfort, or confidence, people want to believe the cost is buying something. Pain starts looking like validation. Friction starts looking like seriousness. The harder the experience feels, the easier it becomes to narrate the suffering as a necessary stage on the way to a payoff.

But worsening conditions are not self-explaining. A team can adopt a messy process that slows work and call the chaos a sign that deeper transformation is happening. A student can stay confused for weeks and assume the confusion proves the learning is becoming more advanced. A person can endure a disruptive habit change and assume the discomfort itself is evidence that the new routine must be effective. The same pattern keeps repeating: the pain is real, but the link from pain to progress is assumed rather than shown.

The practical correction is to ask a more demanding question: what mechanism connects this current worsening to a later improvement? What evidence shows that the present cost is buying something specific rather than merely imposing strain? That question does not reject all hard transitions. It rejects using hardship itself as proof.

Difficulty sometimes is part of growth. The mistake is treating difficulty as if it automatically certifies growth. Better judgment separates painful noise from meaningful investment and asks whether the worsening has earned the story being told about it.
