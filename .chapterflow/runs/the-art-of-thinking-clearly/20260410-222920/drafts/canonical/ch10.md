The same thing can look different depending on what you saw just before it. A reasonable price feels expensive after a bargain. An ordinary room feels elegant after a cramped one. A decent candidate looks exceptional after a weak interview. The target may not have changed at all. The comparison frame did.

That is the contrast effect. People often think they are judging an item on its own merits, but nearby comparisons quietly change the standard. Value, size, quality, difficulty, and attractiveness can all shift because the mind is measuring relatively rather than absolutely.

The mechanism is simple and common. A strong option makes the next option look weaker. A weak option makes the next option look stronger. A hard assignment can make the following one feel easy. A cheap product can make the next price feel steep. The judgment gets pulled by sequence and proximity.

This is why the chapter matters in ordinary decisions. Hiring, shopping, grading, negotiation, product design, and daily choice architecture all become vulnerable when the mind forgets that it is comparing rather than simply evaluating. A neutral target can inherit praise or blame from what stood next to it.

The point is not that comparison should never happen. People need comparisons in order to choose. The problem begins when the nearby frame quietly takes over and gets mistaken for the target's real value. Then the judgment feels objective even though sequence is doing part of the work.

The practical correction is to isolate the target and ask what it looks like on its own. What would this price, person, room, proposal, or option feel like without the item that came right before it? That question exposes whether the evaluation belongs to the object or to the comparison sequence.

Contrast effect matters because it can make ordinary differences look dramatic and neutral things look special or disappointing for the wrong reason. Better judgment notices the frame before trusting the feeling created by it.
