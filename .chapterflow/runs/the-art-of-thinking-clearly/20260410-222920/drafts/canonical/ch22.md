Liking someone makes judgment feel easier. If a person, brand, or source feels warm, familiar, or admirable, their actions and ideas can start looking better too. The feeling spills into the verdict.

That is liking bias. Affection softens scrutiny, and dislike sharpens it. A judgment that should be about the quality of a decision, product, or idea becomes partly about how the evaluator feels toward the source.

This is tempting because feeling is fast. Liking someone makes trust feel natural. Disliking them makes criticism feel justified. The emotional signal arrives before a slower, cleaner evaluation has fully formed.

The trouble begins when warm feeling hides flaws and cold feeling exaggerates them. A liked coworker can get too much credit. A disliked classmate can be judged too harshly. A brand, celebrity, or friend can seem more right or more wrong than the evidence really supports. The problem is not that relationships never matter. The problem is letting affection or dislike write too much of the verdict.

The practical correction is to separate the object from the source. What exactly is being judged here, and would the verdict still look the same if the person or brand attached to it were neutral? Those questions slow the spillover.

The chapter does not ask for emotional emptiness. It asks for cleaner judgment: liking a person or source is not the same as accurately evaluating what they did. Better judgment keeps the feeling visible so it cannot quietly take over the score.
