A coincidence can feel heavier than it is. Two events line up, two details match, or one thing happens just after another, and the mind rushes to treat the overlap as meaningful. Chance feels too thin for something that feels striking.

That is the error around coincidence. A surprising overlap is not automatically evidence of hidden intent, destiny, or a deeper pattern. Sometimes separate events simply meet.

This is tempting because the mind likes patterns and dislikes empty space. Meaning feels satisfying. Chance can feel lazy, cold, or incomplete even when it is enough to explain what happened.

The trouble begins when surprise gets promoted into proof. A manager sees timing overlap and assumes hidden coordination. A student sees repeated test topics and thinks the teacher is sending a message. A person notices repeated numbers, birthdays, or chance meetings and starts reading them as signs. The problem is not that unusual events never matter. The problem is letting emotional impact turn coincidence into evidence too quickly.

The practical correction is to widen the frame. How many opportunities were there for this overlap to happen? How often do similar events occur without being noticed? Those questions restore proportion and make chance easier to accept when it is enough.

The chapter does not ask for flat indifference to every unusual event. It asks for cleaner judgment: a striking coincidence is not the same as a meaningful signal. Better judgment counts opportunities before it writes a story.
