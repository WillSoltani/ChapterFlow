People often explain behavior by character first. Someone seems lazy, selfish, principled, careless, or disciplined. But before those explanations settle, another force deserves inspection: what the system is rewarding.

That is the core of incentive super-response tendency. Incentives do not merely push behavior a little. They can reshape what people notice, what they ignore, what they justify, and what they come to treat as normal. The payoff structure can pull harder than surface explanations admit.

This is tempting to miss because moral stories are easier to tell than system stories. It feels cleaner to blame personality or praise virtue than to trace how rewards are bending conduct. But people often respond strongly to what gets paid, promoted, measured, or socially rewarded, even when they describe their choices in more honorable or personal language.

The trouble begins when incentives hide in plain sight. A sales team can oversell because the target rewards volume over fit. A student can chase grades in ways that weaken learning because the metric rewards performance signals more than mastery. A person can post, buy, or signal things for attention while telling a different story about the motive. The problem is not that character never matters. The problem is that incentives can reorganize behavior more deeply than people admit.

The practical correction is to inspect the reward structure before trusting the visible explanation. What gets rewarded here? What gets punished? What kind of conduct becomes easier to defend once those payoffs are in place? Those questions make behavior easier to read.

The chapter does not ask for cynicism. It asks for sharper realism: before judging conduct only by stated reasons or visible personality, look at the incentives. Better judgment checks what the system is paying for before deciding why people are acting the way they do.
