The moment something becomes mine, parting with it can start feeling strangely expensive. An object that looked ordinary before ownership can begin to feel more valuable once it sits in my room, my bag, my desk, or my account.

That is the endowment effect. Possession changes perception. Ownership itself starts acting like evidence that the thing is worth more, even when the object has not changed.

This is easy to miss because ownership feels intimate and concrete. Once something is mine, giving it up feels like a loss, while keeping it feels natural. The same item can look different from the inside of ownership than it did from the outside.

The trouble begins when possession quietly inflates the price. A seller asks more than a buyer would pay. A founder overvalues a product feature just because the team built it. A student clings too tightly to an idea or project because it feels more special once it belongs to them. The problem is not that use, effort, or familiarity never matter. The problem is letting ownership write too much of the valuation.

The practical correction is to price or judge the object from outside ownership. If this were not already mine, what would I honestly pay for it now? What evidence supports the value besides the fact that I possess it? Those questions weaken the distortion.

The chapter does not ask for detachment from everything you own. It asks for cleaner judgment: owning something is not proof that it deserves a higher price. Better judgment keeps possession from quietly inflating the score.
