People often work differently when their effort is visible than when it disappears into a group. When no one can clearly tell who carried what, personal effort can quietly slip.

That is social loafing. Shared responsibility can weaken individual contribution because accountability feels spread thin. The group still exists, the task still matters, but the connection between one person's effort and the final result gets blurry.

This is easy to miss because it can look like simple teamwork. Sometimes teamwork is working well. But the chapter points to a narrower distortion: when contribution is hard to isolate, people may work less than they would if their role were visible and owned.

The trouble starts when group structure hides effort. A team project lets some work drift because everyone assumes someone else will cover it. A class assignment becomes uneven because no one owns a specific piece clearly enough. A shared household or volunteer task stalls because responsibility belongs to everyone in theory and to no one in practice. The problem is not that groups are bad. The problem is that diffused accountability can lower effort without anyone announcing that it has happened.

The practical correction is to make contribution specific and visible. Who owns this part, what does completion look like, and how will effort be seen? Those questions restore personal connection between action and result.

The chapter does not ask for individualism instead of cooperation. It asks for cleaner ownership inside cooperation. If effort would change the moment contribution became attributable, the group structure is probably inviting loafing.
