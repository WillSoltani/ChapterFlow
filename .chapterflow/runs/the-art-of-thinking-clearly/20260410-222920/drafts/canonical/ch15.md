People often trust certainty too quickly. A confident estimate, answer, or opinion can feel persuasive simply because it sounds settled. The force of the conviction starts standing in for the force of the evidence.

That is the overconfidence effect. It appears when people feel or project more certainty than their information can honestly support. Strong confidence gets confused with strong grounding. Feeling sure starts looking like knowing enough.

This is tempting because confidence is efficient. It speeds decisions, reduces hesitation, and makes a judgment easier to defend. Once someone sounds certain, others can stop asking how much evidence is really underneath the claim.

The trouble begins when certainty outruns support. A team can give a forecast with more confidence than the data justify. A student can defend an answer too strongly before checking the weak spots. A person can make a purchase, health guess, or travel plan with conviction that exceeds what is actually known. The problem is not confidence by itself. The problem is confidence that has escaped calibration.

The practical correction is to separate the feeling of certainty from the evidence that earns it. What facts support this level of confidence? What is known, what is inferred, and what remains unclear? Those questions lower the risk that conviction will start pretending to be proof.

The chapter does not demand paralysis or timid thinking. It demands a cleaner standard: confidence should rise and fall with support. Better judgment acts when needed, but it does not let the strength of a feeling certify the strength of a claim.
