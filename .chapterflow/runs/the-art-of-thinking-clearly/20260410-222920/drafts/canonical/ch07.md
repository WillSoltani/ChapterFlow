You reach a conclusion, and the world starts looking more cooperative. The articles that match your view feel relevant. The examples that support it feel easy to remember. The conversations that confirm it seem clear, while the conflicting material suddenly looks less convincing, less central, or less worth the trouble. The conclusion does not merely sit at the end of your reasoning. It starts shaping what enters the reasoning process in the first place.

That is confirmation bias in its first form. People usually describe bias as a problem of bad argument, but the distortion often starts earlier than argument. An initial view changes attention, search, and selection. Evidence that fits feels more visible and more usable. Evidence that challenges the view creates friction. The result is not only that people defend conclusions. It is that they begin collecting a tilted set of inputs from which those conclusions will later seem to follow naturally.

The mechanism is often quiet. A manager who already believes a candidate is strong keeps noticing signs of polish and initiative while downplaying warnings. A student who thinks a theory is correct keeps highlighting examples that support it and moving quickly past awkward counterexamples. A person with a strong first impression of a friend, purchase, or plan keeps finding little signs that the impression was right all along. The mind does not need to invent evidence from nothing. It only needs to greet matching evidence more warmly than conflicting evidence.

That is what makes this chapter dangerous. Confidence can begin feeling like clarity even when the evidence stream has already been filtered. Once a conclusion gains a head start, confirming inputs arrive with less resistance. Disconfirming inputs do not always get rejected openly. Often they just get noticed later, treated as less representative, or left outside the mental file marked relevant. The conclusion begins receiving cleaner nourishment than it deserves.

The chapter does not say prior beliefs are always irrational. People need working assumptions in order to think at all. The problem is narrower. A belief becomes dangerous when it quietly edits what gets admitted as evidence. Then the conclusion is no longer being tested by the world in a fair way. It is being protected from the world by a biased intake system.

The practical correction is to ask a harder question before collecting more support: what evidence would count against my current view? That question interrupts the easy slide toward only noticing what fits. It makes disconfirming material part of the standard rather than an annoyance at the margins.

Confirmation bias matters because it can make weak conclusions feel well supported. Better judgment does not only ask whether the conclusion sounds persuasive. It asks whether the evidence stream was screened before the conclusion claimed victory.
