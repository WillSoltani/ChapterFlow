People relax when an event fits a clear story. A neat explanation ties motives, causes, and outcomes together in a way that feels finished. Once the pieces click into place, the mind often stops asking whether the explanation is well supported. Coherence starts feeling like truth.

That is story bias. It is the tendency to prefer a satisfying narrative over a messier but more honest reality. A clean story feels safer than uncertainty. It feels more intelligent than loose facts. It feels complete. But that feeling of completeness can outrun the evidence.

This bias is attractive because uncertainty is tiring. A polished explanation gives events shape and direction. It removes awkward gaps. It turns mixed causes into one clear plot. A founder succeeds because of vision. A product fails because of one mistake. A relationship changed because of one turning point. The story becomes easy to remember and easy to repeat.

The problem is not storytelling itself. Stories help people understand and communicate. The problem begins when narrative quality starts replacing evidence quality. A neat account can hide missing data, ignored causes, and open questions. The explanation sounds stronger than the facts that support it.

That is why polished postmortems, classroom essays, and personal self-explanations can all become misleading. A team can tell a smooth failure story that ignores messy contributing factors. A student can force a historical event into one elegant thesis. A person can explain a painful change with one dramatic cause because the simpler story is easier to hold than a tangled reality.

The practical correction is to ask what evidence supports the story and what uncertainty the story hides. What facts are doing real work here, and what has been rounded away so the explanation can sound complete? Better judgment does not reject stories. It refuses to confuse narrative comfort with proof.
