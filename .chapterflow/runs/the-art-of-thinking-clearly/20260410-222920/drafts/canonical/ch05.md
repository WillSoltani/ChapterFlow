You buy a ticket for a bad movie and stay through the second half because leaving would make the first half feel wasted. You keep funding a weak project because too much has already gone into it. You stay in a course, hobby, subscription, or relationship partly because walking away now would force you to admit that earlier time, money, or hope did not buy what you expected. The past starts demanding loyalty from the future.

That is the sunk cost fallacy. A sunk cost is a cost that has already been paid and cannot be recovered. It may be emotionally real, financially painful, and practically frustrating. But none of that automatically makes it a good reason to keep investing. The error begins when prior spending gets treated like forward evidence.

The mechanism is simple and stubborn. People hate waste, and stopping can feel like signing a confession that the earlier decision was wrong. Continuing gives temporary relief. You do not have to admit the loss yet. You can tell yourself the previous cost will still be justified if you just add a little more time, effort, or money. That is how yesterday's investment starts buying votes in today's decision even though yesterday's money is gone either way.

You can see this in work, school, and personal life. Teams keep weak initiatives alive because canceling them would make the earlier budget look embarrassing. Students stay on a path because they have already worked too hard to switch. Individuals keep paying for something they no longer want because stopping feels like admitting that the original purchase was a mistake. In each case, the past cost is real. But the real question is whether more investment improves the future from here.

That is the practical correction. Ask what you would choose if you were standing at the current moment with no emotional claim from the earlier spending. If you had not already paid, would you pay now? If you had not already committed months to it, would you still commit the next month? Those questions do not erase the pain of wasted effort. They stop the pain from recruiting more waste on its behalf.

The chapter does not say that every difficult commitment should be abandoned. Some projects deserve persistence. Some courses become worthwhile after a rough stretch. Some relationships deserve repair work. But the reason to continue must come from present and future value, not from the fact that leaving now would make the past feel worse.

Sunk cost fallacy matters because it turns loss into a trap. People try to rescue what is already gone by sacrificing what is still available. Better judgment accepts the old cost as unrecoverable and makes the next decision from the next decision, not from a bargain with the past.
