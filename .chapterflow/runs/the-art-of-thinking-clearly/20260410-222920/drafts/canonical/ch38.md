The halo effect appears when one visible strength or flaw starts coloring everything else. A person seems charming, polished, beautiful, awkward, or irritating, and the mind quietly lets that one feature decide a much wider judgment.

That is the distortion. The original trait may be real, but the extra conclusions riding behind it may not be. A polished speaker may also seem wiser than the evidence supports. One sloppy mistake may make a capable person look broadly unreliable. The mind moves from one signal to a whole verdict.

This happens because a salient impression is easier to carry than a careful list of separate judgments. Once admiration or dislike gets momentum, unrelated traits start receiving borrowed credit or borrowed blame. A beautiful product looks better designed. A charming candidate looks more competent. An awkward classmate looks less intelligent. One visible feature starts ruling areas it never actually proved.

The chapter does not deny that first impressions contain information. It warns that one real quality can spread too far. The impressive trait may belong only to one domain, and the flaw may be narrower than the broader dislike attached to it.

The practical correction is to separate the original observation from the extra claims you are tempted to make. What exactly did I see? What does that evidence support? What am I inferring that the original trait does not actually prove? Those questions cut the spillover.

Better judgment keeps each claim on its own evidence instead of letting one bright impression govern the whole file.
