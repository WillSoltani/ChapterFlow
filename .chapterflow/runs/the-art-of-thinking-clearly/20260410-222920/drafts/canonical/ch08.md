You put a label on someone, and the person starts becoming easier to read in one direction. The reliable coworker keeps producing evidence of reliability. The difficult student keeps producing proof of difficulty. The careless neighbor keeps leaving traces that seem to confirm what you already thought. Contradictory behavior does not always disappear, but it often shrinks into an exception. The label begins deciding what counts as representative.

That is confirmation bias in its social form. In Part 1, the bias filtered what evidence reached a conclusion. Here it extends into judgments about people and groups. Once a category settles into place, matching behavior feels typical and easy to remember. Conflicting behavior feels accidental, temporary, or somehow less revealing. The stereotype survives not only because people hold it, but because they keep feeding it the kind of evidence it prefers.

The mechanism can look ordinary rather than cruel. A manager decides one employee is dependable and another is volatile. After that, the dependable employee's mistakes get filed as unusual while the volatile employee's mistakes get filed as proof. A teacher decides one student is gifted and another is lazy. The gifted student's lapse becomes a bad day; the lazy student's lapse becomes character. A person forms a fast impression of a neighborhood, family, or friend group and then keeps gathering confirming details while treating counterexamples as noise. The mind does not need a speech about stereotypes. It only needs a label that quietly edits interpretation.

That is why this chapter is more than a warning about rudeness. A social label becomes dangerous when it stops being a tentative summary and starts acting like a lens. Then new evidence no longer arrives cleanly. Behavior is read through the existing expectation. Tone, delay, effort, competence, and trustworthiness all start getting interpreted inside the frame that came first. The label begins producing the stability it seems to discover.

The problem is not that all pattern recognition is forbidden. People need provisional judgments in order to navigate the world. The problem is that those judgments harden too early and then defend themselves by selective interpretation. Once conflicting evidence is regularly demoted to an exception, the person or group under judgment stops getting a fair read.

The practical correction is to force an individuating question before repeating the label: what specific behavior would make me weaken this view? What evidence would count as representative if I truly wanted to revise it? Those questions interrupt the comfort of social sorting. They require you to treat contradictory behavior as possible information rather than a nuisance.

Confirmation bias matters here because stereotypes can feel accurate simply by being self-fed. Better judgment does not only ask whether the label feels familiar. It asks whether the label has been deciding what later behavior is allowed to mean.
