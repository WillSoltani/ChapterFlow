When another person behaves badly, strangely, or lazily, judgment often jumps straight to character. We tell ourselves that the person is rude, careless, weak, selfish, or unreliable.

That is the fundamental attribution error. The behavior is visible, the person is visible, and the surrounding situation is harder to see. Because the actor stands in front of us while the pressure around the actor stays partly hidden, the mind explains the conduct through personality faster than through circumstance.

This does not mean character never matters. It means observers often reach for character too early. A harsh email may come from arrogance, but it may also come from overload, fear, or pressure. A missed deadline may reflect irresponsibility, but it may also reflect unclear instructions, conflicting demands, or a bad system. A student's silence may look like indifference when it is really confusion, exhaustion, or embarrassment.

The chapter's warning is about explanation. Once a trait story forms, it feels complete. The rude driver becomes a rude person. The curt coworker becomes a difficult person. The unresponsive friend becomes a selfish person. Context disappears and the mind acts as if behavior had spoken clearly enough on its own.

The practical correction is to ask what the situation is doing before deciding what the person is. What pressures, incentives, constraints, expectations, or blind spots could be shaping the conduct? That question does not excuse every action. It simply prevents the first explanation from hardening into a false certainty.

Better judgment keeps both possibilities alive a little longer. Character may matter, but situation must be examined before behavior is treated as a clean window into character.
