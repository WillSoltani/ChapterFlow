A detailed forecast can feel safer than it really is. Once numbers, dates, milestones, and causes are laid out with confidence, the future starts sounding less uncertain than it actually remains.

That is the forecast illusion. Precision creates the feeling of knowledge. A plan with exact timing or a prediction with specific figures can seem trustworthy not because uncertainty has disappeared, but because the forecast sounds concrete and complete.

This happens in work, school, and personal life. A launch date feels dependable because the schedule looks polished. A budget forecast seems solid because the numbers are specific. A study plan sounds reliable because every step has been arranged. A life plan feels realistic because the story connecting its stages is smooth. In each case, detail can hide how much the forecast still does not know.

The chapter does not say planning is useless. It says a detailed forecast is not the same as a dependable one. The future does not owe obedience to a polished model, a coherent narrative, or an exact date.

The practical correction is to ask what the forecast leaves uncertain. What range of outcomes is still possible? What variables are being simplified, ignored, or treated as stable even though they may not be? That question reopens uncertainty where false precision tried to close it.

Better judgment keeps the plan, but it also keeps respect for what the forecast cannot actually see.
