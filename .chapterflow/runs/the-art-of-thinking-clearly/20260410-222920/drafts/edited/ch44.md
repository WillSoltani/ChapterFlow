Doing nothing can feel morally lighter than doing something harmful. If harm arrives through passivity, omission, or failure to intervene, people often judge it more gently than similar harm caused by direct action.

That is omission bias. Visible agency attracts blame. Passive agency hides more easily. When someone acts and causes damage, the mind sees a clear line from person to outcome. When someone fails to act, that line can feel softer even if the result is still serious.

This happens in work, school, and personal life. A person may hesitate to warn, report, stop, or intervene because not acting feels less blameworthy than acting and causing conflict. A family may tolerate a harmful pattern because confronting it feels harsher than letting it continue. In each case, omission feels cleaner than commission.

The chapter does not say action and inaction are always morally identical. Outcomes, duties, and context matter. But it does say that passivity should not automatically receive a moral discount just because it feels less active.

The practical correction is to compare the consequences of action and inaction on the same scale. If the omission leads to similar harm, the softer feeling around passivity may be misleading.

Better judgment asks not only what was done, but also what was allowed to happen.
