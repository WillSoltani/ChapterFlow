A small pattern can feel stronger than it is. A few examples line up, the repeated result starts looking stable, and the mind begins turning that limited observation into a general rule.

That is induction. People move from a handful of cases to a much broader conclusion, often before the evidence has earned that leap. A pattern can feel settled while the sample beneath it is still thin.

This is easy to miss because repeated observations feel persuasive even when there are only a few of them. The mind likes closure, and a provisional pattern can quickly harden into something that feels like a law.

The trouble starts when thin evidence gets promoted too fast. A team sees a short market run and starts speaking as if the trend is proven. A student or teacher forms a broad judgment from a few performances. A person meets a small number of examples and turns them into a stable belief about how things always work. The problem is not that small samples teach nothing. The problem is treating them like they have already earned a sweeping claim.

The practical correction is to ask how much evidence is really carrying the rule. How many cases support this pattern, and how stable would it look under more data? Those questions slow the jump from observation to law.

The chapter does not ask for paralysis until perfect evidence arrives. It asks for cleaner judgment: seeing a few instances is not the same as knowing the rule. Better judgment lets patterns stay provisional until the evidence behind them grows stronger.
