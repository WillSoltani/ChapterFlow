Extreme results often do not stay extreme. After an unusually bad week, an unusually strong quarter, or an unusually high score, the next result often looks more ordinary. People are quick to attach that shift to whatever praise, punishment, coaching, or change happened in between.

That is the core of regression to the mean. When a result starts from an unusually high or low point, part of what made it extreme often does not repeat with the same force next time. The next outcome moves closer to a more typical level, and that natural softening invites a false causal story.

This is easy to miss because clean explanations feel satisfying. If a poor result is followed by criticism and then improvement, the criticism gets credit. If a brilliant result is followed by praise and then a decline, the praise gets blamed. But some of that movement may have happened anyway simply because the starting point was far from normal.

The trouble starts when ordinary variation gets mistaken for intervention effect. A manager can overcredit a tough conversation after a rebound. A teacher can overtrust punishment after a very weak score improves. A person can overpraise a new routine because things got better after a terrible stretch. The problem is not that feedback or changes never matter. The problem is claiming too much from one rebound that may have softened naturally.

The practical correction is to ask what would likely happen after an extreme result even without the latest action. What is the typical range here? How unusual was the starting point? Those questions add restraint before the story hardens into certainty.

The chapter does not deny causation. It asks for a cleaner standard: when the baseline is extreme, some movement toward ordinary levels may arrive on its own. Better judgment compares the rebound against natural variation before turning it into proof.
