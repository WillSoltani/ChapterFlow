What comes to mind fastest can feel truer than it really is. A dramatic failure, a vivid news story, a recent accident, or a memorable personal anecdote can make something seem common, dangerous, or important simply because it arrives in memory quickly. Ease of recall starts pretending to be evidence.

That is availability bias. People often judge probability and importance by what memory offers first rather than by what is most representative. Vividness, recency, and emotional intensity make some examples easy to retrieve, and the mind quietly treats that fluency as a signal of significance.

This is why dramatic examples distort judgment so easily. A recent travel scare can make flying feel unusually risky. One striking project collapse can dominate planning more than the many routine successes that are harder to recall. A memorable health story can outweigh calmer statistics. The example is not strong because it is typical. It is strong because it is available.

The point is not that memorable cases never matter. Some vivid examples are genuinely important warnings. The problem begins when recall speed gets mistaken for frequency, risk, or relevance. Then memory convenience outruns representative evidence.

The practical correction is to ask a harder question: what is typical here, not only memorable? What evidence represents the broader pattern rather than just the easiest story to retrieve? Those questions slow the leap from salience to truth.

Availability bias matters because memory is selective, and selectivity can feel objective from the inside. Better judgment notices when an example is easy to recall and then asks whether it has earned the right to dominate the judgment.
