A neat story can feel truer than a messy fact pattern. When events line up into one clean explanation, people often relax. The account feels complete, intelligent, and easy to repeat. A polished postmortem, a clear personal narrative, or a tidy historical explanation can all create the same illusion: because the story makes sense, it must be right.

That is story bias. It happens when narrative quality starts outranking evidence quality. A coherent explanation feels safer than uncertainty, so the mind starts favoring the account that ties everything together most smoothly. The story becomes persuasive not because it is fully supported, but because it is satisfying.

This is tempting because uncertainty is uncomfortable. Mixed causes, missing facts, and unresolved questions feel weak. A clean narrative removes that discomfort. It turns a scattered reality into a readable plot. One cause, one turning point, one lesson. The result is easier to remember and easier to defend.

The trouble starts when the story hides how much it left out. A team can tell a smooth account of why a project failed and ignore the messy chain of contributing factors. A student can write a strong thesis that flattens a complicated event into one simple cause. A person can explain a relationship or career shift with one dramatic reason because the fuller reality feels harder to hold.

The practical correction is to ask what facts support the story and what uncertainty the story is hiding. What evidence is solid, and what has been rounded away so the explanation can sound complete? A useful story can still help understanding, but it does not get to replace evidence.

The chapter does not reject stories. It rejects the habit of trusting them because they feel finished. Better judgment can use narrative as a tool while still asking whether the neat explanation has actually earned the confidence it creates.
