Once the outcome is known, it can start feeling as if it had been obvious from the beginning. A failed launch suddenly looks doomed, a wrong answer looks easy to avoid, and a bad purchase looks predictable. The ending becomes so visible that people forget how much uncertainty existed before it arrived.

That is hindsight bias. It happens when outcome knowledge rewrites memory of the earlier situation. People start seeing the result as more foreseeable and more inevitable than it really was while the decision was still open. Knowing what happened now gets confused with having been able to know it then.

This is tempting because fixed endings create order. Once the result is in place, the earlier facts can be rearranged around it. Warning signs that fit the ending become clearer. Alternative outcomes fade from view. The path that was once uncertain starts looking like the path that was always there.

The trouble starts when this distorts judgment. A team can criticize a past decision as obviously flawed once a project fails. A student can think the answer should have been clear only after seeing the key. A person can insist they knew a relationship, move, or purchase would end badly once the ending is already in hand. The outcome may teach something real, but it can also erase how open the situation actually was.

The practical correction is to rebuild the earlier moment before the ending took over. What was actually known then? What alternatives were still live? What signals were clear, and what only feels clear now because the result is already fixed? Those questions help recover the uncertainty that hindsight tries to flatten.

The chapter does not deny that some warning signs are real. It insists on a narrower discipline: knowing the result now is not the same thing as having known it before. Better judgment learns from outcomes without letting the ending rewrite the past into something that was never so obvious.
