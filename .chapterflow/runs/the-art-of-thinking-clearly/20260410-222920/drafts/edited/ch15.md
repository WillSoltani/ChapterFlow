Certainty can feel like evidence even when it is not. A confident forecast sounds stronger, a firm answer feels smarter, and a decisive opinion seems more trustworthy. The force of the conviction can start doing work that the facts have not actually done.

That is the overconfidence effect. It appears when people feel or project more certainty than their information can honestly support. Strong confidence gets mistaken for strong grounding. Feeling sure starts looking like knowing enough.

This is tempting because confidence is efficient. It speeds decisions, reduces hesitation, and makes a claim easier to defend. Once someone sounds settled, other people can stop asking how much support is really underneath the judgment.

The trouble starts when certainty outruns evidence. A team can state a forecast with more confidence than the data justify. A student can push an answer too hard before testing its weak spots. A person can make a purchase, health guess, or travel plan with conviction that exceeds what is actually known. The problem is not confidence itself. The problem is confidence that has slipped free from calibration.

The practical correction is to separate the feeling of certainty from the evidence that earns it. What facts support this level of confidence? What is known, what is inferred, and what remains unclear? Those questions help keep conviction from pretending to be proof.

The chapter does not demand hesitation for its own sake. It demands a cleaner standard: confidence should rise and fall with support. Better judgment can still act firmly, but it does not let the strength of a feeling certify the strength of a claim.
