When pressure rises, doing something can feel better than waiting. Movement looks responsible. Intervention looks brave. Standing still can feel passive, weak, or careless even when restraint would lead to the better outcome.

That is action bias. Under stress, action can feel like control. A visible move relieves the discomfort of uncertainty, so the mind starts rewarding movement even before it has proved useful.

This happens in work, school, and personal life. A manager changes the plan too quickly because doing nothing looks exposed. A student overcorrects after one bad result because waiting feels irresponsible. A person argues immediately, buys immediately, sells immediately, or intervenes immediately because motion feels safer than patience. In each case, the action reduces emotional tension first.

The chapter does not say inaction is always wise. Sometimes fast intervention is exactly right. But it does say that action is not automatically the better move just because it feels more active. Motion can be comforting without being effective.

The practical correction is to ask whether the action improves the outcome or only relieves the discomfort of doing nothing. That question separates useful intervention from emotional self-soothing.

Better judgment respects the possibility that the right move may be to wait, watch, or hold steady a little longer.
