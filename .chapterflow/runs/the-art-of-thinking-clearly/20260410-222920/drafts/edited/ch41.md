A richer story can feel more likely than a simpler truth. When a description has extra detail, personality fit, and a satisfying shape, the mind often treats it as more believable than a broader plain possibility.

That is the conjunction fallacy. Specificity creates coherence. Coherence creates confidence. A combined story can then feel stronger not because it is more likely, but because it sounds more complete and more representative of what we expect.

This happens in work, school, and personal life. A hiring manager may prefer the story that a candidate is both creative and unreliable because it fits a stereotype better than the simpler possibility that the candidate is just creative. A student may believe a classmate is both brilliant and socially withdrawn because the paired image feels vivid. A person may assume a friend is both upset and ready to cut contact because the dramatic version feels emotionally plausible. In each case, the richer story feels better matched to the picture in the mind.

The problem is that the detailed version must first live inside the broader one. If someone is both one thing and another, that person must first be the broader first thing at all. The narrower combined claim cannot outrank the larger parent case that contains it.

The practical correction is to compare the detailed story with the broader possibility before trusting the detailed one. Ask which claim covers more situations. Ask whether the richer version feels persuasive only because it is vivid, tailored, and easy to imagine. That check weakens the grip of narrative fit.

Better judgment does not confuse a satisfying picture with a more likely truth.
