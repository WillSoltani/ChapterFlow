People often judge a decision by how it turned out. If the result was good, the decision looks smart. If the result was bad, the decision looks foolish. The visible outcome starts writing the verdict.

That is outcome bias. The quality of the decision process and the quality of the final result get blurred together. A result can flatter or punish the choice because luck, timing, and uncertainty still matter, but people often treat the payoff as if it settled the question completely.

This is easy to miss because outcomes are vivid. Process quality is harder to see. Good reasoning, sensible tradeoffs, and the limits of the information available at the time are less dramatic than a win or a loss. The result feels like the cleanest summary of the choice.

The trouble starts when weak decisions get praised for lucky wins and sound decisions get condemned for unlucky losses. An investment can work even if the reasoning was sloppy. A careful strategy can fail even if it was well judged under the information available. The problem is not that outcomes do not matter. The problem is letting outcomes overrule process too completely.

The practical correction is to inspect the decision before inspecting the verdict. What information was available? What alternatives were considered? What risks and tradeoffs were understood at the time? Those questions help separate decision quality from result quality.

The chapter does not excuse bad results. It asks for a cleaner standard: the outcome is evidence, but it is not the whole verdict on whether the decision was wise. Better judgment reviews the process before letting the payoff write the story.
