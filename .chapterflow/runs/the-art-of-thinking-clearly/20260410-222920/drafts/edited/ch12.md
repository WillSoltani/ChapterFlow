Things can get worse before they get better, but that sequence does not prove that improvement is underway. People often tolerate confusion, disruption, or pain by telling themselves that the bad stretch is a necessary sign of progress. A messy reorganization, a draining habit change, or a chaotic learning method can all get protected by the same story: if this feels bad now, it must be working.

That is the it'll-get-worse-before-it-gets-better fallacy. It treats temporary deterioration as evidence of future gain without checking whether any real mechanism links the two. Some difficult transitions do lead somewhere better. Others are just waste, friction, or failure wearing the costume of progress.

The fallacy is tempting because struggle feels meaningful. Once people have paid in time, comfort, or confidence, they want the pain to mean something. Friction starts looking like seriousness. Confusion starts looking like depth. The worsening condition becomes emotionally easier to defend than to question.

This shows up in ordinary decisions. A team can defend a broken rollout as proof that transformation is happening. A student can treat ongoing confusion as evidence of deeper learning. A person can assume an unpleasant new routine is effective because it is unpleasant. In each case, the pain may be real, but the claim that it signals improvement has not yet been earned.

The practical correction is to ask what exactly connects the current cost to the promised benefit. What evidence shows that this disruption is producing capability, clarity, health, or some other real gain? If no mechanism or feedback supports the story, then the suffering may only be suffering.

The chapter does not deny that some worthwhile changes feel bad at first. It insists on a narrower standard: pain can accompany progress, but pain alone does not prove progress. Better judgment asks whether the difficulty is doing useful work or merely asking for blind faith.
