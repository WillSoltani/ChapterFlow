You read a page of complaints, a stack of glowing reviews, or a survey answered by volunteers and it feels as if the whole group has spoken. Often it has not. Only the people with enough reason to step forward have spoken.

That is self-selection bias. The sample is already distorted before interpretation begins because participation was voluntary. The people who answer, join, complain, attend, or rate something are often different from the people who stay silent.

This is why a room full of enthusiasts can make something seem universally loved, and a page full of angry comments can make something seem universally hated. Neither picture has to be fake. It can simply be incomplete in a predictable way. Strong feelings, strong incentives, extra time, and special interest make some people much more likely to appear.

The mistake is to treat visible participants as if they were a random slice of everyone else. They usually are not. The people missing from view may be less excited, less angry, less committed, less available, or simply less motivated to respond.

The chapter does not say feedback is worthless. Reviews, surveys, complaints, and sign-ups can all contain real signal. But they do not automatically represent the whole population.

The correction is to ask who had a reason to show up and who had no reason to appear. Better judgment begins there. Before trusting the pattern, ask what kind of person was likely to volunteer and what kind of person remained invisible.
