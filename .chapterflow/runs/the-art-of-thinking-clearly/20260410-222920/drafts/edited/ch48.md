A person, product, or idea can start looking better than it deserves because it stands next to something admired. It can also start looking worse than it deserves because it stands next to something disliked. The feeling travels through the link.

That is association bias. Judgment borrows emotion from nearby people, symbols, and outcomes. A celebrity can make a brand feel stronger than its actual quality. A scandal can stain people or ideas that did not create it. A respected group can make weak ideas sound smarter. A disliked label can make fair arguments look suspect.

The problem is that borrowed glow or borrowed stain starts doing work that evidence should be doing. Instead of evaluating the thing itself, the mind evaluates the feeling attached to what it is near.

This happens constantly. People trust messages more when they come from admired faces. They dismiss proposals faster when they are linked with someone they dislike. They assume a product is better because it carries prestige signals. They assume a person is worse because of an embarrassing connection.

The chapter does not say associations never matter. Context can matter. Relationships can matter. But linked feeling is not the same as earned merit.

The correction is to separate the object itself from the emotion traveling through the association. Better judgment asks: what am I actually evaluating here, and what nearby glow or stain am I letting do the work?
