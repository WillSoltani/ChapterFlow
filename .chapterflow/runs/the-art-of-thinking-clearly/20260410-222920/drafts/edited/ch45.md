Success often feels like proof of personal skill. Failure often feels like bad luck, bad timing, unfair conditions, or someone else's mistake. The story changes direction depending on whether the outcome protects or threatens self-image.

That is self-serving bias. Good results are easier to internalize. Bad results are easier to externalize. The mind bends explanation toward ego protection, so credit comes inward faster than blame does.

This happens in work, school, and personal life. A promotion feels earned by talent. A missed target feels caused by a bad market or weak support. A strong grade feels like proof of ability. A weak grade feels like unfair instructions or bad luck. A relationship conflict becomes someone else's sensitivity instead of one's own harshness. In each case, the explanation protects identity.

The chapter does not say people never deserve credit or that every excuse is false. Context matters. Luck matters. But it does say that success and failure should not be explained by two different standards just because one story flatters the self and the other does not.

The practical correction is to ask whether the same explanation would still sound convincing if the result had gone the other way. That question exposes whether the story is evidence-based or ego-protective.

Better judgment gives credit and blame by the same rules.
