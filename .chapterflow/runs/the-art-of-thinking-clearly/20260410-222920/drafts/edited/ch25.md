Group decisions can look stronger than they are. A room gets quiet, nobody objects, and the silence starts feeling like agreement. What may actually be happening is that people are protecting belonging more than they are protecting accuracy.

That is groupthink. The desire for unity, safety, or smooth coordination starts crowding out honest dissent. A group can begin to sound more certain than its members really are.

This is easy to miss because disagreement is costly. Speaking up can risk friction, embarrassment, delay, or social penalty. Staying quiet can feel easier, kinder, and safer, especially when the room already seems to be leaning one way.

The trouble starts when silence gets mistaken for consensus. A team approves a plan while private doubts stay underground. A student group rushes into one idea because no one wants to be the difficult voice. A family or committee locks into a choice because harmony feels more urgent than correction. The problem is not that cohesion never matters. The problem is letting belonging pressure do the deciding.

The practical correction is to make dissent normal before closure arrives. Ask what objections are missing, what risks have not been voiced, and what people would say if disagreement were clearly safe. Those questions create room for reality to re-enter the room.

The chapter does not argue for endless conflict. It argues for cleaner consensus: quiet agreement is not the same as a sound decision. Better judgment protects dissent before the group mistakes silence for truth.
