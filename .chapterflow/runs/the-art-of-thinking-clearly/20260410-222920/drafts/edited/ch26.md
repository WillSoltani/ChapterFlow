A dramatic possibility can take over the mind fast. The story of what might happen feels vivid, frightening, or exciting, and that story starts driving the reaction before anyone asks how likely it really is.

That is neglect of probability. People focus on the outcome itself while giving too little weight to the odds of that outcome. A possibility can feel large simply because it is emotionally sharp.

This is easy to miss because vivid scenarios are easy to imagine. Rare danger can feel urgent. Rare upside can feel irresistible. Once the mind starts picturing the outcome clearly, likelihood can fade into the background.

The trouble starts when importance replaces probability. A team overreacts to a low-odds risk because the downside is dramatic. A student panics over an unlikely failure because the image is vivid. A person chases a long-shot reward or fears a rare event because possibility has been mistaken for likelihood. The problem is not that outcomes do not matter. The problem is reacting to them without restoring the odds.

The practical correction is to put probability back into the room. How likely is this outcome, really? What changes when the actual odds sit next to the story? Those questions create distance from the vivid scenario.

The chapter does not ask for cold indifference to serious outcomes. It asks for cleaner weighting: a possible event is not automatically a probable one. Better judgment makes likelihood visible before it lets the story take over the decision.
