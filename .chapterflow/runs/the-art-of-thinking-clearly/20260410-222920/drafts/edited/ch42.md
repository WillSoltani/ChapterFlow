The same choice can feel different when it is described differently. Change the wording, the emphasis, or the label, and people often react as if the underlying facts have changed even when they have not.

That is framing. Presentation steers judgment. A decision described as a gain can feel attractive. The same decision described as avoiding a loss can feel urgent or threatening. A label can make an option sound responsible, risky, generous, or careless without altering the substance underneath.

This happens in work, school, and personal life. A proposal sounds better when it is presented as protecting value rather than limiting damage. Feedback feels easier or harsher depending on the framing. A health choice feels safer or scarier depending on whether survival or risk is emphasized. A household decision can sound prudent or miserly depending on how it is introduced. In each case, wording changes attention and emotion.

The chapter does not say wording is irrelevant, because wording guides what the mind notices first. But it does say the frame should not be allowed to replace the facts. A different package can change the feeling of a choice without changing the choice itself.

The practical correction is to restate the same decision in another frame before choosing. What remains if the option is described as a gain, a loss, and a neutral fact pattern? That comparison reveals whether the preference belongs to the substance or only to the wording.

Better judgment listens to the frame, then looks past it.
