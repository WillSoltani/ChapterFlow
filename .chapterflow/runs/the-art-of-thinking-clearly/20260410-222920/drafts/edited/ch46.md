A new gain can feel life-changing before it becomes ordinary. A promotion, a purchase, a milestone, or an upgrade can seem as if it will permanently raise the level of daily happiness. Then the novelty fades, and the improved situation starts feeling normal.

That is the hedonic treadmill. The mind adapts. What first feels special becomes the new baseline, and the emotional payoff shrinks faster than expected.

This happens in work, school, and personal life. A raise feels huge, then becomes the new number. A good grade feels thrilling, then becomes part of the expected identity. A new device, space, routine, or status upgrade feels exciting, then becomes ordinary background. In each case, the gain can still matter, but its emotional intensity fades.

The chapter does not say gains are meaningless. Better income, better health, better conditions, and real achievements can carry lasting value. But it does say that the emotional thrill of getting them usually lasts less than the imagination predicts.

The practical correction is to ask whether the pursuit is about durable value or about the temporary surge of getting something new. That question separates real improvement from excitement that adaptation will quickly flatten.

Better judgment respects the gain without expecting novelty to stay novel forever.
