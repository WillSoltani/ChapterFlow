# Outline: ch09

## Governing principle

Authority bias begins when a title, role, or prestige cue starts substituting for the actual strength of the evidence.

## Canonical prose movement

1. Hook: the same claim sounds stronger when it comes from a person with rank, title, or visible status.
2. Mechanism: the mind treats authority as a shortcut for competence, safety, or correctness.
3. Anchor: work, school, and personal settings all contain moments where status cues outrun real support.
4. Application: deference can become mistaken trust when the speaker's role gets more weight than the claim itself.
5. Tension: real expertise exists, which is why the bias is dangerous rather than cartoonish.
6. Bridge: better judgment asks whether the claim still holds if the prestige cue is removed.

## Depth intentions

- Easy: important-looking people can sound more correct than they really are.
- Medium: status cues create borrowed credibility that can overpower weaker evidence.
- Hard: authority bias corrupts judgment when source prestige changes evaluation more than the claim's actual support.

## Threshold question for hard

If this same claim came from a lower-status source, would I still find it persuasive?

## Example routing

- work: executives, senior teammates, prestigious consultants, or credential-heavy speakers getting extra trust
- school: teachers, high-achieving students, guest experts, or official-seeming materials receiving deference beyond their evidence
- personal: doctors, influencers, respected relatives, uniformed workers, or polished authority figures getting mistaken trust

## Reinforcement surfaces

- recap retrieve should ask what changes when status gets mistaken for proof
- implementation plan should force one deliberate evidence-first check when a high-status speaker makes a confident claim
