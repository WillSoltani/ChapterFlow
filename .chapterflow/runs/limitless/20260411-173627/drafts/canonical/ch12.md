A lot of people call it studying when they are only staying near the material. They reread, rehighlight, and revisit the same page until it feels familiar, then mistake that familiarity for learning. The chapter pushes against that habit by treating study as active work on the material. It is not only contact. It is interaction strong enough to test, shape, and deepen understanding.

That matters because familiarity can feel convincing without being durable. A learner may recognize the paragraph, the slide, or the example and assume that recognition equals mastery. The chapter argues that this is one of the most common study traps. Looking known is not the same as being retrievable, usable, or deeply understood.

This is why study has to become active. The learner has to do something with the material that exposes whether understanding is real: recall it, restate it, question it, connect it, or work with it in a way that passive exposure cannot fake. The chapter does not reduce studying to one universal trick. Its stronger claim is that learning improves when the learner is actively processing instead of only re-encountering.

The chapter also needs a guardrail. Study should not become frantic technique theater where every session is overloaded with clever methods for their own sake. The point is not to perform intensity. The point is to use attention on actions that produce stronger learning than passive review alone.

That matters because focus by itself is not enough. Chapter 11 protected attention from fragmentation. Chapter 12 asks what the learner should do with that protected attention once it is available. If the session is attentive but passive, the gain is smaller than it could be. Study method decides whether attention gets used well.

This is why the chapter sits before memory. Once the learner starts interacting with material actively, the next question becomes what makes that learning stick and become retrievable later. Study deepens the encounter. Memory asks how that deeper learning can be kept.
