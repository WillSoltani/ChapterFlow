A lot of people think they fail to act because they do not want the goal badly enough. The chapter pushes against that reading by looking at the opening cost of action. A learner can care, have a reason, and even have enough fuel to work, but still not begin if the start feels too large, too vague, or too heavy. Sometimes the problem is not weak desire. Sometimes the first move is simply built too big.

That matters because resistance often lives at the threshold. A task can look reasonable from far away and still feel impossible at the moment of entry. The mind sees the full weight of the session, the unfinished project, or the long block of effort and backs away before anything starts. The chapter treats that as a design problem. If the start is oversized, delay becomes easier than action.

This is where small simple steps become serious. The point is not to pretend that the whole job is small. The point is to reduce the cost of beginning. A learner who cannot face an hour of revision may still be able to open the notes, label one page, or solve one problem. That smaller move does not finish the work, but it breaks the frozen edge where avoidance usually wins.

The chapter also needs a guardrail. Small steps are not a cute excuse to stay permanently comfortable. They are not a slogan about doing almost nothing and calling it progress. The stronger claim is narrower: shrinking the first move lowers resistance enough for effort to begin. The ambition can stay large while the entry point becomes lighter.

That shift matters because beginning changes what is possible next. Once the learner is moving, attention has somewhere to go and momentum has something to attach to. A worker who starts with one concrete action often finds that the second action costs less than the first. The chapter does not promise magic, but it does argue that easier starts make repetition and follow-through more available.

This is why the chapter follows energy. Chapter 8 asked whether the learner had the fuel to keep honoring purpose. Chapter 9 asks how to make the first move possible with the fuel that is actually there. Once action can begin without a dramatic burst of force, the next question becomes deeper: how do you turn that beginning into immersive engagement instead of scattered effort?
