Two people can commit comparable crimes in the same court and leave with different sentences because different judges happened to hear them. That is where noise stops sounding like a management problem and starts sounding like a civic wound. A pricing spread can waste money. A sentencing spread can rearrange a life. The chapter's force comes from making one idea hard to soften: if punishment varies more than the case deserves, justice is no longer being distributed only by law. It is also being distributed by instability.

The first source of that instability is the difference between judges themselves. Some judges are harsher than others would be on the same case. A legal system wants to believe punishment tracks the crime, the record, and the governing standard. Noise enters when the person on the bench adds more spread than those case differences can justify. What feels like principled discretion inside one courtroom can look like unequal treatment once comparable sentences are lined up.

The second source is harder to face because it lives inside one person. The same judge can shift across moments, moods, or conditions. A morning case may not feel the same as an afternoon one. A tired judge may not weigh severity like a rested one. The chapter does not need to claim that every fluctuation is dramatic. It only needs to show that punishment can move for reasons not really in the case.

That is why punishment is the right proving ground for noise. Liberty makes variability visible in a way other domains do not. People can hear that inspectors, doctors, or managers differ and still treat the issue as routine friction. Sentences are harder to shrug off. If two defendants deserve roughly similar treatment but draw meaningfully different outcomes because severity varies by judge or by occasion, the law's promise of equal treatment starts to fray.

The chapter still needs a boundary. Some cases differ in morally relevant ways. Some discretion is necessary if punishment is supposed to remain humane rather than stupidly rigid. The issue is not whether all sentencing differences are noise. The issue is how much variation a justice system can tolerate before discretion stops looking principled and starts looking like cover for spread it has not measured honestly.

Once that line comes into focus, the chapter points beyond courts. Sentencing is the sharp test case, not the whole problem. The real challenge is a system that permits instability without seeing it. Punishment makes the stakes obvious. The next step asks how many other institutions are drifting the same way.
