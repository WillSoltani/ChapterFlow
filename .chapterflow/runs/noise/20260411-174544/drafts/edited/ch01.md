Forty-eight underwriters looked at the same cases and produced a spread wide enough to rattle the company paying them. The shock was not ordinary disagreement. It was the amount of variation hiding inside work that was supposed to be disciplined. The company could tolerate the idea that its pricing might be a little off on average. What it had missed was that the answer changed too much depending on who happened to make the call. That is the opening force of *Noise*: some costly failures in judgment come not from everyone leaning the wrong way together, but from comparable cases being pulled in different directions.

That distinction changes what the reader is supposed to notice. Bias points in a direction. Noise shows up as spread. A system can look respectable on average and still treat matched cases unevenly. The problem is easy to miss because each judgment can sound defensible on its own. One underwriter can explain a higher premium. Another can explain a lower one. The deeper failure appears only when the answers are lined up and the range becomes impossible to ignore.

The stakes sharpen when the book turns to punishment. If similar crimes receive different sentences because different judges happen to hear them, the issue is not abstract measurement. It is fairness. People can live with the thought that judgment is imperfect. They have a harder time accepting that liberty might depend on which person is in the room or how a discretionary call lands on a given day. Sentencing makes variability feel like unequal treatment, not harmless texture.

Medicine and other professional settings make the same problem impossible to dismiss. Two doctors can see matched patients and land on different diagnoses. Two inspectors can look at similar conditions and return different ratings. Two employees handling comparable complaints can leave customers with different outcomes for no better reason than who picked up the case. The spread does not have to be malicious to be costly. It only has to be wider than the task can justify.

Institutions often chase bias before they notice noise. Bias has a direction, a story, and often someone to blame. Noise hides inside ordinary discretion until somebody measures it. That is why the opening chapter does not sprint into remedies. It first trains the eye. If prices swing too widely, or a court cannot keep comparable punishment reasonably aligned, the first failure is not fixing the spread. The first failure is overlooking that the spread itself deserves attention.

The boundary matters. Not every difference in judgment is a defect. Some cases are hard. Some variation reflects uncertainty, incomplete information, or humane discretion. The live question is where acceptable discretion ends and costly inconsistency begins. That tension keeps the chapter honest and points cleanly into the next test case. A noisy forecast wastes money. A noisy sentence can alter a life.
