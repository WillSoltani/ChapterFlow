Some decisions feel like matters of judgment, while others feel closer to retrieval. A score can be looked up. A deadline can be checked. But a candidate can be evaluated, a forecast can be assessed, and a student file can be read in ways that still require interpretation. This chapter's job is to mark that difference without pretending the line is perfectly clean.

That distinction matters because noise belongs where a human scale is active. If a process still depends on someone deciding how strong, weak, convincing, risky, or promising something is, then judgment is doing real work. The output is not simply being fetched from the world. It is being produced through assessment. That is the zone where unstable readings become a serious problem.

The chapter therefore resists two lazy simplifications. One is that every decision is equally subjective, so the idea of judgment explains nothing. The other is that rules remove judgment completely, so noise disappears wherever a standard exists. Neither move is serious enough. Many processes mix rules and interpretation. A rubric can still need reading. A guideline can still need scaling. A category can still need a human assessor to decide how the case fits.

That gray area is important because it explains why noise can survive inside respectable institutions. People are not always inventing answers from nothing, but they are often doing more than retrieving a fixed fact. Whenever interpretation remains active, the judge and the instrument matter. The case is passing through a human scale, and human scales can vary.

The chapter's aim is not to romanticize judgment or to dismiss rules. It is to clarify where the book's problem really lives. Noise matters most where interpretation still shapes the output. That is why the boundary has to be drawn with some care.

Once the judgment zone is clearer, the next step follows naturally. If we know where assessment is truly active, we can start asking how error inside those readings should be measured.
