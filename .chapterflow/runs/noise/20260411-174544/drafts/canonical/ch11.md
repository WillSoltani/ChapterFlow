Once the judges themselves come into view, better judgment stops looking like a matter of good intentions alone. The chapter's title adds a more practical answer. If judgments can be improved, they may need debiasing and decision hygiene.

That move matters because awareness by itself does not reliably steady judgment. A person can want to be fair, careful, and objective, yet still judge in ways that drift. The chapter's pressure is therefore procedural. Better judgment may require disciplines that shape how a decision gets made, not only how sincere the judge feels.

This is where debiasing and decision hygiene start to separate while still belonging together. Debiasing points toward reducing predictable distortions in how people think. Decision hygiene is broader. It asks what routines, constraints, or process choices can make judgments less vulnerable to avoidable error and noise.

The chapter keeps one useful limit in place. Hygiene is not a promise of perfect judgment, and debiasing is not a cure that removes every weakness from the mind. The point is more practical than that. Process discipline can improve the baseline even when uncertainty and human limitation remain.

That is why the chapter matters after the turn toward better judges. If judge quality matters, then the next honest question is how to support judges so that better judgment is not left to character alone.

Once that logic is accepted, the book can move into a concrete case. The next step is to show how the sequence of information itself can shape judgment quality in forensic science.
