One inconsistency can trigger suspicion, but it does not explain much by itself. One evaluator gives a different score. One panel sees the case differently. One forecast looks off against another. The chapter's task is to move from that moment of surprise into something stricter: the analysis of noise.

That shift matters because noise becomes intelligible only when scattered inconsistencies are examined as a pattern. A single surprise may be real, but it can still remain anecdotal. Analysis asks what kind of variation is happening, how often it appears, and what the repeated differences are starting to reveal. The point is not to inflate every mismatch into a theory. It is to stop leaving variation in a fog.

This is why the previous chapter had to make error visible first. Once misses can be seen, they can also be compared, grouped, and inspected together. The conversation changes from complaint to structure. Instead of saying the judgments feel uneven, the reader begins asking what sort of unevenness is showing up and whether it forms a real pattern.

That distinction matters because remedies are weak when the problem is still blurry. If noise is going to be reduced later, it first has to be understood as something with shape rather than as an atmosphere of inconsistency. Analysis is what gives that shape.

The chapter still keeps one limit. One odd case does not prove everything, and analysis should not become a grand theory built on one surprise. The move is broader than anecdote but still disciplined about what the evidence can show.

Once that analytic posture is in place, the book can move toward improving judgments. Structure comes before remedy.
