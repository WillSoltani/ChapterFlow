Two people can commit comparable crimes in the same court and leave with different sentences because different judges happened to hear them. That is where noise stops sounding like a management problem and starts sounding like a civic wound. A pricing spread can waste money. A sentencing spread can rearrange a life. The chapter's force comes from making one idea impossible to soften: if punishment varies more than the case deserves, justice is no longer being distributed only by law. It is also being distributed by instability.

The first source of that instability is the difference between judges themselves. Some judges are simply harsher than others would be on the same case. That matters because a legal system usually wants to believe that punishment tracks the crime, the record, and the governing standard. Noise enters when the person on the bench adds more spread than those case differences can justify. What looks like principled discretion from inside the courtroom can look like unequal treatment once comparable sentences are lined up side by side.

The second source is harder to face because it lives inside one person rather than between people. The same judge can shift across moments, moods, or conditions. A morning case may not feel the same as an afternoon case. A tired judge may not weigh severity the same way as a rested one. The chapter does not need to prove that every fluctuation is wild to make its point. It only needs to show that punishment can drift for reasons that are not really in the case. Once that possibility is admitted, sentencing starts to look less like a stable expression of justice and more like judgment under variable weather.

That is why punishment is the right proving ground for noise. Liberty makes variability visible in a way other domains do not. A person can hear that doctors differ, inspectors differ, or managers differ and still treat the issue as routine friction. Sentences are harder to shrug off. If two defendants deserve roughly similar treatment but draw meaningfully different outcomes because severity varies by judge or by occasion, the law's promise of equal treatment starts to fray. The problem is not only that someone suffers a harsher result. The problem is that the institution loses credibility when comparable lives are handled too differently.

Still, the chapter cannot become a childish demand for machine justice. Some cases differ in morally relevant ways. Some discretion is necessary if punishment is supposed to remain humane rather than stupidly rigid. That boundary matters because it keeps the argument serious. The issue is not whether all sentencing differences are noise. The issue is how much variation a justice system can tolerate before discretion stops looking principled and starts looking like cover for spread it has not measured honestly.

Once that line becomes the focus, the next move is obvious. Sentencing is one domain, but the deeper issue is a system that permits instability without noticing it. The chapter begins with punishment because punishment sharpens the stakes. It points forward because the real challenge is broader: what else looks principled one case at a time while drifting badly in the aggregate?
