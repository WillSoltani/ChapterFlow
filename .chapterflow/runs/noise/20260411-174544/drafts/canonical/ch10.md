Improvement can sound abstract until the human side of judgment comes back into view. A process may be redesigned, standards may be clarified, and variation may be mapped, but the chapter's title adds another pressure. Better judgments may also require better judges.

That move matters because the quality of a judgment depends partly on the people and conditions producing it. The chapter is not arguing for heroic individuals who solve everything by talent alone. It is arguing that judgment quality is shaped not only by systems but also by the quality of the judges inside those systems.

This changes the improvement discussion in a useful way. Better judgments are not only a matter of better complaints, better maps, or better intentions. They also raise questions about who is judging, under what conditions, and with what steadiness. The human side is not the whole answer, but it is too important to ignore.

The chapter still keeps an important limit. Better judges do not mean perfect judges, and the point is not to replace system design with faith in superior people. The pressure is narrower than that. Once the book admits that human judgment varies, the quality of judges becomes one real lever among others.

That is why the chapter serves as a hinge. Improvement is no longer only about changing the environment around judgment. It is also about taking the judging agents themselves seriously.

Once that human-side lever is visible, the next move can become more concrete. The book can turn toward debiasing and decision hygiene.
