Once a pattern of noise becomes visible, complaint is no longer enough. A process can be criticized forever for being uneven, but the chapter's title changes the question. If judgments can be studied, how should they be improved?

That shift matters because visible variation creates a practical obligation. Noise is no longer just something to notice or analyze from a distance. It becomes something that calls for design. Better judgment does not have to mean flawless judgment. It means taking the quality of decisions seriously enough to shape them rather than merely endure them.

This is where the book moves from diagnosis into betterment. The reader is no longer only asking what went wrong or what pattern appeared. The reader is asking what kind of change would make the judgments steadier, more defensible, or less vulnerable to the variation already uncovered. That makes improvement a practical task rather than a moral wish.

The chapter keeps one needed limit. Improvement does not promise perfect certainty, and it does not imply that all betterment means rigid rules. The point is not utopia. The point is that once judgment quality is visible, leaving it untouched becomes harder to defend.

That is why the chapter feels like a pivot. Analysis without change would leave the book hovering over the problem. Improvement is the first sign that noise can be addressed instead of merely described.

Once that turn is made, the next question follows naturally. If judgments are to become better, what makes for better judges in the first place?
