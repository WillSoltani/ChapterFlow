Core Claim:
Noise is unwanted variability in judgments that should line up more closely. The opening chapter makes the problem visible by showing that professionals facing the same case often disagree far more than institutions assume. Its practical force is that organizations obsess over bias while often ignoring variation that is costly even when average judgment looks acceptable. The chapter's first job is not to fix the whole problem. It is to make the reader stop treating disagreement as harmless background static.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
It does not begin with a taxonomy or remedy. It begins by making hidden variation feel concrete, expensive, and morally serious across law, medicine, and organizational decision-making.

Author Logic Chain:
First the author establishes that people using judgment on the same problem can produce strikingly different answers.
Then the author shows that this variability is a separate problem from bias and is often neglected because it is less visible.
Then the author concludes that institutions need to start noticing noise before they can reduce it.

Frameworks or Terms Introduced:
Noise: unwanted variability across judgments that ought to be steadier.
Bias: average directional error, contrasted with noise rather than equated with it.
Noise neglect: the institutional habit of noticing bias while overlooking variability.

Specific Applications:
- sentencing fairness
- diagnosis or inspection consistency
- pricing, forecasting, or complaints handling in organizations

Common Misreadings:
- noise is just another word for bias
- disagreement always means healthy discretion
- fixing noise only matters in highly technical fields

Counterarguments or Limits:
Some variation reflects real discretion, incomplete information, or humane judgment rather than obvious failure. The chapter should keep alive the question of where acceptable discretion ends and costly noise begins.
