Core Claim:
The sentencing chapter makes noise feel morally urgent by showing that punishment can vary more than justice should allow. Comparable crimes can receive different outcomes because different judges, or even the same judge in different conditions, do not respond with stable severity. The chapter uses punishment to show that noisy judgment is not just inefficient. It can become unequal treatment under the name of discretion. Its job is to make the cost of variation feel civic and human.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
It takes the general problem from Chapter 1 and tests it where liberty, fairness, and institutional legitimacy are at stake.

Author Logic Chain:
First the author shows that punishment reveals judgment noise in a domain people expect to be principled.
Then the author shows that both differences between judges and fluctuations within judges can widen outcomes.
Then the author concludes that justice cannot rely on discretion alone without confronting how much variability it is tolerating.

Required Anchors:
- similar crimes receiving different sentences from different judges
- sentence shifts that can depend on timing, mood, or decision context
- fairness pressure: liberty changing because of judicial variability rather than case difference

Frameworks or Terms Introduced:
Noisy punishment: sentencing spread that exceeds what comparable justice should allow.
Level noise: stable differences in severity across judges.
Occasion noise: shifts in one judge's own severity across moments or states.

Specific Applications:
- criminal sentencing
- disciplinary boards
- school conduct hearings

Common Misreadings:

Counterarguments or Limits:
Cases are not mechanically identical, and some discretion is part of humane judgment. The chapter must keep alive the tension between individualized justice and intolerable variability.
