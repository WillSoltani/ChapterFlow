# Chapter 7 Edited Draft

A company can know the right job and still fail to deliver it consistently. That is the pressure Chapter 7 has to face. Chapter 6 clarified that an offering builds an earned record through the jobs it repeatedly gets hired to do. The next question is what the organization must do once that record is clear enough to trust. If the firm keeps operating through disconnected functions, feature agendas, or local targets, the job may be understood in theory while being weakened in practice.

*Integrating Around a Job* names the organizational consequence of Jobs Theory. The point is not that teams should adopt a better slogan about the customer. The point is that the trusted progress buyers hire for has to become a coordinating reference point across decisions. A clear job record is wasted if product, service, operations, and support still optimize around their own fragments while no one protects the full progress the buyer actually came to hire.

That makes integration narrower and more demanding than generic collaboration language. The chapter does not need to promise a full reorganization blueprint. It only needs to show that once the job is clear, the firm should stop treating that knowledge as one insight among many. The job has to shape how priorities are chosen, how tradeoffs are judged, and how consistency is protected. Otherwise the offering's earned resume and the organization's daily behavior begin pulling in different directions.

The chapter also has a practical warning built into it. Internal convenience is often easier to protect than customer progress. Separate groups can each improve their own metric while the buyer experiences more friction, more handoffs, or a less coherent result. From the inside, the company may feel productive. From the outside, the hired progress may be getting weaker. Integrating around a job means refusing to let local optimization outrank the full progress the customer is trying to make.

That is why Chapter 7 matters as the opening move of the jobs-focused organization section. The book can no longer stop at identifying demand well. It has to ask what kind of company can repeatedly deliver on what it has learned. Once the job becomes the integrating logic, the organization has a better chance of making its choices reinforce one another instead of quietly breaking the experience apart.

This chapter also prepares the next pressure. Even if a firm begins integrating around the right job, it can still lose focus over time. Attention can drift back toward internal categories, legacy habits, or attractive side goals. So the bridge forward is clear: after the organization is integrated around the job, it has to keep its eye on that job without letting the coordinating logic fade.
