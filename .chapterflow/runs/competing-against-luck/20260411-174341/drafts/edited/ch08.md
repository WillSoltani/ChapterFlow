# Chapter 8 Edited Draft

A company can begin integrating around the right job and still lose that discipline later. That is the pressure Chapter 8 needs to address. Chapter 7 showed why the job must become the coordinating logic inside the firm. The next question is how that focus stays visible once the excitement of initial alignment fades and everyday pressures start competing for attention again. An organization does not drift away from the job only through one dramatic mistake. It can do so gradually, through familiar routines, attractive side goals, and a quiet return to internal categories.

*Keeping Your Eye on the Job* names that risk directly. The chapter is not arguing that every change is dangerous or that the job must become a frozen slogan. It is arguing that the job has to remain an active reference point. New priorities, feature ideas, process exceptions, and local requests should still be tested against whether they protect or blur the progress buyers actually hire the offering to deliver.

That makes focus an organizational discipline rather than a mood. Once a company has done the hard work of clarifying the job and integrating around it, old habits can still pull attention backward. Teams may slip into product-attribute thinking because it feels familiar. Departments may revive measures that are easier to manage internally. New initiatives may look exciting while quietly diluting the clarity of the hired progress. The chapter treats that relapse as a structural risk, not just as a failure of intention.

The frozen support allows this chapter to stay careful. Jobs Theory emphasizes the circumstances or forces behind customer behavior, and the forces framing keeps habit visible as something that works against change. That is enough support for a narrow claim: organizations can fall back into familiar logic even after sincere progress. Keeping an eye on the job means repeatedly checking whether decisions still serve the same progress rather than assuming the answer stays true automatically.

This chapter therefore sharpens the meaning of consistency. It is not only about aligning the organization once. It is about resisting drift after alignment begins. If the firm stops using the job as a filter, internal categories can slowly become the real decision makers again. The company may still talk as though it serves the same progress, yet its roadmap, processes, and exceptions may be bending toward other priorities.

That is why Chapter 8 matters before the book describes the jobs-focused organization more fully. A company cannot become jobs-focused merely by naming the job or integrating around it once. It has to sustain attention long enough for the job to keep shaping choices under pressure. That is the bridge forward. Once the firm can hold focus on the job, the book can describe what a broader jobs-focused organization looks like.
