The first mistake in innovation is staring at the product too hard. The second is thinking that once you stop staring at the product, you have solved the problem. Chapter 1 showed that the buying moment matters more than the category label. That still leaves a harder question. What exactly is happening inside that moment that makes one answer worth hiring and another easy to ignore?

The book's answer is progress. People are not only choosing an object. They are trying to move from one state to a better one inside specific circumstances. That is why "products" is too thin a frame. A product can describe what changed hands. It cannot explain what kind of movement the buyer was trying to make. Once progress becomes the focus, the job stops sounding like a feature request and starts sounding like a real shift in the person's situation.

That shift matters because the progress is rarely only practical. A customer may want a product to save time, reduce hassle, avoid embarrassment, feel responsible, look competent, or relieve a low-grade anxiety that never appears in a survey box. The chapter's pressure point is that these forces can be functional, social, and emotional at the same time. If a team studies only the practical layer, it may understand what the product does while missing why the choice felt compelling enough to make right now.

This is where a lot of product thinking goes soft. Once people hear that jobs include emotional and social forces, they start talking as if the chapter were about empathy in the abstract. It is not. The emotional layer matters only because it is tied to a particular circumstance. A parent may not want a snack simply for calories. The parent may want speed, calm, and the feeling of handling the moment well. A commuter may not want a breakfast item simply to eat. The commuter may want a small form of relief, routine, and control during a dull drive. The emotion does not float above the scene. It comes from the scene.

That is also why the same person can look inconsistent when the job changes. If you only study the buyer's identity, the choices look noisy. If you study the progress being pursued in each moment, the noise starts to sort itself. A person is not betraying a stable preference by hiring different solutions in different contexts. The active progress target has shifted, so the criteria for a good answer shift with it. Chapter 2 makes that move explicit. The customer is not a fixed profile moving through the market with one unchanging demand. The customer is encountering different struggles that activate different forms of desired progress.

The gain is not philosophical. It is operational. Once a team asks what progress the customer is trying to make, it can judge features, messages, and experiences against a clearer standard. Does the offering help the person move where the person is actually trying to go? Does it remove the friction that makes the old situation frustrating? Does it address only the practical layer while leaving the emotional or social blockage intact? Those are stronger questions than "what would you like us to add?"

But the chapter also carries a limit that keeps progress from becoming a vague slogan. Progress has to stay attached to specific circumstances or it collapses into aspiration talk. "I want to be healthier" is too broad. "I want something that gets me through this drive without hunger or mess" is sharp enough to organize design. The same discipline holds across domains. A useful progress statement names the movement the person seeks and the situation that makes that movement urgent.

That is why the title matters. Progress, not products. Not because products stop mattering, but because products become legible only after the desired movement is clear. Once the movement is clear, the product can be designed, judged, and compared in a more honest way. The next chapter has to prove that this is not a one-case insight by showing the same logic at work across many settings in the wild.
