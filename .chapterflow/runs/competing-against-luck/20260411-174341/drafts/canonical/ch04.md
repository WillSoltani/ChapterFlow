# Chapter 4 Canonical Draft

It is easy to admire a good jobs insight after someone else has already found it. The harder question is how a team goes out and finds one on purpose. That is the turn this chapter makes. If Jobs Theory is supposed to improve innovation rather than just improve explanation, then the book has to move from recognition to search.

The title *Job Hunting* makes that shift explicit. The theory has already shown that demand becomes clearer when the team studies progress in a circumstance instead of product categories alone. The next practical move is to ask where that kind of evidence can actually be found before the job is neatly named. The answer cannot be passive. Teams do not usually discover jobs by waiting for a dashboard to become poetic or for a feature request to reveal its own causality.

That is because most familiar inputs arrive too late or too flattened. A feature request may tell you what someone says they want changed, but it often arrives after the struggle has already been translated into solution language. A demographic segment may describe who bought something, but it still leaves the pressured moment blurry. Job hunting has to push earlier and closer to the live situation where progress feels difficult, tradeoffs become visible, and substitutes start to matter.

This makes the search discipline more specific than generic customer research advice. The point is not simply to collect more opinions. The point is to look for moments where people are already trying to make progress and where the current answers feel incomplete, awkward, risky, or costly. Those strained moments are where the job starts to show itself. The friction in the present and the alternatives orbiting that friction give the team something sturdier than a wishlist.

Substitutes matter for the same reason. A team may think it is searching inside one product category, while the real evidence lives in what buyers nearly chose, currently tolerate, or piece together instead. Job hunting therefore means looking at the surrounding battlefield, not just at the focal product. The active struggle often reveals itself by showing what the person is comparing, avoiding, postponing, or reluctantly accepting.

The chapter also has to draw a limit around the search. Hunting a job is not a formula, and it is not the same as forcing every observed frustration into a grand demand story. The team still needs discipline about circumstance. A recurring complaint without a clear pressured situation may remain too vague. A vivid anecdote without comparison to alternatives may remain too thin. The search gets better when the team keeps asking where the movement is, why it matters now, and what real substitutes belong in the moment.

That is why Chapter 4 sits at the opening of the book's practical section. Once jobs can be seen across settings, the next step is to search for them deliberately in the field rather than waiting for a lucky case to appear. But search alone is not enough. Even after the team has gone to the right place, it can still mishear what the customer is showing. That is the bridge to the next chapter. After you know where to look, you still have to learn how to hear what customers do not say directly.
