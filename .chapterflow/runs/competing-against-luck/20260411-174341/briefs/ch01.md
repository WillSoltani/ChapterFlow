Core Claim:
The opening chapter argues that customer choice becomes clearer when you stop classifying buyers by profile or preference alone and instead ask what progress they are trying to make in a specific circumstance. The frozen bundle repeatedly ties the opening to the milk-shake case, where observation reveals that the same product can be hired for different jobs. The chapter therefore needs to frame the milk-shake story as a change in question, not as a cute anecdote about one food item.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
This chapter is about discovering the right unit of analysis. The distinctive move is that the product stays the same while the job changes with the circumstance. That makes the chapter about situational causality and overlooked progress, not about feature optimization.

Author Logic Chain:
First the authors set up the problem that innovation feels like luck when firms ask the wrong customer questions.
Then the milk-shake case shows that observing when and why people buy exposes a job that demographics and flavor tweaks miss.
Then the chapter generalizes the lesson: customers hire products to make progress in a lived situation, and innovation improves when that job becomes the starting point.

Required Anchors:
- McDonald's wants to improve milk-shake performance and initially uses standard customer research; the friction is feature-focused questions fail to explain buying behavior, the consequence is weak innovation insight.
- Observation reveals a strong morning-commute purchase pattern; the friction is commuters need a one-handed, filling, boredom-reducing option, the consequence is the job becomes legible.
- The same product can also be hired for a different occasion, such as a parent trying to give a child a treat without a messy fight; the friction is one product hides multiple jobs, the consequence is one-size optimization breaks.
