There is a strange relief in meeting someone who seems more certain than you are. Their confidence can feel like shelter. Their judgment can feel clearer than your own. Their approval can steady a self that feels scattered or burdened. Becker's seventh chapter asks why this happens so powerfully. His answer is that persons can become more than companions, lovers, teachers, or authorities. They can become psychic centers from which others borrow strength, worth, and reality itself.

That is the spell cast by persons. Becker is not describing ordinary respect alone. He is describing the way another human being can begin to feel like the place where life makes sense. A powerful person can seem to hold courage, permission, certainty, and value in a more concentrated form than the dependent self can hold alone. The attraction is not foolishness in the simple sense. It is an attempt to ease the burden of being separate, finite, and responsible for one's own meanings.

This is where transference matters. The person under the spell does not merely like or admire the other. They invest the other with stabilizing force. They begin to live by borrowed reality. A mentor, boss, partner, therapist, parent figure, or leader can become the one who seems to know, who seems to stand firmly where the self wavers. The dependent person then gains something real from the bond: protection against doubt, fear, and inner diffusion.

That is why dependency feels relieving before it feels degrading. Freedom is not always experienced as dignity. It can feel like exposure. To be separate is to bear uncertainty, ambivalence, mortality, and the pressure of making oneself. Another person's strength can promise release from that burden. Their certainty becomes a place to rest. Their admiration becomes a substitute for inner worth. Their direction becomes a way to avoid the strain of having to author reality alone.

But the same bond that protects also narrows. Once another person becomes the center of borrowed strength, reality starts organizing itself around them. What they bless feels real. What they reject can feel unreal or dangerous. One's own judgment weakens. The self does not disappear, but it becomes dependent on another person's orbit for orientation and permission. That is the nexus of unfreedom Becker wants to name.

This is why the chapter is more severe than a simple warning about manipulation. People do not only get trapped because others are domineering. They get trapped because dependence solves something. It offers borrowed heroism. By attaching to someone who seems larger, steadier, or more sacred, the person can feel enlarged as well. The relationship becomes an answer to helplessness and insignificance, not merely a bad social habit.

Becker's point reaches beyond romance or obedience in the narrow sense. Charismatic leaders, revered intellectuals, gifted mentors, idealized lovers, and emotionally central friends can all become bearers of borrowed reality. The form changes, but the psychological logic remains: another person carries what the dependent self cannot yet bear to carry alone. This is why surrender can feel like love, loyalty, gratitude, or realism rather than like bondage.

The cost appears when the bond must either deepen into self-loss or break into panic. If your reality is leaning on another person's power, then disagreement, distance, or abandonment become existential threats. Freedom no longer feels like release. It feels like collapse. Becker leaves the chapter there because the problem is now large enough to widen beyond persons alone. Once dependency on powerful figures is visible, the next question is whether love, creativity, and religion can answer the hunger for transcendence without reproducing the same instability in another form.
