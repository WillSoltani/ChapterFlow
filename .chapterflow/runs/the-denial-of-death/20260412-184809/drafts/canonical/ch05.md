Some people can explain themselves brilliantly and still remain strangers to themselves. They have the language for patterns, motives, childhood wounds, coping styles, and recurring fears, yet something central stays untouched. Becker turns to Kierkegaard at this point because he thinks ordinary psychology can stop too close to symptom and strategy. The issue is not only what a person does to stay stable. It is what kind of self they are becoming, and whether they can live honestly inside that knowledge.

That is why Kierkegaard matters here as a deeper psychologist. Becker is not abandoning psychology for sermon or creed. He is arguing that anxiety and despair reach a depth that a merely clinical vocabulary can miss. A person does not suffer only from bad habits or mismanaged impulses. They also suffer from the difficulty of becoming a self under the pressure of freedom, dependence, finitude, and the demand for meaning. Kierkegaard sees that inward burden with unusual clarity.

The chapter therefore shifts the problem of defense inward. Earlier Becker showed how terror gets repressed, how human beings recoil from creatureliness, and how character becomes a vital lie. Kierkegaard helps him ask what happens once those defenses are not merely described from the outside but encountered from within. A person may understand their own defenses quite well and still remain divided. They may see the role they hide in, the illusion they rely on, or the fear they keep circling, but still fail to stand honestly in relation to it.

That failure is why despair in this chapter is deeper than sadness or discouragement. It names a trouble in the self's relation to itself. A person may be socially competent, productive, admired, and emotionally articulate while still living evasively. They can remain restless because the problem is not one isolated symptom. It is the whole posture of the self toward dependence, limitation, and what Becker takes to be the spiritual dimension of existence. Kierkegaard gives Becker a language for that deeper level.

Anxiety also changes meaning here. It is not only nervousness before an event or pressure from too many obligations. It is exposure. It is what appears when the self senses freedom, risk, dependence, and the possibility of becoming more honest than it wants to be. That is why the chapter feels more demanding than the earlier ones. Becker is no longer only explaining why people defend themselves. He is showing why explanation alone does not necessarily transform them.

A person can become very skilled at using psychological insight defensively. They can learn to narrate themselves in a way that sounds mature while still avoiding the central surrender the chapter implies. They can manage their symptoms, reduce conflict, improve performance, and even speak fluently about fear, all while remaining protected from the hardest truth. Kierkegaard matters to Becker because he refuses to confuse self-description with inward change. He keeps the problem at the level of becoming.

This is also why Becker broadens psychology toward a spiritual dimension without turning the chapter into theology. The point is not doctrinal detail. It is that human beings do not suffer only from misfiring mechanisms. They suffer from how they stand before meaning, truth, dependence, guilt, and transcendence. A psychology that cannot name that depth may still be useful, but it will remain incomplete. It can help a person function better without helping them face what they most need to face.

The chapter leaves a hard pressure in place. If the self can understand its own defenses and still remain inwardly false, then healing cannot mean explanation alone. It has to involve a more dangerous honesty, one that does not merely observe the self but risks becoming different. That turn prepares Becker's next move. Once Kierkegaard has widened psychology beyond symptom control, Freud can be revisited not only as a genius of insight but as someone whose own character may have limited the truths he could live with.
