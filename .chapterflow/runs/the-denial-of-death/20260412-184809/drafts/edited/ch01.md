People do not ask for big ideas when life feels settled. They ask for them when ordinary explanations start sounding decorative, when the scale of the problem is too large for tidy advice. Becker opens there. He is writing in a world that still has institutions, credentials, and inherited language, but less confidence that any of them can tell a person why being alive should feel meaningful. So he reaches for a harsher word than purpose or success. He reaches for heroism.

He does not mean battlefield glory. He means the need to feel that your life counts for more than simple survival. A human being eats, sleeps, gets sick, panics, ages, and dies like any other animal. Yet a human being also wants to matter, to stand for something, to feel elevated above accident and decay. Becker treats that wish as basic. People do not only want comfort. They want significance. They want some proof, social or moral or spiritual, that they are not just vulnerable flesh waiting for its turn to fail.

That is why the chapter moves so quickly from heroism to narcissism. In Becker's telling, narcissism is not shallow vanity. It is the refusal to feel negligible. Even a child arrives with a demand to be central in its own world. Later that demand gets civilized, disciplined, or disguised, but it does not disappear. It turns into status hunger, moral seriousness, ambition, self-sacrifice, ideological certainty, and private fantasies of being the one who really matters. The forms differ. The pressure underneath them stays recognizable. If a person cannot bear to feel small, life becomes a search for some stage on which smallness can be denied.

Culture provides that stage. A society does more than coordinate labor and punish crime. It tells people what kind of significance is available and what counts as a worthy life. A soldier, a scholar, a saint, a parent, a provider, a reformer, a loyal citizen: each role offers a script by which an ordinary person can feel stitched into something larger and more lasting than the body's narrow span. Culture is not only a set of rules. It is a machinery for distributing heroic permission. It lets people believe their daily effort belongs to a world that means something.

This makes the modern problem sharper than simple unbelief. Becker's concern is not that people became secular and therefore sad. It is that inherited worlds of meaning have weakened while the need they answered has not. A person can stop trusting the old language of salvation, destiny, honor, or communal ritual and still wake up hungry for a life that feels justified. The appetite remains after the feast has been discredited. That is why modern disillusion is so unstable. It can look like freedom because fewer myths command obedience. It can also feel like exposure because the old routes to significance no longer persuade.

Becker sees this strain in the modern young, who inherit institutions without automatically feeling enlarged by them. They can see the compromise in public life, the vanity in ambition, the hollowness in prestige, the theatricality in patriotism, the fraud inside moral performance. What they often cannot see, at least not yet, is how to live once those exposures have done their work. Tear away a false stage and you have removed a source of deception. You may also have removed the platform on which a person stood. The need to matter does not vanish because its inherited costumes stop fitting.

That is why the chapter does not read like a celebration of demystification. Becker is too alert to what human beings use myths for. He is not saying that every heroic script deserves loyalty. Some are childish, cruel, inflated, or brittle. But he is saying that people cannot live on negation alone. If all prestige is fake, all institutions compromised, all symbolic claims suspect, then the self does not become pure. It becomes stranded. The person still has a body that can be injured, humiliated, ignored, and erased. Without some symbolic lift, the body starts to feel like a trap rather than a life.

This gives heroism a double meaning. It is a dignity problem and a danger problem at once. The same force that pushes a person toward discipline, creation, sacrifice, and moral seriousness can also turn into posturing, domination, and desperate attachment to any system that promises importance. Becker's opening chapter matters because it refuses to sentimentalize that force. The need for significance is not proof of nobility. It is proof that human beings are strange creatures, capable of imagining ideals while unable to forget that they are breakable.

By the end of the chapter, heroism no longer sounds decorative. It sounds like the main way humans try to answer the humiliation of being finite. Culture matters because it organizes that answer. Modernity matters because it weakens confidence in old answers without reducing the pressure that made them necessary. That leaves Becker with a harder question than whether people are brave enough. If people work so hard to matter, what pressure are they defending themselves against when they do?
