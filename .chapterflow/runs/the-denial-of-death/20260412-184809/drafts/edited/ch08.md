People often ask love, work, art, or faith to do more than accompany life. They ask them to rescue life. Becker turns to Otto Rank because he thinks this rescuing impulse reveals something basic about human beings: we do not only want comfort or success. We want transcendence. We want some way of rising above smallness, finitude, and the pressure of being merely creaturely.

That is why Rank matters here. Becker uses him to widen the psychological field beyond narrower psychoanalytic closure. If human beings are struggling not only with symptom and repression but with the need for meaning and enlargement, then love, creativity, and religion cannot be treated as decorative extras. They are serious attempts to answer the burden of existence.

Romantic love is one of those answers. Another person can appear not merely attractive but saving. Love promises to gather scattered worth, heal loneliness, and make life feel sufficient. In that form it is carrying more than companionship. It is being asked to bear transcendence. The beloved becomes someone through whom the self hopes to rise above incompleteness.

Creativity can take the same burden in another register. A work, a calling, or an act of making can seem to offer more than expression. It can feel like redemption through achievement, form, and permanence. The artist or builder is not only producing something. They are trying to secure a life that matters enough to outlast inner smallness and death pressure.

Religion belongs in the same field because it addresses the human need for participation in a larger order of meaning. Becker does not treat that need as trivial. The reach toward transcendence is real. Rank matters because he refuses to keep psychology sealed off from that seriousness. Human beings are not only trying to manage symptoms. They are trying to find a bearable relation to ultimacy.

The problem is that these answers become unstable when finite forms are asked to carry total salvation. A lover cannot safely hold all of a person's worth. A creative project cannot permanently secure immortality for the self. A finite practice of faith can also be overburdened when it is forced to erase every anxiety and contradiction. The issue is not that love, art, or religion are fake. It is that they crack when asked to do more than any finite form can sustain.

This is why Becker's tone stays serious rather than dismissive. He is not mocking romance, creativity, or religion as naive illusions. He is showing that they are genuine responses to a genuine problem. The hunger for transcendence is real. The instability appears when a partial answer is turned into total rescue. Then what once gave enlargement begins to produce collapse, resentment, or unbearable pressure.

Rank therefore helps Becker state a larger criticism of psychoanalysis. If psychology stops at symptom and adjustment, it closes too soon on Kierkegaard and on the human need for transcendence. It leaves out the very scale of the problem. That is why Chapter 8 matters. It prepares the next argument that modern psychoanalysis still lacks a convincing world-picture strong enough to address failed heroism at its roots.
