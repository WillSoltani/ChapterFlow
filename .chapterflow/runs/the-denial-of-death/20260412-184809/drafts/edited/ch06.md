It is one thing to analyze other people's defenses and another to ask what your own character allows you to see. Becker returns to Freud with that harder standard in mind. He does not come back to dismiss Freud. He comes back because Freud's greatness is real enough to make the question serious. If a thinker can illuminate hidden motives, repression, conflict, and illusion so powerfully, then it matters to ask what kind of character made that possible and what limits came with it.

This is why the chapter treats Freud himself as a case. The move is not cheap psychobiography. Becker is applying the same insight he has already used elsewhere: character is never just private style. It organizes what a person can admire, endure, master, and admit. A theory can therefore carry the shape of the self that produced it. Freud's severity, courage, discipline, and relentless truth-telling are part of what gave psychoanalysis its force. Becker wants to hold onto that force.

But the same structure that makes certain insight possible can narrow vision too. Becker's issue is not that Freud lacked brilliance. It is that brilliance itself can be defended. A strong character does not merely produce strength. It also filters. If Freud's style leaned toward toughness, control, reduction, and demystification, then those strengths may also have set the range of truths he could most easily affirm. Theory becomes, at least partly, the preferred world of a particular heroic self.

That is what makes the chapter more unsettling than a simple critique of one founder. Freud becomes an example of a broader problem. Human beings do not think from nowhere. They think from within a character structure, and that structure carries both courage and defensiveness. Theories can therefore be revelatory and narrowed at the same time. Becker is not accusing Freud of fraud. He is showing how a defended style can become an instrument of truth in one direction and limitation in another.

This also explains why Becker's tone remains mixed. Freud is not reduced to a blind spot with a famous name. He remains a major truth-teller. He named repression, conflict, dependency, and illusion with a severity that changed psychology. Becker depends on that insight even while questioning the character that shaped it. The chapter's moral pressure comes from refusing the easy options. Neither pure reverence nor easy debunking is adequate.

Once character is brought into view, reductionism looks different too. It is no longer just an abstract mistake in theory. It can be the way a particular kind of self secures mastery. If one style of truth-telling makes ambiguity, transcendence, or spiritual depth harder to bear, then theory will narrow around what that style can control. Freud's own character becomes relevant not because personal gossip is interesting, but because defended identity can shape what kind of truth feels livable.

The chapter therefore widens a problem that goes beyond Freud. Every strong thinker may carry a mixed inheritance. The same structure that produces courage, rigor, and sharpness may also produce blindness. This is not a reason to stop thinking or stop trusting theory. It is a reason to read all major insight with more humility. A theory may tell the truth, but it may also tell the truth in the contour of the person who needed it.

That conclusion prepares the next movement in Becker's argument. Once a founder's character can shape what others come to see, the power of persons becomes central. The issue is no longer only what one thinker believed. It is how human beings become bound to powerful figures, borrow their reality, and lose freedom inside those attachments. Freud's character problem therefore opens naturally into the next chapter on the spell cast by persons.
