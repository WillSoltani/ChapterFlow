Core Claim:
Becker argues that human beings often surrender freedom to powerful persons because dependency and transference promise protection from terror, uncertainty, and self-burden. This chapter matters because it shifts the problem of defended selfhood into relationships: people do not only build inner defenses, they also borrow stability, reality, and significance from others who seem stronger or more certain.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
This is the transference and dependency chapter. It must not reduce unfreedom to simple gullibility or weak character. The key move is that submission to powerful persons is a terror-management strategy and an attempt to secure borrowed heroism.

Author Logic Chain:
First the author argues that persons can become more than companions or authorities; they can become sources of protection, orientation, and worth.
Then the author shows how transference and dependency bind people to powerful figures.
Then the author explains why this creates unfreedom: reality and selfhood become organized around another person's power.
Then the author concludes that the spell cast by persons reflects a deep human need for borrowed strength and meaning.

Frameworks or Terms Introduced:
- transference: investing another person with stabilizing power beyond the immediate relationship
- borrowed heroism: using another person's significance to support one's own sense of worth
- dependency bind: safety purchased through attachment and surrender
- unfreedom: loss of reality-testing and self-direction inside another person's orbit

Specific Applications:
- reading charisma and authority as existentially attractive, not just socially persuasive
- understanding why mentors, lovers, bosses, or leaders can become psychic necessities
- seeing how deference can feel like relief from freedom

Common Misreadings:
- treating dependency as stupidity
- reducing transference to romantic obsession alone

Counterarguments or Limits:
Keep the chapter centered on Becker's terror-and-dependency logic. Do not drift into modern cult discourse, attachment jargon, or clinical manuals. The claim is broader: people often submit because another person seems to hold the reality they cannot hold alone.

Previous Chapter Bridge:
Chapter 6 showed that powerful thinkers shape worlds others may enter. Chapter 7 asks what happens when a person becomes the emotional center from which others borrow certainty and worth.
Next Chapter Bridge:
Once dependency on persons is clear, Becker can turn to Otto Rank and ask whether love, creativity, and religion can answer the hunger for transcendence without reproducing the same bind.
