Core Claim:
Becker argues that fear of death is universal, but people usually carry it indirectly rather than as a constant explicit thought. Childhood helplessness, dependence, and bodily vulnerability feed that terror, and ordinary development becomes a long effort to hide or manage it. The chapter matters because it converts the opening chapter's language of heroism into a pressure source: people need symbolic significance because mere creaturely dependence feels intolerable. The prose should keep the chapter grounded in fear, repression, development, and coping, not jump ahead to later chapters on culture or religion.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
This is the chapter that names death fear as a pervasive organizing pressure and links it to childhood vulnerability. It is not yet the full bodily-symbolic paradox chapter and not yet a general discussion of neurosis. It must feel like a diagnostic deepening of the opening, not a repeat of Chapter 1.

Author Logic Chain:
First the author reviews competing views about fear of death and sides with the view that it is universal.
Then the author shows that people cannot keep this terror fully conscious and therefore repress or transform it.
Then the author traces the issue into childhood helplessness, fear of the world, and dependence on caretakers.
Then the author concludes that adult coping systems often hide death terror rather than erase it.

Required Anchors:
- Becker's contrast between minimizing death fear and treating it as a universal condition.
- Childhood helplessness, fear, and immature ego as developmental conditions that intensify mortality pressure.
- Adult coping forms such as dependence, overcontrol, superstition, or staying small enough to feel safe.

- immature ego: the child's weak command over world, self, and others

Specific Applications:
- reading overcontrol as a defense against helplessness
- understanding why dependency can feel safer than freedom
- seeing ordinary busyness or conformity as partially terror-management

Common Misreadings:
- claiming Becker means people consciously think about death all day
- reducing the chapter to childhood trauma only

Counterarguments or Limits:
The chapter uses broad developmental claims and older psychoanalytic language. The prose should present Becker's model clearly while staying narrower than modern universalizing psychology claims.
