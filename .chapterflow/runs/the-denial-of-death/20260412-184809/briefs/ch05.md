Core Claim:
Becker turns to Kierkegaard as a deeper psychologist because Kierkegaard sees anxiety, inwardness, and selfhood as spiritual problems rather than only technical symptoms. This chapter matters because it pushes beyond character structure into the question of what it means for a self to confront despair, dependence, and the difficulty of becoming a whole person before transcendence. The chapter must stay narrow: Kierkegaard is used as a lens on anxiety and inward struggle, not as an excuse for a broad theology survey.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
This is the Kierkegaard pivot chapter. It must feel like Becker widening psychology toward inwardness, despair, and the self's spiritual task. It must not collapse into generic religion talk and must not pre-solve Chapter 6's critique of Freud or later chapters on Rank, love, or mental illness.

Author Logic Chain:
First the author argues that a merely clinical psychology misses the depth of human inward struggle.
Then the author turns to Kierkegaard as a thinker who sees anxiety and despair as conditions of selfhood.
Then the author shows that the self's problem is not just symptom reduction but the difficulty of standing honestly before dependence, finitude, and transcendence.
Then the author concludes that deeper psychology must include the spiritual dimension of becoming a self.
