Book: Meditations
Chapter: 8
Title: Book VIII
Locked edition: George Long translation (Project Gutenberg ebook 15877)

Frozen support:
- Book VIII opens by telling Marcus to stop caring how he seems and to live the rest of life as nature wills, with good defined only by virtues like justice, temperance, courage, and freedom.
- The chapter repeatedly asks for present-act accountability: ask of every act how it stands with you, regret neglected useful things, and stop choosing to be good tomorrow rather than today.
- Marcus returns to social action, plain speech, correction without blame, and the uselessness of fault-finding when one can instead correct the cause or the thing itself.
- It presses classic Stoic reductions around body, fame, death, pain, imagination, and first appearances, often by insisting that no external thing harms the ruling faculty unless judgment adds the harm.
- Later sections intensify the theme of complaint removal: turn aside from what is bitter, stop asking why such things exist, accept hindrance conditionally, reunite yourself with the whole, and use every obstacle as material.

Supported chapter-local notes:
- Book VIII is a present-action and anti-complaint book.
- The opening anti-fame move matters because Marcus admits he is visibly far from the reputation of a philosopher and tells himself to drop appearance-management.
- The chapter is unusually direct about procrastinated goodness: tomorrow-thinking is named as a moral failure.
- It repeatedly ties plainness, usefulness, and non-complaining action together.
- The book keeps reconnecting individual discipline to natural unity and social relation.

Allowed claims:
- seeming philosophical is worthless compared to living by nature now
- useful action in the present matters more than self-display or regret loops
- fault-finding should give way to correction where possible
- obstacles and disagreeable materials can be used without complaint
- the ruling faculty stays unharmed unless judgment joins the disturbance

Do not claim:
- that Marcus is satisfied with hypocrisy so long as appearance is dropped
- that pain or external loss are literally nothing
- that social duty means never correcting others
- that the chapter advocates passivity instead of redirected action
