Book: Meditations
Chapter: 7
Title: Book VII
Locked edition: George Long translation (Project Gutenberg ebook 15877)

Frozen support:
- Book VII opens by saying badness is familiar, nothing is new, and principles die only if matching thoughts are allowed to go out.
- Marcus repeatedly commands recovery of life through restored judgment, attention to speech and movement, and quick handling of present things instead of fear of future ones.
- The chapter strengthens social logic through one law, one common reason, membership in the system of rational beings, and the duty to be helped when help serves society.
- It returns to classic Stoic boundaries around harm, ruling faculty, imagination, change, fame, pain, death, and the need to examine first appearances without adding inner surplus.
- Later sections press practical maxims: be good whatever others do, love even wrongdoers as kin, attend to the present act, treat pain without imaginative addition, and use what happens as material for virtue.

Supported chapter-local notes:
- Book VII is a dense maxim-gathering book rather than a single-image chapter.
- Recurrent pressure comes from reanimation of principles: the mind must keep fanning its best judgments back into flame.
- The book repeatedly joins rational and political life rather than separating them.
- Change, fame, pain, and death are compressed into familiar matter that should not shock the ruling part.
- The chapter is rich in short operational commands and perspective resets.

Allowed claims:
- principles stay alive only if corresponding thoughts are kept active
- future fear weakens when the same present reason is trusted ahead of time
- one common law and reason bind rational beings into cooperation
- first appearances should be held apart from added judgments
- pain and fame shrink when imagination and false scale are removed

Do not claim:
- that Marcus erases all novelty in a literal historical sense
- that help from others makes one morally weaker
- that social membership removes the distinct work of one's own ruling faculty
- that the chapter endorses emotional deadness rather than disciplined judgment
