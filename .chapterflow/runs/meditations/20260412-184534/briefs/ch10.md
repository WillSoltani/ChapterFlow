Core Claim:
Book X asks the soul whether it will ever become good, simple, one, and content with what comes from the gods. Marcus drives toward inner nakedness, social duty, and full acceptance of what nature assigns, while cutting off vanity, complaint, and distraction.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
It is the simplicity-and-sufficiency book. The opening interrogates the soul directly, then the chapter keeps returning to contentment with present condition, part-whole reasoning, maintained moral names, contemplation of change, and the command to stop talking about goodness and simply be good.

Author Logic Chain:
First Marcus asks whether the soul will ever become simple, affectionate, content, and free of craving.
Then he grounds that in nature: bear what can be borne, act socially, and accept what belongs to the whole.
Then he presses stable self-naming around goodness, rationality, equanimity, and magnanimity.
Then he strips away vanity, fear, and social comparison through contemplation of change, mortality, and insignificance.
Finally he returns to internal sovereignty, asking what the ruling faculty is now making itself and whether it lives as reason everywhere.

Required Anchors:
- the opening interrogation of the soul about being good, simple, one, and naked
- the part-whole argument that nothing assigned by the whole harms the part
- the maintained names passage: good, modest, true, rational, equanimous, magnanimous
- the command to stop talking about what a good man should be and be such
- the repeated calls to contemplate change, live as on a mountain, and ask what the ruling faculty is now doing

Specific Applications:

- treat present assignment as enough for goodness
- use repeated self-audit to keep the ruling faculty from mixing with vanity and fear

Common Misreadings:
- reading simplicity as passivity or emptiness
- treating part-whole contentment as resignation without social action
- flattening magnanimity into confidence branding

Counterarguments or Limits:
The chapter does not ask Marcus to stop caring about justice or other people. Its simplification is meant to preserve those duties by removing vanity, complaint, and hunger for better conditions before acting. It is inward stripping for the sake of better action, not retreat from action.
