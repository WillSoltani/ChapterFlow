Core Claim:
Book III intensifies the pressure of limited life by adding a harsher limit: understanding may fail before death arrives. Marcus pushes the reader to hurry toward clear judgment, purposeful action, and clean attention while there is still intellectual force left. The chapter's real move is to turn mortality, distraction, and public praise into reasons to protect the inner daemon with disciplined vision.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
It is the make-haste book. It combines urgency, beauty in natural process, disdain for idle social curiosity, and the demand to keep principles ready like tools.

Author Logic Chain:
First Marcus reminds himself that not only life but understanding itself can run out.
Then he trains perception to see nature's beauty even in decay and consequence.
Then he attacks distraction by other people's motives and praise.
Then he tightens the standards around purpose, justice, and the few things worth preferring.
Finally he calls for principles kept ready and idle hopes thrown away while time remains.

Required Anchors:
- the opening warning that understanding may fail before life ends
- the examples of beauty in ripe figs, bent grain, age, and natural consequence
- the instruction not to waste life in thoughts about others
- the commands to stand erect, keep principles ready, and hurry while it is still in your power

Frameworks or Terms Introduced:

Specific Applications:
- stop postponing the work of clear judgment
- resist wasting attention on other people's motives and praise
- prepare brief governing principles before a difficult day rather than improvising under pressure

Common Misreadings:
- taking urgency as panic rather than disciplined haste
- treating the beauty passage as decorative instead of perceptual training
- reading indifference to praise as social contempt instead of clarity about value

Counterarguments or Limits:
The chapter does not ask for frantic busyness. It asks for purposeful economy. The useful claim is that time and attention are too short to spend on vanity, idle curiosity, or delayed self-command.

Previous Chapter Bridge: Book II established dawn discipline against friction and distraction.
