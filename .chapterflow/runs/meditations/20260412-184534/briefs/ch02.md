Core Claim:
Book II turns inherited standards into active daily discipline. Marcus begins by expecting difficult people, reminding himself of shared nature, shrinking the body to flesh and breath, and insisting that the ruling part stay free, purposeful, and uncorrupted. The chapter's real move is not optimism but readiness: act each day as if time is short and keep judgment aligned with nature instead of being dragged by irritation, distraction, or fear.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
It is the dawn-and-friction book. Unlike Book I, it is not a ledger of influences. Unlike later books, it is unusually compressed around morning readiness, mortality, providence, and the demand to act now.

Author Logic Chain:
First Marcus tells himself to expect difficult people and answer them without hatred.
Then he strips identity down to flesh, breath, and ruling faculty.
Then he reconnects individual action to providence, nature, and the shortness of time.
Then he insists that every act be done as if it were the last and that attention stay inward rather than prying into other minds.
Finally he closes on opinion, mortality, and philosophy as the guardian of the inner daemon.

Required Anchors:
- the opening morning reminder about busybodies, the ungrateful, and cooperation like feet and hands
- the reduction of the self to flesh, breath, and the ruling part
- the instruction to do each act as if it were the last
- the claims that all is opinion, that the present is all anyone loses, and that philosophy keeps the daemon within free from violence

Frameworks or Terms Introduced:
Morning readiness; shared nature; ruling faculty; providence; mortality as urgency; opinion and assent; the daemon within.

Specific Applications:
- prepare for difficult people without treating their vice as permission for your own
- shrink ego by separating externals from the ruling part
- act with purpose instead of scattering attention across other people's minds

Common Misreadings:
- reading the opening as passive tolerance instead of disciplined cooperation
- turning mortality into despair rather than urgency
- flattening "all is opinion" into anything-goes subjectivism

Counterarguments or Limits:
The chapter does not deny injury, conflict, or real pain. Its narrower claim is that moral damage begins when judgment gives externals rule over the inner faculty. Keep the limit visible: cooperation does not require naivety, and detachment does not erase public duty.
