Book XII opens with a surprising claim: the things you hope to reach by a long detour can be had now, if you stop refusing them to yourself. Marcus is not promising instant control over circumstances. He is saying that the shortest path to steadiness is available in the present ruling faculty. Leave the past alone, trust the future to providence, and direct the present according to piety and justice. The first movement of the final book is therefore compression. It strips away circuitous self-delay and asks what can be done immediately by a soul that stops handing its life to memory, forecast, and external pressure.

That compression becomes sharper when Marcus asks why people value the opinions of others more than their own. The question is humiliating because it is familiar. We claim to love ourselves most, yet live as if neighbors' judgments carried more authority than the mind that must actually answer for our conduct. Book XII attacks this dependence because it is one of the fastest ways the ruling faculty becomes alien to itself. A soul cannot live presently if it is always kneeling before the imagined tribunal outside it.

From there Marcus tightens ethics rather than relaxing them. Presentness does not mean drift, and opinion-control does not mean relativism. The chapter keeps bringing conduct back to fixed terms: say the truth, do what justice requires, and make action answer to a social end. When he writes, "If it is not right, do not do it: if it is not true, do not say it," he is not offering a decorative maxim. He is naming the non-negotiables that remain after noise, vanity, and fear are cut down to scale. The more externals are reduced, the more decisive rightness and truth become.

This is also why the chapter's line about opinion has to be handled carefully. Marcus says everything is opinion, then immediately uses that claim to return responsibility to judgment. He is not erasing the world. He is refusing to let appearances govern the soul without examination. Disturbance arrives mixed with assent. Cast away the opinion and the soul is saved, not because injustice ceases to exist, but because the ruling faculty stops collaborating with needless additions: panic, vanity, grievance, and dramatized self-importance. Book XII turns opinion into the fast lever because it sits nearest to disturbance.

Mortality gives the chapter its severity. Marcus keeps narrowing life to the present fragment actually being lived, then sets that fragment against the abyss of past and future, the smallness of matter, the short career of fame, and the constant turnover of persons and events. This is not pessimism. It is scale correction. Much of what seems crushing becomes lighter once seen inside change, recurrence, and brevity. The chapter is trying to make drift look expensive and pride look ridiculous because both waste the only time that can actually be used.

Even providence serves this compression. Whether events come by fixed order, providence, or some confusion, Marcus keeps returning to the same decisive site: what is the ruling faculty doing with what arrives? Do not blame the gods. Do not blame men for what they do involuntarily. Do not act without purpose. Refer your acts to the social end. The final book keeps stripping away every excuse for spiritual diffusion until one question remains: how does the ruling faculty make use of itself right now? If that question is faced honestly, much of the surrounding agitation loses jurisdiction.

Book XI asked whether the soul could stay complete in public friction. Book XII asks what remains when the whole discipline is compressed to its bare commands. The answer is not vague calm. It is presentness without drift, opinion without surrender to appearance, truth without disguise, justice without postponement, and mortality without self-pity. Marcus closes the work by putting everything back into the soul's own use of itself. The shortest road was never elsewhere.
