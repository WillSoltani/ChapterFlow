Core Claim:
This chapter argues that better decision-making requires other people who can challenge, calibrate, and refine your thinking. Duke uses the buddy system to show that solitary judgment is too vulnerable to self-serving stories, selective memory, and ego-protective updating. The chapter has to present outside feedback as disciplined support, not as vague encouragement.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
This is the chapter where the book turns from private learning to shared correction. If earlier chapters taught how to price beliefs and learn from outcomes, this chapter teaches why another mind is needed to keep those moves honest.

Author Logic Chain:
First Duke shows why individual thinkers are unreliable auditors of their own certainty and revisions.
Then she explains what a useful thinking partner does and does not do.
Then she frames the buddy system as a method for challenging bias without turning every exchange into a fight.
Then she shows how better social feedback strengthens judgment quality over time.

Required Anchors:
- The need for a trusted thinking partner or decision buddy.
- Outside correction as protection against self-serving narratives and biased updates.
- The difference between supportive challenge and blind agreement.

Frameworks or Terms Introduced:
- buddy system: trusted outside challenge for decisions and reviews
- truthseeking feedback: shared effort to find what is true instead of who feels right
- accountability partner for thinking: someone who checks confidence, evidence, and revision quality
- social calibration: using another mind to reduce self-deception

Specific Applications:
- Use a thinking partner to review forecasts before and after outcomes arrive.
- Replace agreement-seeking with truth-seeking in decision conversations.
- Build routines where someone else can question confidence and evidence quality.

Common Misreadings:
- The chapter is just about emotional support.
- A good buddy should mainly reinforce your confidence.

Counterarguments or Limits:
The chapter must acknowledge that not every friend is a good decision partner and that poor feedback can harden bias instead of correcting it. Its point is not social comfort. Its point is disciplined outside correction.
