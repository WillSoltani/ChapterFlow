Chapter: ch02 — Life is poker, not chess
Part: Core framework
Richness: high
Source IDs: src-openlibrary-edition, src-tedneward-summary, src-gq-interview
Canonical edition: 2018 Portfolio/Penguin English edition, ISBN 9780735216358
Quote posture: paraphrase_first

Core claim:
- Real life contains hidden information and luck, so decisions must be judged more like poker hands than chess positions.
- A good decision can still lose, and a bad decision can still win, without destroying the distinction between skill and luck.
- Admitting uncertainty is a more accurate and useful stance than pretending certainty.

Distinct mechanism:
- The chapter uses poker versus chess to show why black-and-white judgment is structurally wrong in many real situations.
- It links human certainty hunger to resulting and to overconfidence.
- It reframes calibrated confidence as a practical strength.

Required anchors:
- Chess gives near-complete visibility; poker does not.
- People resist saying "I'm not sure" even when the situation warrants it.
- Good process and good outcome can diverge in a probabilistic environment.

Common misreadings:
- Poker means luck dominates so process barely matters.
- Uncertainty talk is weakness.

Specific applications:
- Review decisions by what was visible when the call was made.
- Use ranges and probabilities instead of fake certainty when discussing forecasts.
- Keep accountability focused on process quality rather than on single outcomes alone.

Hard-edge requirement:
- Hard mode must keep both truths alive: uncertainty is real, and decisive action is still required.

Threshold question:
- How do you act with conviction while staying honest about missing information?
