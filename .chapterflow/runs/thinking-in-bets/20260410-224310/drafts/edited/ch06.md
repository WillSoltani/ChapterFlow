Groups often treat harmony as proof that the thinking is solid. Duke argues that this is a dangerous shortcut. When a room moves too quickly into agreement, weak assumptions stay untouched, missing evidence stays hidden, and the team can walk out feeling more certain than the decision deserves.

Her answer is dissent. Not noise, not ego display, and not reflexive contrarianism. Duke wants disagreement that improves the decision by testing whether the claim is too confident, incomplete, or socially protected. A useful dissenter names the risk that comfort is hiding and asks the question the room is avoiding.

The chapter depends on a hard distinction between evidence conflict and ego conflict. Evidence conflict is about whether the forecast, assumption, or interpretation is strong enough. Ego conflict is about status, identity, and who feels threatened by being challenged. If groups confuse those two, they either punish dissent or let disagreement turn personal. Neither one improves judgment.

Better groups build norms that protect challenge without glamorizing hostility. Before a decision closes, someone should be able to raise the strongest objection, point to the weakest assumption, and ask what evidence would change the group's view. That makes dissent part of the process rather than a breach of team loyalty.

Duke does not romanticize conflict. More heat does not guarantee more truth. Poorly managed dissent can become chaos or status theater just as easily as suppressed dissent can become false confidence. The standard is disciplined disagreement in service of accuracy. That sets up the next move in the book, where the lens expands beyond present-group conflict toward future-oriented thinking.
