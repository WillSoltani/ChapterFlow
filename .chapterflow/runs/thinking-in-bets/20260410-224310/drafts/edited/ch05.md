The same mind that makes a decision often has to review it later. Duke treats that as a structural weakness, not a minor quirk. People are skilled at turning their own motives into noble ones, their own evidence into stronger evidence, and their own revisions into cleaner revisions than they really were. Left alone, they can build a convincing story about why they were right, why the outcome was misleading, or why their latest update is obviously the honest one.

That is why Duke turns to the buddy system. A good decision buddy is not just supportive company. It is an outside mind that can see self-serving moves you are least likely to catch in yourself. The point is not mainly comfort or agreement. The point is better thinking. A real buddy asks sharper questions, notices weak evidence, and pushes back on convenient rewrites before they harden.

This changes the meaning of support. Most people want agreement when they feel uncertain or embarrassed. Duke wants something tougher. She wants a partner who is on your side in the long run, not someone who automatically defends your current story. That difference is what separates truth-seeking correction from reassurance.

A strong thinking partner helps both before and after outcomes arrive. Before a decision, the buddy can question confidence, assumptions, and what evidence is actually carrying the claim. After an outcome, the buddy can test whether you are updating honestly or using elegant language to protect ego. In both directions, the outside voice keeps the process from becoming a private courtroom where you get to act as both defense attorney and judge.

Duke is careful about the limit. Not every supportive person is a good decision buddy, and not every challenge is useful. The partner has to be trusted enough to hear the truth and sharp enough to confront it. That is why the chapter matters. Private calibration still needs outside friction when embarrassment, status, or self-protection enter the room.
