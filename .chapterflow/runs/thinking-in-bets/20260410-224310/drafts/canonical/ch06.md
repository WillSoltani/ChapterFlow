Agreement feels efficient in groups because agreement lowers social friction. When everyone nods, a room can mistake comfort for clarity. Duke uses this chapter to argue that the calm surface of consensus often hides a decision problem. If no one pushes back, weak assumptions stay untested, missing evidence stays missing, and the group leaves with more confidence than it actually earned.

That is why Duke treats dissent as a decision tool rather than as a personality problem. Useful dissent is not random opposition and it is not status performance. It is disagreement aimed at discovering whether the current story is incomplete, too certain, or too convenient. A dissenter can improve the group's judgment by naming the risk no one wants to raise, asking what evidence is being ignored, or separating what feels harmonious from what is actually well supported.

The chapter also makes a sharp distinction between evidence conflict and ego conflict. Evidence conflict is about the claim, the forecast, or the assumptions underneath it. Ego conflict is about who gets to look smart, who gets protected, and who feels challenged by the disagreement itself. Groups often avoid dissent because they confuse those two things. Duke wants them disentangled so challenge can stay useful instead of turning into personal combat or polite silence.

This changes what good group norms look like. A strong group does not reward the loudest contrarian or the smoothest consensus-builder. It creates enough safety for unpopular concerns to be voiced and enough discipline for those concerns to stay tied to evidence. Before a decision locks, the group should know who sees the main risk, what assumption is weakest, and what counterargument has not yet been answered. Those norms make better forecasts and better postmortems because they reduce the cost of telling the room it may be wrong.

Duke is careful about the limit. More conflict is not automatically more truth. A room can decay into chaos, status theater, or endless objection if disagreement becomes a performance instead of a search. The point is not maximum friction. The point is productive dissent that helps the group think more honestly than comfort would allow. From there the book can widen its horizon again, asking how decision quality changes when people learn not only to hear each other better in the present, but also to think across time and toward their future selves.
