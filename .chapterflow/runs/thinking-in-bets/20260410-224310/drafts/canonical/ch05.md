One of the hardest things about improving judgment is that the same mind making the decision is often the mind reviewing it later. Annie Duke treats that as a structural problem, not as a small personal flaw. People are talented at turning their own motives into noble ones, their own evidence into stronger evidence, and their own revisions into cleaner revisions than they really were. Left alone, they can talk themselves into almost any story about why they were right, why the result was misleading, or why the update they just made was obviously the sensible one.

That is why Duke turns to the buddy system. A good decision buddy is not just company. A good buddy is an outside mind that can see the self-serving moves you are most likely to miss in yourself. The role is not mainly to comfort you, back you up, or protect your confidence. The role is to help you think more honestly by asking better questions, noticing weak evidence, and challenging convenient rewrites before they harden into belief.

This changes what support means. Most people instinctively want agreement when they feel uncertain or embarrassed. They want someone to say the decision was understandable, the outcome was unfair, or the evidence still basically supports the original story. Duke wants something tougher. She wants a partner who is on your side in the sense that they care about your long-term judgment, not in the sense that they automatically defend your current explanation. That distinction is what makes the buddy system useful instead of merely soothing.

A real thinking partner improves both forecasting and review. Before a decision, the buddy can question confidence, assumptions, and what evidence is actually carrying the claim. After an outcome, the buddy can test whether you are updating honestly or protecting your ego with elegant language. In both directions, the outside voice keeps the process from becoming a private courtroom where you always get to act as both defense attorney and judge.

Duke is careful not to make the chapter sentimental. Not every supportive person is a good decision buddy, and not every disagreement is useful. A weak partner can reinforce your blind spots just as easily as a harsh one can shut thinking down. What matters is the mix of trust and candor. The partner has to be safe enough that you will tell the truth, and sharp enough that the truth will still get challenged when it needs to be.

The chapter matters because solitary calibration has limits. Earlier chapters taught the reader how to price beliefs and learn from feedback, but those skills can still be corrupted by self-serving stories once emotion, embarrassment, or status get involved. A buddy system creates friction against that corruption. It gives truth a second voice in the room.

That is also why the chapter leads naturally into dissent. Once one trusted partner becomes valuable, the next question is what happens when a whole group learns how to challenge one another without turning the room into chaos or flattery. Duke is moving from private honesty toward shared truth-seeking. The buddy system is the bridge.
