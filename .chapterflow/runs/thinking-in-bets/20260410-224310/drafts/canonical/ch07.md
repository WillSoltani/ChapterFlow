People often make decisions from too small a frame. The current emotion is loud, the current room is loud, and the current deadline is loud. Duke appears to use this chapter to argue that better judgment sometimes comes from stepping outside that immediate frame and asking how the choice would look from farther down the line. The chapter title points toward mental time travel, which suggests using a future vantage point to inspect the present more clearly.

That move does not eliminate uncertainty. It changes perspective. A person can ask what a future self might say about the confidence they are showing now, what later evidence would matter most in reviewing the choice, or how today's pressure may be shrinking the visible field. The benefit is not magical foresight. The benefit is that distance can loosen the hold of immediate emotion, social pressure, and short-run comfort.

This chapter naturally fits the rest of the book's method. Earlier chapters taught readers to price beliefs, learn from outcomes, invite outside correction, and use dissent to improve group judgment. Mental time travel seems to add one more corrective. It lets a thinker test a present decision against a later review point instead of letting the present moment act like the only court that matters.

The chapter likely needs a limit stated clearly. Imagining the future is not the same as knowing it. Duke's method throughout the book resists false certainty, so this closing move should not turn into confident fantasy. A widened frame helps because it exposes current bias, not because it removes uncertainty from the decision.

That is what makes the title feel like a real closing move. Better decisions come from seeing more of the problem than the present moment wants to show. A future-facing perspective can help readers ask what later judgment, later evidence, or later selves would notice that current comfort is hiding. The goal stays the same as before: better judgment under uncertainty, now with a longer horizon around the choice.
