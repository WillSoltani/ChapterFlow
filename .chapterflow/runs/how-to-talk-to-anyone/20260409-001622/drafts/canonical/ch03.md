Most people notice verbal status only after it has already done its work. A conversation can be warm, active, and full of goodwill, yet one clumsy answer still lowers the level of how you sound. Someone asks what you do. Someone praises your work. Someone important enters the exchange. Suddenly the question is no longer whether you can keep talking. It is whether your language makes you sound composed or hungry. This part argues that people sound substantial less by claiming importance than by removing the little verbal leaks that make them sound needy, vague, or overeager.

That is why `What Do You Do-Not!` and `The Nutshell Resume` belong near the front of this cluster. Many people hear "What do you do?" as an invitation to justify themselves. Their answer starts wandering, expands to cover every credential they hope will land, and leaves the listener doing cleanup work. The chapter pushes the opposite move. Compress. Shape. Give the other person a usable identity sketch instead of a verbal rummage bag. A nutshell answer sounds stronger not because it is grander, but because it suggests you know which parts of yourself matter in this setting. Concision, here, is not shyness. It is control over how much explanation you need before the room can place you.

The next set of techniques shows that status also lives at the level of diction. `Your Personal Thesaurus`, `Don't Touch a Cliche with a Ten-Foot Pole`, `Use Jawsmith's Jive`, and `Call a Spade a Spade` all point at the same risk: generic language makes you sound like you borrowed your thoughts on the way in. Cliches save effort, but they also flatten distinction. Vague praise, padded business talk, and recycled phrasing all tell the listener that you are reaching for ready-made speech instead of saying what you actually mean. This chapter is not asking for ornate vocabulary. It is asking for exactness. The right word makes you sound as though you have already sorted your thinking before speaking. That feels different from trying to sound smart in public.

Just as important, polished talk is not only about the words you choose for yourself. It is also about where the center of the exchange sits. `Kill the Quick "Me, Too!"`, `Comm-YOU-nication`, and `It's the Receiver's Ball` all attack the same low-status impulse: the need to rush in and claim sameness, relevance, or equal billing before the other person has finished being seen. Quick "me, too" responses often come from a good instinct, but they can cheapen the interaction by making your connection bid too visible. Receiver-centered talk sounds more substantial because it is not scrambling for proof that you belong. It lets the other person's point land before deciding what to add. That restraint is part of polish. You do not sound smaller because you spoke less. You sound smaller when your eagerness becomes audible.

The limit case appears most clearly around prestige and gratitude. `Big Shots Don't Slobber` and `Never the Naked Thank You` expose opposite versions of the same problem. One makes people gush upward around importance. The other leaves appreciation so bare that it sounds undercooked. In both directions, the issue is not manners alone. It is proportion. If a famous person, powerful manager, or admired professor enters the room and your language suddenly gets wetter, flatter, or more worshipful, the room can hear the status swing happen inside you. If your thank-you is naked and generic, the room hears that you did not take the trouble to name the value. Polished gratitude is specific without groveling. Polished admiration is warm without spilling over into slobber.

That is why this chapter is sharper than it first sounds. It is not just about sounding educated. It is about sounding unhurried in the right places. `The Exclusive Smile` and `Trash the Teasing` support that broader posture. Forced insider joking, cheap teasing, and overfamiliar charm can all lower the level of your language because they reveal how quickly you are trying to manufacture intimacy or social advantage. The part keeps pressing toward the same standard: use language that sounds chosen, not grabbed. Give attention that sounds directed, not clingy. Let precision replace inflation. Let warmth replace performance.

Still, there is a trap under all of this. Once polish becomes self-conscious, the chapter breaks. People can hear when somebody is swapping in thesaurus words, avoiding every common phrase like a contest, or shaping every answer as if it were going on a plaque. That is no longer composed speech. It is visible management. The same restraint that signals status can become stiffness. The same precision that sounds deliberate can become sterile. So the real lesson is not "sound fancy." It is remove the verbal habits that make your neediness louder than your meaning.

Seen together, these techniques answer the question Chapter 2 leaves behind. After you learn how to keep conversation alive, how do you make your words sound worth listening to? You do it by speaking with clearer borders. Answer cleanly. Choose language that carries your real thought. Stop lunging for false sameness. Keep the receiver in view. Show gratitude without shrinking or gushing. Then your words start sounding less like bids for approval and more like the natural surface of a settled person. And once your speech has that kind of shape, a new problem appears: how do you enter rooms where everyone already seems to speak a language you have not learned yet?
