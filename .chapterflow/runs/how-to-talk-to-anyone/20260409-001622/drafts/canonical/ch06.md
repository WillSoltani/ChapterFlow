Everyone has heard both kinds of compliment. One lands and changes the room a little. You can feel the person straighten, soften, or carry the words with them after the moment ends. The other lands with a faint chemical smell. It may sound positive on the surface, yet something in it makes the listener brace, smile politely, or wonder what is being bought. This part argues that the difference is credibility. Praise becomes powerful when it feels observed, specific, and socially believable. Flattery fails when the motive gets louder than the recognition.

That is why the chapter begins with indirect praise. `Grapevine Glory`, `Carrier Pigeon Kudos`, `Implied Magnificence`, and `Accidental Adulation` all point toward the same truth: admiration often carries more weight when it does not arrive as a blunt bid for approval. Praise that travels through others, through implication, or through a context that makes it feel less self-serving can sound cleaner because it carries less visible agenda. The chapter is not glorifying indirection for its own sake. It is saying people trust praise more when it sounds like a real observation that would still exist even if no social reward came back to the speaker.

The direct-praise cluster sharpens that distinction. `The Killer Compliment`, `Little Strokes`, and `The Knee-Jerk "Wow!"` all suggest that praise works best when it is both specific and proportionate. A giant speech can feel heavy or embarrassing if the observation inside it is thin. A small but exact line can feel stronger because it sounds earned. "You handled that question with real calm" carries more weight than a gush about how amazing someone always is. The knee-jerk reaction matters for the same reason. Immediate admiration can feel clean when it sounds like you were genuinely struck, not like you had the compliment prepared in your pocket.

Then the chapter goes deeper with `Boomeranging`. The strongest praise does not only describe a visible act. It reflects a valued trait back to the person. Instead of complimenting the presentation, you name the steadiness, generosity, discipline, or judgment that produced it. That changes the force of the recognition. Surface praise tells people what looked good. Boomeranging tells them what kind of person they appeared to be in the moment. That can stay with someone longer because it does not only reward performance. It affirms identity.

`The Tombstone Game` pushes that logic as far as the chapter can take it. The deepest compliment is often about what someone would want remembered, not what looked impressive for thirty seconds. Legacy-frame praise has unusual power because it reaches for durable character rather than momentary polish. The point is not to speak in grand speeches about immortality. It is to ask what quality, if named, would feel permanently true rather than cheaply flattering. This is where the chapter stops sounding like compliment advice and starts sounding like social discernment. Praise matters most when it makes people feel accurately seen at the level they most hope is real.

But the chapter only holds because it keeps the danger visible. Praise becomes flattery the instant it starts smelling like leverage. If the compliment is too broad, too intense, badly timed, or disconnected from what anyone else could plausibly observe, it exposes the speaker instead of elevating the listener. Even true admiration can go bad when it arrives with too much social oil on it. The listener starts hearing your need to please, impress, soften, or win something. And once that motive becomes audible, the positive words stop feeling like recognition and start feeling like pressure.

That is why this part is less sentimental than it first appears. It is really about disciplined admiration. Notice something real. Name it with the right weight. Let the compliment travel in a form the situation can believe. Aim beneath surface shine when the moment can support it. And stop before your praise becomes another way of talking about yourself. Do that well and recognition can genuinely lift people. Do it badly and flattery cheapens both of you.
