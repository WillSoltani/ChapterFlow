Core Claim:
Most preventable thinking errors are not exotic. They recur in recognizable patterns tied to self-interest, overconfidence, conformity, memory limits, and emotional pressure. The practical advantage comes from learning to spot the pattern early enough to interrupt it. This chapter should teach the reader to treat mistakes less like surprises and more like familiar traps.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
It reframes error as patterned, ordinary, and diagnosable. The chapter is not about one bias or one tool. It is about building the habit of pattern recognition before a decision hardens.

Author Logic Chain:
First the author explains that judgment is shaped by brain, experience, situation, fear, reward, status, and uncertainty.
Then the author shows that misjudgments recur in families rather than appearing as unique accidents.
Then the author points toward a checklist mindset: if patterns recur, decisions can be reviewed against them before commitment.

Specific Applications:
- slow a high-stakes decision by asking which familiar pattern it resembles
- separate a public commitment from the evidence that now argues against it
- notice when group agreement is being mistaken for proof

Common Misreadings:
- thinking the chapter says every mistake fits a neat label
- treating pattern recognition as cynicism rather than prevention

Counterarguments or Limits:
Patterns help only when the reader stays humble about uncertainty. Over-labeling can become its own distortion. The chapter should preserve the limit that not every hard call reduces cleanly to one bias.
