Knowing the better move is not the same as being able to use it when the moment turns hot. A person can understand the principle, respect the checklist, and still lose the decision because fear, ego, embarrassment, or emotional speed gets there first. The mistake then looks irrational from the outside, but from the inside it often felt urgent, protective, or deserved.

That is why temperament matters. Bevelin's broader argument about misjudgment is not only about collecting better ideas. It is also about keeping access to those ideas when social pressure, status threat, reward, or fear starts distorting the picture. Knowledge can be present and still become unreachable if the emotional temperature in the room gets high enough.

Fear and ego are especially dangerous because they borrow the language of necessity. Fear says act now or lose everything. Ego says defend yourself now or lose standing. In both cases, the mind begins treating reaction as judgment. A sharp reply in a meeting, a reckless trade, a defensive explanation, or a status-driven choice can then feel justified in the moment and foolish once the pressure has passed.

Memory and self-story make the problem worse. After the fact, people revise what they saw, what they intended, and why they acted. The mind protects itself by editing the record. That makes learning harder because the lesson gets bent around pride, self-interest, or selective recall. If the story keeps getting cleaned up afterward, the same mistake stays available the next time pressure returns.

Temperament is a practical protection against that drift. It does not mean becoming emotionless. It means slowing the first reaction long enough to let judgment re-enter the room. It means noticing when fear is borrowing certainty, when ego is demanding a fast defense, or when the urge to act is mainly an urge to escape discomfort. A steadier person is not someone with no feeling. It is someone less willing to let feeling seize the steering wheel unchecked.

That is also why the chapter belongs at the end of the book. Models help. Probability helps. Checklists help. None of them help much if the user abandons them at the exact moment they are most needed. The deeper task is to build enough steadiness that knowledge, process, and perspective remain usable under live pressure rather than only in calm hindsight.

The book's closing standard is therefore realistic. Wisdom is not perfect control and not freedom from error. It is a quieter, harder aim: reduce preventable mistakes by seeing clearly, thinking honestly, and staying steady enough to use what you already know when it matters. That standard does not remove uncertainty. It makes self-inflicted damage a little less frequent, which is what serious practical judgment has been trying to do all along.
