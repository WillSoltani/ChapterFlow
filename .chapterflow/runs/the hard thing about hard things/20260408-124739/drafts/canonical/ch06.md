# Chapter 6: Take Care of the People, the Products, and the Profits, In That Order

The dashboard says growth is strong, but the room feels unstable. New hires are arriving every week, managers are improvising job definitions, product decisions are bouncing between teams, and nobody can explain who owns two of the most important priorities. Revenue is up, yet execution is getting noisier. This is where many companies misdiagnose the problem. They assume the business is straining because the market got harder. Often the first failure is closer to home: growth outran the management system.

That is the transition in this chapter. After learning how to survive acute crisis, the task now is to design a company that can keep performing under scale. The central question is practical: when a company grows faster than its management system, what breaks first?

## Growth Outruns Management

The first cracks usually appear in the people system, not the profit line. Role boundaries blur. Managers apply different standards to similar work. Strong contributors get contradictory direction depending on which meeting they attended. Training is deferred because every week feels urgent. These are not soft problems. They are operating failures that eventually show up in product quality and financial results.

Nia, a founder-CEO, sees this in a single month. Her company posts record sales while customer escalations climb. Reed, her operations lead, points out that team leads are making promotion calls without shared criteria. Two groups are shipping related features with incompatible assumptions. Nobody is malicious or lazy. The system is underbuilt for the speed of growth.

If leadership waits for visible profit damage before acting, the repair bill gets larger. By the time margins collapse, the root issues have already been compounding through hiring decisions, management inconsistency, and preventable product rework.

## People, Then Product, Then Profit

The chapter’s ordering rule is causal, not sentimental. Take care of the people, then the products, then the profits. The order matters because each layer depends on the one before it.

People are first because organizations execute through human judgment. If managers cannot set clear expectations, coach performance, and make consistent calls, product quality becomes uneven regardless of strategy slides. Product comes next because market promises are kept through product reliability and usefulness, not through motivational language. Profit follows from repeated good execution, not from declaring profit as the first priority in every meeting.

This is not an anti-profit argument. Profit is required. A company that cannot earn durable returns does not keep serving customers or employing people for long. The point is that profit becomes durable when leadership builds the conditions that generate it repeatedly.

## A Good Place To Work Means A High-Performance Culture

A good place to work is often misunderstood as a place with low friction. In this chapter, it means something tougher: a place where people can do excellent work with clear standards, real support, and accountable leadership.

Comfort without clarity is expensive. If no one can tell whether performance is strong, average, or failing, high performers leave and underperformance spreads quietly. Culture then becomes a story told in all-hands meetings rather than a system that improves execution.

Care and accountability are not opposites. Care is giving people the conditions to succeed and telling the truth when they are not meeting the bar. Accountability is part of care because ambiguity is not kind. It protects short-term harmony while damaging long-term trust.

Nia adjusts her culture language after learning this the hard way. She stops praising general effort and starts rewarding specific behaviors tied to outcomes: clear ownership, thoughtful handoffs, direct feedback, and reliable follow-through. Team morale does not instantly spike. Execution quality improves first, then morale stabilizes around credibility rather than slogans.

## Training And Executive Hiring Are Core Startup Work

Many founders treat training as a luxury for large companies. That belief creates weakness exactly when complexity rises. Training is a force multiplier for a startup because it raises the quality of dozens of decisions leaders will never directly see.

Reed builds a lightweight manager training loop: weekly cases from real incidents, shared criteria for performance reviews, and explicit practice on difficult conversations. None of this looks glamorous. Within a quarter, escalation patterns change. Problems surface earlier, and fixes require less executive intervention because frontline managers can diagnose and act.

Executive hiring is necessary, but it is not a substitute for internal management development. Bringing in one experienced leader does not repair a company where every manager operates from a different playbook. External hires can add leverage only when the surrounding system can absorb and extend their standards.

When training is skipped and hiring is treated as the full solution, leadership bandwidth collapses. Founders become the default reviewer for decisions that should be handled two layers down. Quality becomes inconsistent across teams. The organization starts moving at the pace of executive rescue.

## Management Debt And Its Compounding Cost

Technical debt is familiar. Management debt is less visible but often more dangerous. It accumulates when leaders postpone management discipline because the startup still feels small, fast, and informal.

The early trade is seductive. You skip management basics to ship faster now. No role calibration. No consistent feedback system. No manager onboarding. No clear standard for what good leadership looks like. In the short run, this can look efficient.

Later, the bill arrives with interest. Product decisions conflict because ownership was never clarified. Coordination failures increase as teams multiply. Performance issues linger because managers were never trained to address them. Profit pressure rises because the company pays for rework, delays, attrition, and executive firefighting all at once.

This is why management debt is not a metaphorical warning. It is a delayed operating cost generated by neglected people systems.

## One Operating System, Not Three Competing Priorities

People, product, and profit are often discussed like separate lanes that compete for attention. This chapter argues they are one operating system with ordered dependencies.

Culture quality affects management behavior. Management behavior shapes product decision quality and execution reliability. Product reliability drives customer outcomes and financial durability. Break one link and the downstream links degrade.

A graduate seminar rehearsal offers a smaller parallel. Lena and Soren prepare a high-stakes presentation. They focus on slide polish and timing while ignoring team process. Feedback ownership is unclear, rehearsals are inconsistent, and the final deck has contradictory arguments. They interpret this as a content problem. It is a people-system problem first. Once they assign ownership, set review standards, and practice feedback discipline, the content improves quickly because the team mechanism improves.

The misread to avoid is treating people care, product excellence, and profit goals as ideological choices. In practice, they are operational dependencies.

## The Expensive Delay Pattern

The hardest contradiction in this chapter is psychological. Founders delay management discipline because delay feels rational at small scale. They want speed. They fear bureaucracy. They identify with improvisation. They worry formal systems will slow momentum.

Short-term, that instinct can help. Long-term, it can create expensive fragility.

Nia postpones manager standards for two hiring cycles because she believes her team is still small enough to coordinate informally. Output stays high for a while, so the decision appears correct. Then variation compounds. One manager tolerates weak execution to preserve harmony. Another manages by pressure spikes. A third gives no developmental feedback at all. Product quality starts drifting by team. Customer trust becomes uneven. Finance reports rising costs but cannot isolate one cause because the causes are distributed across management behavior.

What looked like speed was borrowed capacity. The company spent future reliability to buy present motion.

This is the hard edge of the chapter. Delayed discipline does not fail gracefully. It fails as a system, then forces costly correction under pressure. The corrective work is almost always harder than the preventive work leaders postponed.

## Bridge To Going Concern

Once a company installs real management infrastructure, the challenge changes. The next set of hard problems includes process load, executive fit, organizational politics, and the balance between control and adaptability. That is the terrain ahead.

But this chapter sets the precondition for that terrain. If leaders do not build a performance-supporting culture, train managers, hire executives with system fit, and pay down management debt early, later scaling discussions become theater. Strategy cannot compensate for a weak management substrate.

The practical rule remains clear. Take care of the people first by building management quality. Take care of the product next through reliable execution. Then take care of profit through durable performance. Reverse the order and you may still grow, but the growth will be brittle, costly, and hard to sustain.
