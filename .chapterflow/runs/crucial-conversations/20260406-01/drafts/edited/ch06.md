The fight sounds like budget, but it is not really about budget anymore. Owen wants to hire one expensive senior engineer. Priyah wants three juniors. Ten minutes into the conversation, both of them are still citing runway numbers and delivery risk, but the actual room has changed. Owen hears Priyah as willing to gamble the next milestone. Priyah hears Owen as willing to burn the company to protect his preferred strategy. The numbers are still on the screen. Safety is already gone. Chapter 6 begins here because it is the hinge question of the whole framework: once safety breaks, how do you restore it well enough for the hard issue to stay discussable?

The chapter's answer starts with diagnosis. Safety depends on two things at once: Mutual Purpose and Mutual Respect. Mutual Purpose means both people believe the other is working toward a goal they both care about. Mutual Respect means both people believe the other sees them as worth hearing. Lose either one and the conversation starts to leave dialogue. That is why repair begins with a specific question: do they think I am against them, or do they think I am beneath them? A strategy fight usually sounds like content, but underneath it the room may be losing one of those two conditions.

That is what has happened to Owen and Priyah. Their argument looks financial, but it is really a mutual-purpose problem. Each is hearing the other's strategy as proof of bad intent. Chapter 6 answers that kind of failure with CRIB. First commit to finding a purpose that works for both people. Then recognize the purpose underneath the strategy. Owen is not actually devoted to seniority itself. He wants the next milestone to ship on time. Priyah is not actually devoted to junior hiring as a symbol. She wants enough hands to hit the fundraising target without burning cash too fast. Once those purposes are named, the room climbs one level above the strategy fight. Only then can they invent a mutual purpose and brainstorm new ways to serve it. The disagreement is still real, but it is no longer pretending to be a moral war.

The chapter then narrows to the repair move most readers can use tomorrow: Contrasting. Camille tells her direct report Darren, "Your work has been slipping lately," and watches his face change immediately. He is no longer hearing a schedule concern. He is hearing, "I do not believe in you anymore." That is not the moment for a softer repetition of the same sentence. It is the moment to correct the misread cleanly. I do not mean that you are failing as a person or that I have given up on you. I do mean that the last two releases missed their dates, and I want to understand why before the problem gets bigger. The order matters. Correct the false meaning first. Then restate the real purpose. The move only works when the "do not" half is true. Used mechanically, it becomes worse than nothing.

Apology belongs to a different category. It is not for every tense conversation and not for every hurt reaction. It is for the moments when you actually did something that damaged safety. Lila snaps at her brother during a family planning argument, then spends the next hour defending the tone because she still believes her point was valid. Chapter 6 says the valid point is not the issue yet. The safety breach is. A real apology does not perform softness while preserving self-innocence. It acknowledges the specific thing that damaged respect. It gives the room something firmer than tone management: evidence that the speaker can see the harm they actually caused.

The chapter gets stronger when it refuses to turn these moves into scripts. Contrasting is not a trick for saying something hurtful and then escaping the cost. CRIB is not a charm that makes strategy conflict disappear. Apology is not a way to get back to your original point faster. Every move depends on a real diagnosis and a sincere purpose. That is why the chapter keeps returning to safety rather than style. The question is not whether the sentence sounds polished. The question is whether the room can now hold candor again.

It also needs one honest limit. The less-powerful person in a conversation may not be able to restore safety alone. A junior employee speaking upward, a student dealing with a professor, a family member in a long pattern of disrespect may need something outside the framework too: a different forum, an ally, a pause, an exit. Chapter 6 becomes untrustworthy if it pretends one sincere line can repair every unsafe room. Its real promise is narrower and more useful. It teaches the reader to diagnose whether purpose or respect broke, choose the matching repair, and avoid making the room worse through bluntness or fake soothing.

That is why this chapter is the hinge. Until safety is restored, the conversation cannot carry a difficult truth well. Once safety is back, the reader can finally do the next hard thing: say what they actually need to say without blowing the room apart. That is the work of Chapter 7.
