Core Claim:
Most people keep tracking the topic and miss the moment the room itself starts to break. The chapter teaches the reader to watch both content and conditions at once, notice the signals that safety has slipped, and name the exact move happening next: masking, avoiding, withdrawing, controlling, labeling, or attacking. Naming the move early is the practical win because it gives the reader a chance to interrupt it before the conversation fully leaves dialogue.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
This chapter is not about solving the disagreement yet. It is about seeing the turn fast enough that a solution is still possible. Its vocabulary is diagnostic, not moralizing.

Author Logic Chain:
First the author distinguishes content from conditions.
Then the author argues that novices track content only while skilled operators watch both channels.
Then the author identifies two broad failure modes when dialogue breaks: silence and violence.
Then the author breaks those into six recognizable moves.
Then the author argues that naming the move early strips it of momentum.

Required Anchors:
- Zoom design review: a junior colleague's face tightens, they go quiet, and the skilled person names it as withdrawal rather than agreement.
- Relationship argument: one partner starts labeling while the other starts withdrawing, and the whole room turns before the topic changes.
- Heated text thread: masking and labeling are easier to spot because the language is frozen on screen.

Frameworks or Terms Introduced:
- Content: the topic being discussed.
- Conditions: the emotional and relational state of the people discussing it.
- Silence: pulling contribution out of the conversation.
- Violence: forcing meaning into the conversation.
- Masking / Avoiding / Withdrawing: the three silence moves.
- Controlling / Labeling / Attacking: the three violence moves.

Specific Applications:
- catching silent withdrawal in hybrid or video meetings
- spotting labeling before a personal conversation hardens into caricature
- recognizing that hedged text language may be masking rather than politeness

Common Misreadings:
- Silence and violence are not personality types.
- A conversation can still look calm while conditions are collapsing.
- Naming the move is not the same as shaming the person doing it.

Counterarguments or Limits:
The cues are harder to read in remote and cross-cultural settings. The chapter should admit that some silence is harder to see on video and that indirectness is not always dysfunction. Naming the move should stay descriptive enough that it does not become another form of attack.

Previous Chapter Bridge:
Chapter 4 cleaned up the reader's internal interpretation. Chapter 5 turns outward and teaches the reader to notice when the room itself is slipping.

Next Chapter Bridge:
Once the reader can see that safety is gone, the next question is how to put it back. Chapter 6 teaches the repair moves.

Cross-Chapter Tensions:
- topic focus versus room awareness
- descriptive diagnosis versus personal accusation
- early interruption versus overreading ambiguous cues

Moral Complexity:
The chapter should treat silence and violence as human stress moves, not as moral identities. Everyone uses both in different moments.

Concept Budget:
- target concept count: 5
- rationale: content/conditions, silence/violence, plus the six named moves grouped under two clear headings.

Hard-Depth Minimum:
Hard depth must preserve the idea that naming the move is powerful because it interrupts the stress pattern early. It should also preserve the limit that some cues are ambiguous, especially on video and across cultural styles.

Unsupported Zones:
Do not turn the chapter into body-language pseudoscience. Do not imply that every indirect move is manipulative. Do not flatten silence and violence into fixed identity labels.
