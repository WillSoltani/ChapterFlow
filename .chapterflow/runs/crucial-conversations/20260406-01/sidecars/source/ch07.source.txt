Chapter 7 — STATE My Path: How to Speak Persuasively, Not Abrasively
--------------------------------------------------------------------
Now the reader is ready to actually say the hard thing. The STATE framework
is a structured way to put a difficult message into the pool without
triggering the other person's defenses.

  S — SHARE YOUR FACTS. Start with observable, verifiable data — the
      least inflammatory, most objective ground. Not interpretations. Not
      characterizations. Actual facts: what was said, what was done, what
      was in the report.

  T — TELL YOUR STORY. Then share what story you are telling yourself
      about those facts. Own it as your story, not as the truth: "Here is
      what I am starting to believe, based on what I've seen." This is
      where the conversation gets hard, and where most people either
      collapse (skipping straight back to facts) or escalate (presenting
      the story as if it were obviously true).

  A — ASK FOR OTHERS' PATHS. Then invite the other person to add their
      facts and their story. Genuinely invite. Not as a rhetorical move
      to lead them to agree with you.

  T — TALK TENTATIVELY. Soften the language throughout. Not because the
      speaker is unsure of themselves, but because the goal is to keep
      the other person in dialogue. Phrases like "I'm starting to
      wonder" or "it seems to me that" or "I could be wrong, but" make
      the difference between a story that the other person can push back
      on and a story that has the force of a verdict.

  E — ENCOURAGE TESTING. Explicitly welcome disagreement. Tell the other
      person that you want them to challenge the story if it doesn't
      match their experience, and mean it.

The STATE framework is the single most teachable skill in the book, and
it is also the one readers most often butcher. The common failure modes
are: skipping Share (jumping straight to story), presenting story as
fact (no "I'm telling myself" framing), and collapsing under the first
pushback (abandoning the story at the first sign of discomfort).

Modern example to generate: A team lead needs to tell a peer that their
project is missing milestones. A clumsy version: "You're falling behind
and everyone's noticed." A STATE version: "In the last three sprints,
the team has moved two tickets from 'done' back to 'in progress'
[facts]. I'm starting to worry that the project is slipping, and I
wanted to check in before it becomes a bigger problem [story, tentative].
What am I missing? [ask]."
