Chapter 1 — What Is a Crucial Conversation and Why Should I Care?
-----------------------------------------------------------------
Most of the worst moments in a person's work life and in their closest
relationships are not random bad luck. They are the downstream consequence of
a single conversation that was handled poorly, skipped, or replaced with a
surrogate action — a passive-aggressive email, a quiet decision to stop
trying, a sudden outburst over something small. The claim of the book is
that the quality of a person's life is in large part the sum of how they
handle the conversations that matter most.

There are three common responses to a crucial conversation, and two of them
are unhelpful:

  1. AVOID IT. Pretend the conversation isn't necessary. Hope the problem
     resolves itself. This is the default move for most people, and it
     compounds: the unspoken issue grows in the silence.

  2. HANDLE IT BADLY. Blurt. Attack. Shut the other person down. Win the
     moment and lose the relationship. This is the other default, usually
     triggered when avoidance has gone on long enough that the emotion
     overflows.

  3. HANDLE IT WELL. Engage the conversation on purpose, stay in honest
     dialogue, and resolve it in a way that leaves the relationship intact or
     stronger.

The teaching premise is that "handling it well" is a set of learnable skills,
not a trait. Some people come to it easier than others, but nobody is born
with it and everybody can improve.

Teaching implication: This first chapter should convert the reader's vague
sense that "I'm bad at conflict" into a specific recognition of the three
conditions and their own typical default. By the end of the chapter, the
reader should be able to point to a conversation in the last month that
qualified as crucial and name which default they used.
