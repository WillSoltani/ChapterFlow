Chapter 9 — Move to Action: Turning Crucial Conversations Into Results
-----------------------------------------------------------------------
Most crucial conversations fail not in the talking but in what comes after.
People reach an apparent agreement, go back to their desks, and do nothing
different. The final conversational skill is to close the conversation in a
way that actually produces action.

The book distinguishes four ways a decision can be made, and teaches the
reader to pick the right one on purpose rather than defaulting:

  1. COMMAND — Someone with authority decides. Fast, appropriate for
     low-stakes decisions or emergencies. The cost is that the people
     who didn't decide have to be persuaded to care about the outcome.

  2. CONSULT — The decision-maker gathers input from a defined set of
     people, then decides. Most of the best organizational decisions
     get made this way. The failure mode is pretending to consult when
     the decision is already made.

  3. VOTE — A group decides by majority. Fast, legitimate, but leaves
     the minority alienated. Appropriate when any of the options on the
     table would be acceptable and the group just needs closure.

  4. CONSENSUS — Everyone has to agree. Slow, expensive, and appropriate
     for decisions where universal buy-in is actually necessary. Most
     overused in groups that confuse consensus with fairness.

The skill is to decide up front which of these four applies to the
decision at hand, and to be honest with everyone in the room about it.
"I'm going to decide this one, but I want your input first" is a
legitimate statement. "What do you all think?" when the decision is
already made is a dishonest one, and the group will figure it out.

Once a decision is made, the book uses the WWWF closer — actually, the
book's mnemonic is the question set "Who does What by When, and how will
we Follow up?"

  WHO — Name a specific person. "The team" and "we" are not people.
  WHAT — A specific, observable deliverable.
  WHEN — A specific date. "Soon" is not a date.
  FOLLOW-UP — How will everyone know whether the thing was done? Who
              checks? When?

Modern example to generate: A product team has a heated two-hour
meeting about a customer-complaint backlog and leaves feeling like
they've "aligned." No WWWF. Three weeks later the backlog is exactly
the same size. The entire meeting was a crucial conversation that
ended without an action.
