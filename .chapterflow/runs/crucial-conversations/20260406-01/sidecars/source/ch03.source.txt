Chapter 3 — Start with Heart: Focus on What You Really Want
-----------------------------------------------------------
The first skill is internal. Before a person speaks, they have to get clear
on what they actually want from the conversation. Most people who are "bad
at conflict" are not bad at conflict — they are bad at noticing that, under
pressure, their goals have silently shifted. What they wanted at the start
of the conversation was to solve a problem with their colleague. What they
find themselves wanting two minutes in is to not look stupid, or to punish
the other person for a perceived insult, or to end the conversation as fast
as possible.

The teaching move is a short internal question the reader can ask
themselves in the middle of a heated moment: "What do I really want? For
myself, for the other person, for the relationship?" And then a harder
follow-up: "How would I behave right now if that was what I really wanted?"

The other half of this skill is refusing what the book calls the "Fool's
Choice" (or sucker's choice). A Fool's Choice is a false binary the brain
offers up under stress: "I can be honest OR I can keep this relationship."
"I can tell the truth OR I can avoid hurting their feelings." "I can get
what I need OR I can be kind about it." These binaries feel true, but they
are almost always wrong. The skilled move is to refuse the binary and ask:
"How can I be both honest AND kind? Both direct AND respectful?"

Modern example to generate: A first-time engineering manager is about to
tell a direct report that their code reviews are too harsh. Under pressure
they feel the Fool's Choice: "Either I give them honest feedback and damage
the relationship, or I soften it into nothing and keep the peace." The
skilled move is to refuse both options and look for a path that delivers
the feedback cleanly without collapsing the relationship.
