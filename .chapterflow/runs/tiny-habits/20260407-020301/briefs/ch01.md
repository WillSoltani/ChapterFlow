Core Claim:
Behavior is not a moral mystery. It becomes easier to change once you inspect motivation, ability, and prompt as interacting parts. The chapter's main gift is diagnostic clarity. Readers should leave with a model they can actually use to explain why a behavior did or did not happen.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
It establishes B=MAP as the book's backbone and frames behavior change as design rather than character judgment.

Author Logic Chain:
First the author introduces the three-part behavior model.
Then the author shows how each missing part explains failed behavior.
Then the author concludes that better behavior design starts with diagnosis, not blame.

Required Anchors:
- B=MAP itself as the central anchor
- the design-not-blame framing
- one practical habit diagnosis that changes once the model is applied

Allowed Quotes or Near-Quotes:
- "Behavior happens when motivation, ability, and a prompt converge at the same moment" | behaviormodel_home | status: near_quote_allowed

Frameworks or Terms Introduced:
B=MAP; behavior design.

Specific Applications:
- explain why flossing fails when effort is too high
- explain why reminders fail without fit
- explain why motivation alone is unstable

Common Misreadings:
- treating the model like a motivational slogan
- assuming motivation is always the missing part

Counterarguments or Limits:
The model is only useful if readers actually diagnose the right missing piece instead of using it as jargon.

Previous Chapter Bridge: introduction promises that change can be easy and fun; this chapter explains the mechanism behind that claim.
Next Chapter Bridge: motivation is one part of the model, but not the only or most reliable lever.

Cross-Chapter Tensions:
