Miles starts with one tiny habit and then notices something curious. The behavior does not always stay tiny. Once the step becomes stable, it sometimes grows on its own. Chapter 6 is about that shift. BJ Fogg's point is that tiny habits are not meant to stay tiny forever. They are meant to root first so growth has something real to grow from.

This is why the chapter uses the language of seeds and gardens. The metaphor only works if it stays concrete. A seed is not powerful because it is small. It is powerful because it can grow once it is rooted in the right conditions. A tiny behavior can become a larger behavior, or it can spark related behaviors nearby. One tooth can become more flossing. One minute of movement can become a longer walk. One planning step can spill into a calmer morning.

The chapter therefore respects natural growth more than forced escalation. If the habit is stable, it may stretch upward without so much pressure. If the reader tries to force expansion too soon, the habit can become fragile again. Chapter 6 keeps the order clear. First get the behavior real. Then let growth happen from repetition instead of impatience.

It also matters that not every tiny habit needs to become dramatic to count. Some will stay small and still improve life. The chapter is not promising that every seed becomes a tree. It is offering a calmer way to think about transformation. Tiny can stay useful. Tiny can also become bigger. Both are allowed.

That gives the chapter a steadier view of change than readers often expect. Transformation is not always one huge turn. Sometimes it is the visible shape of many tiny reliable repetitions that finally had room to grow. Chapter 6 protects that slower path by refusing to confuse urgency with development.
