Sofia wants to review one note card after class, and somehow the habit keeps dissolving between the classroom door and the hallway noise. Chapter 4 says that is not mysterious. A behavior can be well matched and easy and still fail if nothing starts it. BJ Fogg calls that missing piece the prompt.

This chapter's practical move is the power of after. Instead of hoping the habit will rise out of memory at the right moment, tie it to something that already happens. After I brush my teeth, I floss one tooth. After I open my laptop, I check the task list. After class ends, I review one card. The point is not clever wording. The point is sequence. An existing action can become a cleaner starting line than a vague intention ever could.

That is why Chapter 4 treats prompts as more than reminders. A prompt is not just a buzzing note in the background. A strong prompt appears at the right moment and fits the flow of the day. If it comes too early, too late, or in the wrong context, it becomes noise. The chapter respects the trailing edge of a real routine because that is where the next tiny behavior can attach without so much strain.

The chapter also keeps a useful warning in view. Not every existing routine is a good anchor. If the anchor is unstable, rushed, or emotionally chaotic, the new behavior can feel forced or brittle. A good anchor is reliable enough to be noticed and ordinary enough to repeat. That is why the chapter cares about fit as much as about prompts themselves.

Once that lands, the method looks cleaner. Motivation is not carrying the habit alone. Ease is not carrying it alone. The prompt is what gets the whole sequence moving at the right time. Chapter 4 makes behavior design more precise by respecting when a behavior begins, not only what the behavior is.
