Lena writes a brave weekly plan every Sunday night. By Wednesday she is already negotiating with it. The problem, she thinks, must be motivation. That guess is understandable, but BJ Fogg's second chapter asks a more exact question: what if the behavior was mismatched from the start? The chapter does not deny that motivation matters. It denies that motivation is stable enough to carry a badly chosen habit every day.

Fogg's practical answer is matching. If you want a habit to survive, choose a behavior that fits both the aspiration and the person who must actually do it. A reader may sincerely want to exercise more, write more, or study more. But if the chosen behavior assumes ideal energy, ideal weather, ideal mood, and ideal time, it is not a good match. It is an ambition wearing the costume of a plan.

This is why the chapter feels gentler than standard self-help and also sharper. A mismatch does not mean the dream is wrong. It means the current version of the behavior is wrong for now. Someone who wants to become consistent with exercise may need a two-minute walk first, not a six-day heroic program. Someone who wants to study more may need to review one card after class, not promise a perfect ninety-minute block every afternoon. Matching protects the aspiration by choosing a behavior the real person can actually sustain.

The chapter also narrows the role of motivation. Motivation can help you begin. It can make a hard push feel possible on a good day. But it is volatile. If your system depends on feeling fired up every time, the system is fragile by design. Matching is stronger because it respects fluctuation. It asks what behavior still fits when the day is crowded, the weather is bad, and the mood is flat.

That is where the chapter's compassion becomes useful rather than soft. Fogg is not telling the reader to lower every standard forever. He is telling the reader to stop choosing habits that repeatedly prove impossible under ordinary conditions and then reading that impossibility as a character flaw. Matching says: choose the version you can honor now, let consistency become real, and then let the behavior grow later if it wants to.

The live tension is obvious. A principle like matching can be misused as a polished excuse to avoid challenge forever. Chapter 2 does not need to deny that danger. It only needs to keep the order straight. Fit comes before stretch. If the behavior does not match your real life, there is nothing stable to build on. The chapter's deeper promise is that honest fit is not settling. It is a stronger foundation for ambition than fantasy can ever be.
