## Core Claim
Once you stop causing as many unforced errors yourself, the next threat is structural: systems, incentives, time horizons, and interventions can still bend your actions into unintended consequences. Better thinking here means planning for failure paths, not just good intentions.

## Source Truth
- Murphy's Law frames the chapter as preparedness, not fatalism.
- Perverse Incentives shows that targets and rewards can corrupt the behavior they were meant to improve.
- Cobra Effect sharpens the warning: a fix can satisfy the stated goal while worsening the actual problem.
- Short-termism gives the chapter its time-horizon critique: near-term optimization can hollow out future strength.
- Market Failure expands the chapter from local bad incentives to system-level outcomes that remain suboptimal without correction.

## Required Anchors
1. Murphy's Law as the chapter's broad defensive rule.
2. Perverse incentives and the Atlanta testing scandal as the clearest concrete picture of metric distortion.
3. Cobra effect as the named mechanism for a fix that worsens the original problem.
4. Short-termism as the chapter's long-term cost lens.

## Live Tension / Boundary
- If you only optimize for intended goals, hidden side effects can dominate the outcome.
- If you become too cynical about side effects, you stop acting at all.
- The chapter should resolve this by favoring second-order thinking and preparedness, not paralysis or fatalism.

## What Must Stay Out
- No unsupported policy history beyond the authorized references.
- No sweeping ideological claims about markets or regulation.
- No later-chapter decision-tree or statistics language.
- No claim that every intervention backfires or every metric corrupts behavior completely.

## Reader Payoff
The reader should leave with a usable rule: before trusting a plan, ask what behavior it rewards, what failure path it opens, what future it steals from, and what you will do if the system responds in the worst plausible way.
