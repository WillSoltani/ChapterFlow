## Core Claim
Statistical thinking gets better when the reader stops trusting vivid examples by default and starts asking what the broader rate, comparison frame, and missing context actually say.

## Source Truth
- Base Rate Fallacy gives the chapter its main skepticism rule: background frequency matters before any dramatic single case.
- The chapter should stay anchored in misread numbers, missing context, and the danger of overreacting to striking evidence.

## Required Anchors
1. Base rate as the chapter's first corrective against vivid-case thinking.
2. Numbers needing context rather than isolated interpretation.
3. Statistical skepticism that helps judgment instead of turning into paralysis.

## Live Tension / Boundary
- If you trust the vivid case alone, you misread the odds.
- If you distrust every number, you become useless instead of careful.
- The chapter should resolve this by teaching context-first judgment rather than cynical dismissal.

## What Must Stay Out
- No unsupported formulas or textbook detours.
- No invented survey, medical, or polling data.
- No imported case studies treated as if they came from the frozen support.

## Reader Payoff
The reader should leave with one usable rule: before reacting to a number or story, ask what usually happens in the broader population and what context is still missing.
