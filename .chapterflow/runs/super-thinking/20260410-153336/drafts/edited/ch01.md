An unforced error is the kind that hurts twice. You lose the point, and you know you handed it away yourself.

That is the opening pressure in *Super Thinking*. The chapter does not promise perfect judgment. It asks for something tougher and more useful: be wrong less. In tennis, an unforced error is not some untouchable shot from the other side of the net. You miss the easy return. You mistime the swing. The damage comes from your own judgment or execution. A lot of thinking failures work the same way. They do not begin with a world too chaotic to read. They begin with avoidable mistakes you created before reality had to get difficult.

"Be right more" sounds exciting, but it is vague. It invites fantasies of sharper instincts and cleaner predictions. "Be wrong less" is stricter. It forces a narrower question: where am I creating mistakes I do not need to create? Which part of this story do I only feel sure about? What would survive if I stripped the conclusion down to what I actually know? Mental models matter here because they are not decorations for sounding smart. They are tools for catching avoidable error before it hardens into cost.

The chapter's clearest discipline is arguing from first principles. That phrase only sounds lofty until you make it practical. Start with the basic pieces you think are true, then build upward. Do not inherit a polished conclusion and mistake it for understanding. Most bad thinking survives on surface reasoning. Someone borrows a convention, a summary, or a smooth explanation and treats it like bedrock. First-principles reasoning interrupts that. What do I know? What am I assuming? Which assumption, if removed, would make this whole conclusion wobble? That move does not guarantee brilliance. It does something more dependable. It cuts away fake certainty.

Still, the chapter is not a sermon about caution. If the only rule were "avoid error," the safest life would be a tiny one. You would wait for airtight proof before trying anything. You would confuse fear with wisdom. That is why the chapter brings in antifragility. Some systems do not merely endure stress. They improve from bounded exposure to it. The point is not that all pressure is good. The point is that being wrong less does not mean removing every shock from your life. It means learning the difference between stress that teaches and stress that merely punishes.

A fragile thing breaks under pressure. A robust thing resists pressure and stays the same. An antifragile thing gets better from the right kind of stress. Once that distinction is visible, the chapter gets sharper. The real enemy is not discomfort. It is preventable damage. You are not trying to eliminate every failed attempt. You are trying to eliminate the kind that comes from overclaiming, overcommitting, or refusing to inspect the foundation under your confidence. If a small test reveals a bad assumption early, that sting is useful. If a cheap experiment exposes a flaw before the flaw becomes expensive, the stress did its job.

That is the logic behind de-risking. Good judgment asks more than whether an idea sounds promising. It asks what happens if the idea is wrong. Can you shrink the downside? Can you test the claim before you let it shape your time, money, or reputation? This is less glamorous than people want thinking to feel. It is also more serious. A lot of avoidable damage comes from treating confidence like evidence and scale like proof.

The minimum viable product belongs here for the same reason. A stripped-down version of an idea is not just a product tactic. It is a judgment tactic. Instead of betting everything on your first confident story, you expose the idea to reality in a form that limits the cost of error. Learn cheaply first. Scale later. If reality pushes back, you want that pushback while the stakes are still small enough to absorb.

Put the chapter's moves together and a clear sequence appears. First, cut your unforced errors by noticing where you keep creating avoidable mistakes for yourself. Next, reason from first principles so the conclusion rests on something firmer than borrowed confidence. Then ask what kind of stress this decision needs. Some exposure will reveal weakness early and make the system stronger. Some exposure will only break something fragile before you understand it. Finally, de-risk the whole process by testing in ways that keep the downside small.

None of this makes you invulnerable. The world will still surprise you. Other people will still act unpredictably. Some failures will still come from outside your control. But a surprising amount of damage arrives earlier, in the quiet moment when you talk yourself into a conclusion you have not earned. That is the ball drifting toward the net. That is the easy shot you miss because you rushed, guessed, or never set your feet.

Being wrong less is not modest thinking. It is disciplined thinking. You give up the vanity of seeming certain in exchange for the much more useful habit of making fewer mistakes you could have prevented. And even after you start doing that, another problem waits: careful choices still live inside systems full of side effects and hidden pressures that can go wrong in ways you never intended.
