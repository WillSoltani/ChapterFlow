Some systems look dead right up until the moment they start moving faster than anyone expected.

That is the pressure Chapter 4 teaches the reader to notice. Not every important change is linear. Not every meaningful result responds to force in the same way. Some systems change because the environment favors one pattern over another, because momentum alters the cost of the next push, or because accumulation crosses a threshold and the whole situation starts behaving differently. Natural selection gives the chapter its opening lens. Traits spread because they fit an environment well enough to persist, not because someone declared them desirable. That moves the chapter away from heroic control and toward adaptation.

This does not mean passivity. It means force without fit is often wasted. If the environment keeps selecting against what you are trying to build, extra effort alone may not rescue the attempt. The chapter's first practical move is therefore diagnostic. Ask what this system rewards, what kind of pattern can survive inside it, and what conditions make progress easier instead of harder.

Flywheel turns that lesson into an experience of effort. A flywheel is hard to start and easier to keep moving once momentum builds. The merry-go-round image is useful because it makes the cost curve visible. At first, every push feels heavy. Later, the same push does more because the system is already in motion. The chapter uses that to correct a common misreading. People often treat early resistance as proof that the whole project is wrong, then treat later acceleration as mysterious when it is really the delayed payoff of sustained effort inside a system that has finally started helping.

That correction matters because early friction is ambiguous. Sometimes it means the environment is wrong for the thing you are trying to do. Sometimes it means you are still paying the startup cost of momentum. The chapter's lesson is not to worship persistence blindly. It is to read the system carefully enough to tell the difference.

Then the chapter shifts from momentum to threshold. A tipping point is the moment when accumulated change reaches a level that makes the system behave differently. What looked slow can become fast. What looked marginal can become obvious. The chapter treats this as a threshold story rather than as magic. The dramatic change may appear sudden, but it usually rests on prior buildup that was easy to underestimate while it was happening.

Network effects show the same structure in a more social form. A network becomes more valuable as more participants join it. The system does not merely gain another unit. It becomes more attractive to the next participant because the existing participation changes the payoff. That is what makes some patterns self-reinforcing. Growth improves the conditions for more growth and can push the system toward critical mass.

Read together, the chapter's models form a coherent rule. Natural selection says fit matters. Flywheel says effort changes shape once momentum exists. Tipping point says thresholds can change the visible behavior of the whole system. Network effects say participation can make future participation more rewarding. The chapter is not asking the reader to become mystical about emergence. It is asking them to stop assuming that the current pace tells the whole truth about the system.

That creates a productive kind of patience. Not passive waiting, and not brute-force impatience either. Build where the environment can support the pattern. Keep pushing long enough to see whether momentum is changing the cost. Watch for the threshold where the system stops behaving as though nothing is happening. Ask whether each added participant or contribution improves the conditions for the next one.

The boundary matters here too. Respecting natural dynamics can turn into an excuse for hiding. People can talk about timing, fit, and alignment when the real problem is that they do not want to pay the early cost of motion. The chapter pushes back against that. Natural selection still implies persistence. Flywheels still need early force. Tipping points still depend on prior accumulation. Networks still need participation. The point is not to sit still and hope the system eventually becomes generous. The point is to stop confusing frantic force with intelligent participation in a system that has its own structure.

By the end, effort looks different. The smartest move is sometimes less about pushing harder and more about reading fit, momentum, thresholds, and reinforcement clearly. That practical rule matters before any later analytical system chapter arrives: do not let today's pace fool you into missing the deeper structure that decides how change really happens.
