Some decisions feel impossible mainly because too many different futures are being carried in the mind all at once.

That is the opening pressure of Chapter 6 in *Super Thinking*. The chapter does not promise certainty. It promises structure. Decision tree gives the chapter its central move: break a complicated choice into visible branches, possible outcomes, and consequences so the reader is no longer reacting to one blurred emotional mass. The problem with many hard choices is not only uncertainty. It is that uncertainty stays bundled.

When everything stays bundled, the mind reaches for shortcuts. A person says a choice feels risky, overwhelming, or complicated without being able to show which branch creates the fear or which outcome carries the real cost. The chapter pushes back by decomposing the decision. Instead of asking, "How do I feel about the whole thing?" it asks, "What are the main paths from here, and what follows from each one?"

That move is useful because complexity often becomes more manageable once it is separated. One option leads here. Another option leads there. One branch creates a set of downstream consequences that differs from the next branch. The decision has not become easy, but it has become visible enough to inspect. The chapter uses that visibility to replace vague dread with clearer comparison.

A decision tree also changes what counts as good judgment. Good judgment is not pretending to know the future perfectly. It is laying out the meaningful branches clearly enough that uncertainty can be reasoned through instead of merely felt. The chapter therefore turns decomposition into a kind of discipline. If a choice is hard, split it. If a path is still too broad, split it again until the consequences can actually be discussed.

That does not mean endless diagramming for its own sake. The point is not bureaucratic neatness. The point is to stop letting complexity hide inside a single label. "Take the job" is not one outcome. "Stay where you are" is not one outcome. "Change directions" is not one outcome. Each path contains later branches, tradeoffs, and consequences that deserve to be seen before the decision gets reduced to mood.

This is where the chapter earns its title. Decisions, decisions. The repetition in the title hints at the real problem: a decision is rarely just one moment. It is often a sequence of contingent futures. Decision tree thinking gives the reader a way to hold that sequence without collapsing into confusion. It does not eliminate uncertainty, but it gives uncertainty shape.

That boundary matters because structure can become fake precision if it starts pretending to know more than it does. The chapter should not be read as a promise that every difficult choice can be solved by a perfect diagram. Some branches stay uncertain. Some consequences remain hard to compare. The value of the tree is not that it abolishes ambiguity. The value is that it makes ambiguity easier to locate.

The practical effect is immediate. A reader facing a hard choice can stop asking for one global feeling to carry the whole burden. They can name the main options, trace the likely branches, and compare consequences path by path. That often reveals that two fears were being mixed together, or that one supposedly obvious option hides several different futures the reader had not separated before.

The chapter therefore turns decision-making into a process of visible reasoning. Instead of worshipping intuition or outsourcing thought to vague confidence, it asks the reader to expose the structure of the choice. What branches matter? What consequences matter? Which path stays attractive once it is fully unpacked? Those questions do not make life simple, but they make it less foggy.

The bridge to the next chapter is important. Once a decision has been decomposed, another problem appears: what happens when other people are involved, with their own incentives, pressures, and strategic moves? Chapter 6 prepares for that turn. It teaches the reader to separate branching outcomes clearly before Chapter 7 adds conflict, persuasion, and social strategy to the picture.
