An unforced error is the kind that hurts twice. You lose the point, and you know you handed it away yourself.

That is why the opening move in *Super Thinking* is not to promise perfect judgment. It starts somewhere humbler and more useful: be wrong less. In tennis, an unforced error happens when the other player does not beat you with some impossible shot. You miss the easy ball. You mistime the return. The damage comes from your own judgment or execution. The same pattern shows up in thinking. A lot of bad decisions are not the result of a world that was too chaotic to read. They come from avoidable mistakes you created before reality ever had to get difficult.

That shift matters because "be right more" sounds glamorous but vague. It invites people to imagine smarter predictions, sharper instincts, bigger wins. "Be wrong less" is harsher and cleaner. It asks a different question: where am I making mistakes that I do not need to make? Which parts of this problem am I pretending to understand? What would disappear if I stripped the story down to what I actually know? Mental models help because they are not ornaments for sounding smart. They are tools for catching your own avoidable errors before those errors turn into commitments, costs, and consequences.

One of the chapter's clearest moves is arguing from first principles. The phrase can sound grand until you cut it down to size. It means starting from the basic pieces you think are true and building upward from there instead of borrowing a polished conclusion and calling it understanding. Most thinking errors survive because people reason from surfaces. They inherit a story, a convention, or a familiar explanation and treat it like bedrock. First-principles reasoning forces a reset. What do I know? What am I assuming? Which part of this conclusion is actually doing the work? That bottom-up move does not guarantee brilliance. It does something more reliable. It removes a class of fake certainty.

This is where the chapter becomes more demanding than a simple warning against mistakes. If the only rule were "avoid error," the safest life would be a small one. You would never test an idea until it felt airtight. You would confuse caution with wisdom. The chapter pushes against that by bringing in antifragility. Some things are not merely able to survive stress. They get better from it. That distinction is important because being wrong less does not mean building a life with no friction, no volatility, and no exposure. It means learning to tell the difference between stress that teaches and stress that merely punishes.

A fragile system breaks when pressure arrives. A robust system resists the pressure and stays the same. An antifragile system takes a bounded shock and improves from it. Once that distinction is on the table, the chapter's logic sharpens. The real target is not discomfort. It is preventable damage. You want fewer unforced errors, not a padded existence. If a small test reveals a bad assumption early, that sting is useful. If a cheap experiment exposes a flaw before the flaw becomes expensive, the stress did its job. The mistake you are trying to avoid is not every failed attempt. It is the kind that comes from overclaiming, overcommitting, or refusing to inspect the foundation under your confidence.

That is where de-risking enters. Good judgment does not only ask whether an idea sounds promising. It asks what happens if the idea is wrong. Can you shrink the downside? Can you test the claim before you build your schedule, budget, or reputation around it? This is less heroic than most people want thinking to feel. It is also more effective. A lot of apparently high-level decision making is just someone protecting their ego from the embarrassment of a small test. They would rather leap and narrate courage than run a modest check that might expose a weak assumption.

The minimum viable product belongs in this chapter for the same reason. A cheap, limited version of an idea is not just a product tactic. It is a way of thinking. Instead of betting everything on the first confident story, you expose the idea to reality in a form that limits the cost of error. That does not mean moving thoughtlessly or treating every problem like a startup. It means respecting the order of operations. Learn cheaply first. Scale later. If reality pushes back, you want that pushback while the stakes are still small enough to absorb.

Put those moves together and the chapter stops sounding like generic caution. It becomes a practical sequence. First, cut your unforced errors by noticing where you are creating avoidable mistakes for yourself. Next, reason from first principles so your conclusion has something sturdier beneath it than borrowed confidence. Then ask what kind of stress this decision needs. Some pressure will reveal weakness early and make the system stronger. Some pressure will just break something fragile before you understand it. Finally, de-risk the whole process by testing in ways that keep the downside small.

The appeal of all this is not that it makes you invulnerable. It does not. The world will still surprise you. Other people will still behave unpredictably. Some failures will still arrive from outside your control. But a surprising amount of damage comes earlier, in the quiet moment when you talk yourself into a conclusion you have not earned. That is the ball drifting toward the net. That is the easy shot you miss because you rushed, guessed, or never set your feet.

Being wrong less is not modest thinking. It is disciplined thinking. It asks you to give up the vanity of seeming certain in exchange for the much more useful habit of making fewer mistakes you could have prevented. And once you start doing that, a new problem appears. Even careful decisions still live inside systems full of side effects, hidden pressures, and outcomes that can go wrong in ways you did not intend.
