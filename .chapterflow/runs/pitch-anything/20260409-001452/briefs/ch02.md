Core Claim:
Chapter 2 argues that audiences filter pitches through a fast, primitive attention system before deeper analysis ever gets a turn. Klaff uses the practical croc-brain model to explain why dense logic, abstraction, and long setup often die on contact. The chapter's job is to make the reader stop blaming only the room's patience and start respecting the first filter that decides whether a pitch is even worth hearing.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
This is the audience-processing chapter. It is not yet the step-by-step method chapter, but it gives the pressure model that justifies why the later method exists. It must stay practical and bounded rather than inflating into fake neuroscience.

Author Logic Chain:
First the author argues that the audience does not receive a pitch through calm analysis alone.
Then the author introduces a practical brain-filter model oriented around novelty, simplicity, threat, and obvious relevance.
Then the author shows why long, dense, rational-first pitches get filtered out early.
Then the author implies that an effective pitch must be built for this first filter before it can reach deeper reasoning.

Required Anchors:
- the practical croc-brain model as a filter for threat, novelty, and simplicity
- the failure of long, detail-heavy, purely rational pitches to survive the first mental screen
- the idea that hot cognition and clear contrast help a pitch get noticed before analysis begins

Frameworks or Terms Introduced:
- croc brain: the practical first-filter model for immediate pitch reception
- first filter: the mind's fast gate for novelty, threat, and relevance
- hot cognition: emotionally salient processing that grabs attention earlier than abstract analysis

Specific Applications:
- opening an investor pitch with too much context and losing attention
- presenting a class proposal with analysis before the judges know why they should care
- making a negotiation ask that starts with justification instead of a clear, high-contrast point

Supported Concrete Details:
- the chapter treats audience reception as fast and selective rather than endlessly patient
- dense explanation is framed as a liability when it arrives before relevance is clear
- the later method is justified as a way to survive the audience's first filter

Common Misreadings:
- treating the brain model as precise lab neuroscience
- assuming logic no longer matters at all
- reading the chapter as a defense of shallow manipulation

Counterarguments or Limits:
The brain model is useful because it simplifies reality into a pitch rule. It becomes weak when readers treat it as a total theory of human thought or as an excuse to avoid substance altogether. The prose should keep that boundary alive.
