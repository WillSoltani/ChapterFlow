Core Claim:
Chapter 4 argues that whoever controls the frame controls the meaning of the interaction. Klaff positions frame control as the first live move in STRONG because a pitch does not land inside neutral interpretation. It lands inside a social contest over status, assumptions, pace, and who gets to define what kind of conversation is happening.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
This is the first operational chapter in the solution section. It must make frame control concrete enough to feel useful while staying clear of empty dominance theater. The chapter should show that frame is not a side issue; it is the container that changes how the same facts are received.

Author Logic Chain:
First the author treats the room as a contest of interpretation rather than as a neutral fact exchange.
Then the author explains that the dominant frame shapes status and momentum.
Then the author shows how reactive answering, overexplaining, or approval-seeking often give the frame away.
Then the author implies that later STRONG moves depend on a frame strong enough to hold them.

Required Anchors:
- frame control as the first move in the STRONG method
- the idea that a pitch unfolds inside competing interpretations rather than inside a neutral room
- the warning that reacting too fast or too needily often means surrendering frame

Frameworks or Terms Introduced:
- frame: the live interpretive structure that tells the room what kind of interaction this is
- frame control: holding that structure rather than reacting inside someone else's assumptions
- status: the social reading that changes how confidence, pressure, and value are perceived

Specific Applications:
- holding ground when an investor tries to turn the meeting into pure interrogation before the opportunity is clear
- managing a case-competition Q&A without letting judges define the team as defensive
- making a compensation ask without slipping into permission-seeking or apology

Supported Concrete Details:
- frame control is the opening move of the book's practical sequence
- frame loss often shows up through reactive pacing, overanswering, or neediness
- later moves depend on the frame because meaning and momentum are not neutral

Common Misreadings:
- treating frame control as being rude or dominant for its own sake
- assuming frame means ignoring all questions or objections
- reducing the chapter to posture tricks with no link to meaning or opportunity

Counterarguments or Limits:
Holding frame can create clarity, but if pushed into ego performance it can make the presenter ignore real concerns or confuse aggression with control. The prose should keep that limit visible so the chapter stays practical rather than theatrical.
