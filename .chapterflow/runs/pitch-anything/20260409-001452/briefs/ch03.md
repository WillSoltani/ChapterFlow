Core Claim:
Chapter 3 argues that pitching improves when the reader stops treating success as a personality trait and starts using a controllable sequence. Klaff names that sequence the STRONG method and positions it as the operating system that follows naturally from the audience-filter problem in Chapter 2. The chapter's job is to make the method feel like a strategic order, not like a motivational acronym.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
This is the method-naming bridge chapter. It is still introductory, but it must do more than list the six letters. It has to explain why a sequence is necessary, why the moves belong together, and why later chapters will unpack them one by one.

Author Logic Chain:
First the author turns the earlier diagnosis into a practical demand for method.
Then the author names the STRONG sequence as the book's answer.
Then the author frames the six moves as an order with strategic purpose, not as disconnected tips.
Then the author implies that later chapters will deepen each move, beginning with frame control.

Supported Concrete Details:

Common Misreadings:
- treating STRONG as a flat checklist with no strategic order
- assuming the acronym itself is enough without judgment, timing, or frame control
- reading the sequence as a substitute for substance rather than as a way to deliver substance well

Counterarguments or Limits:
A named method creates discipline, but it can also tempt readers to confuse memorized order with room judgment. The prose should preserve that tension: sequence matters because it structures pressure, not because acronyms magically win meetings.
