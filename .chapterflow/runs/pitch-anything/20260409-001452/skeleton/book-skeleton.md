# Book Skeleton

## Metadata
- Book: Pitch Anything
- Author: Oren Klaff
- Locked edition: 2011 McGraw-Hill Professional English trade family
- Numbered chapter range: 1-11
- Wave size: 2 by default
- Premium / solo routing candidates: ch02, ch04, ch10, ch11

## Rotation Plan For Examples
- Maintain the canonical six-format rotation once per chapter: decision_point, postmortem, dialogue, predict_reveal, dilemma, before_after.
- Maintain 2 work / 2 school / 2 personal examples in every chapter package.
- Rotate endings across broader_principle, self_directed_question, surprising_implication, cross_domain, common_trap, perspective_reframe.

## School-Setting Plan
- Vary school contexts across case competitions, student-founder pitches, classroom presentations, startup incubators, scholarship interviews, debate-team prep, and group-project proposal meetings.
- Keep school examples tied to pitch dynamics, frame control, attention, and status rather than generic study advice.

## Vocabulary Watchlist
- Keep frame, status, hookpoint, prize, intrigue, neediness, novelty, decision, and STRONG precise and chapter-bound.
- Avoid generic persuasion buzzwords, unsupported neuroscience language, and empty closing-talk slogans.

## Motif / Callback Opportunities
- Early brain-attention limits from ch02 should echo whenever later chapters talk about novelty, brevity, and hookpoints.
- Frame control from ch04 should stay visible in story, intrigue, prizing, and decision chapters rather than appearing once and disappearing.
- Status and neediness should recur as hidden forces under the method, not as detached side ideas.
- The ideal pitch in ch10 should feel like a convergence of earlier moves, and ch11 should land as identity shift rather than a flat victory lap.

## Chapter Plan
### ch01 Why This Book Can Help You
- Section: Section 1: The Problem
- Intent: Establish why traditional, logic-heavy pitching loses attention and why a more status-aware, frame-aware method matters.
- Source richness: high
- Concept density: medium
- Moral complexity: medium
- Thin-chapter risk notes: do not let the opening become generic sales hype; keep the mismatch between effort and results visible.
- Wave routing: baseline

### ch02 This Is Your Brain, This Is Your Brain During a Pitch
- Section: Section 1: The Problem
- Intent: Explain the attention and threat model behind why audiences reject complex, low-novelty, or weak-status pitches.
- Source richness: high
- Concept density: high
- Moral complexity: medium
- Thin-chapter risk notes: avoid unsupported neuroscience detail; keep the brain model practical and bounded.
- Wave routing: baseline
