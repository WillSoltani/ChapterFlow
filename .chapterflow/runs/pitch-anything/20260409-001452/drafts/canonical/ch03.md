By the end of the second chapter, the reader knows what is broken. Audiences filter early, patience is scarce, and dense rational openings often die before the real substance arrives. That diagnosis is useful, but it creates a new problem. Knowing why pitches fail is not the same thing as knowing what to do in the room tomorrow morning. Awareness without sequence can leave a presenter with sharper anxiety and no operating system.

Klaff answers that gap by naming a method. The book's official overview freezes the sequence as STRONG: set and own the frame, tell the story, reveal intrigue, offer the prize, nail the hookpoint, and get a decision. The importance of that move is larger than the acronym itself. Klaff is saying that pitch success should be treated as something that can be structured and repeated. The method is the bridge between the early chapters' diagnosis and the practical chapters that follow.

The order matters. Set and own the frame comes first because a pitch never enters a neutral room. It enters a room that is already deciding who has status, who is reacting, and what kind of interaction this is. Tell the story comes next because story can move meaning quickly without drowning the listener in explanation. Reveal intrigue keeps the room leaning forward instead of relaxing into predictability. Offer the prize changes the social balance so the opportunity does not feel cheap or needy. Nail the hookpoint gives the pitch a moment where novelty, consequence, and desire peak together. Get a decision turns all that controlled attention into commitment instead of endless pleasant talk.

What makes the sequence useful is that each move solves a different pressure problem. Frame addresses interpretation. Story addresses compression. Intrigue addresses attention. Prize addresses scarcity and status. Hookpoint addresses impact. Decision addresses closure. A presenter who treats all pitching problems as a single need for more confidence or more detail will miss that structure. Klaff's method says the room has phases, and each phase needs a different kind of control.

That is why the chapter cannot be read as a simple checklist. The six labels only help if the reader feels the logic underneath them. A pitch fails when the moves happen in the wrong order, get skipped, or collapse into one another. If someone tells a story before holding the frame, the story lands inside someone else's terms. If someone asks for a decision before creating intrigue or value, the close feels premature. If someone tries to use the prize move without real control, it can look like empty posturing. The method is therefore not six isolated tricks. It is one sequence for moving a room from first contact to decision.

The promise here is practical and demanding at the same time. Practical, because a named sequence is easier to rehearse, diagnose, and improve than vague advice about being persuasive. Demanding, because readers can now no longer hide behind the excuse that winning rooms is mysterious. Once the method exists, a weak pitch can be analyzed move by move. Where was the frame lost? Where did the intrigue die? Was there ever a real hookpoint? Did the meeting drift because the pitch never asked for a decision cleanly enough?

Still, a method can create its own illusion. Readers may think that memorizing six labels is the same thing as mastering six moves. It is not. Sequence creates discipline, but discipline is not magic. The method still depends on judgment, timing, and a real offer beneath it. Klaff's point is not that STRONG turns bad opportunities into good ones. The point is that it gives strong opportunities a repeatable path through the pressures that usually weaken them.

That makes the next step obvious. If STRONG is the operating system, then the first move cannot stay abstract for long. The pitch begins by setting and owning the frame because every later move depends on the kind of room the presenter allows to exist. Before story, intrigue, prize, or decision can land, the interaction itself has to be defined.
