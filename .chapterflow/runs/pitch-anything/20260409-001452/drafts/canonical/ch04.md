The first move in the method is not story, evidence, or clever phrasing. It is frame. A founder walks into a meeting ready to describe an opportunity, but the investor starts firing small skeptical questions before the opportunity has even taken shape. A job candidate begins a compensation conversation and starts answering an implied accusation instead of making a case. In both situations, the facts may still be available, but the room is already being defined in a way that weakens them. Klaff's claim is that the pitch is never landing inside a neutral container.

That is what frame control tries to solve. A frame is the live structure that tells everyone what kind of interaction this is, who has status, what counts as relevant, and how meaning is being organized. If the presenter loses that structure early, the rest of the pitch starts arriving under someone else's assumptions. The audience is no longer evaluating an opportunity on the pitcher's terms. It is evaluating a reactive performance, a defense, or a plea for approval.

This is why frame belongs first in STRONG. Story matters, intrigue matters, and the hookpoint matters, but all of those moves change their meaning depending on the frame around them. The same story can sound compelling inside a strong frame and evasive inside a weak one. The same ask can sound selective inside a strong frame and needy inside a weak one. Klaff is pushing the reader to notice that content quality and frame quality are not the same thing. One is what you say. The other is the condition under which what you say gets heard.

Weak frame control often looks deceptively reasonable. The presenter answers too quickly because he wants to seem cooperative. He adds extra explanation because he wants to look prepared. He adopts the other side's urgency, skepticism, or dismissive tone because he is afraid of losing the room. Each move feels polite in isolation. Together they can surrender the interaction. The presenter stops shaping the meeting and starts chasing it. Once that happens, even good answers can lower status because the room now experiences the presenter as reactive rather than as someone guiding a real opportunity.

The point is not to become rude, theatrical, or obsessed with dominance. That would turn frame into a costume. Klaff's practical edge is narrower. Control the interaction enough that the opportunity does not get buried under someone else's premature interpretation. Hold your footing long enough for the room to understand what kind of thing it is being asked to judge. A calm re-anchor can do more than a fast answer if the fast answer locks you deeper inside a bad frame.

That is also why neediness matters here. Neediness is not only emotional tone. It is structural. The moment the presenter behaves as though acceptance must be chased at any cost, the frame starts shifting against him. Questions become tests to survive instead of openings to direct. Objections become commands instead of material to work with. The room begins to feel like it belongs to the other side. Frame control is the discipline that prevents that slide before it becomes invisible.

Held well, the frame gives the rest of the method somewhere to stand. Story can then carry meaning instead of sounding defensive. Intrigue can create forward pull instead of confusion. Prize can create selective value instead of fake scarcity. Decision can feel earned instead of begged for. That is why this move comes first. Before the pitch can advance, the interaction itself has to stop working against the presenter.
