Naomi has the room with her for the first two minutes. The frame is steady, the story is clear, and the audience understands the problem. Then she answers every open question before anyone has to ask it. She explains the surprising customer behavior, the hidden cost, the downstream implication, and the solution logic all at once. The pitch becomes complete, but it also becomes flat. The room stops leaning in because there is nothing left to discover. That is the problem Chapter 6 takes on. Once the audience can follow the story, the presenter still has to keep attention alive. Klaff's answer is intrigue.

Intrigue matters because attention does not stay active on clarity alone. People keep scanning when they sense that something important is still unfolding. Novelty, surprise, and controlled unresolved significance keep the room mentally engaged without forcing it into confusion. This is not empty suspense. It is disciplined timing. The presenter reveals enough for the audience to care and understand, but not so much, so early, that all tension disappears before the stakes have had time to register.

That is why intrigue follows story instead of replacing it. Story gives the audience a path. Intrigue keeps the path charged. Once the room understands what changed and why it matters, the presenter can shape curiosity around what comes next, what implication is bigger than it first appeared, or what fact will land hardest once the setup is in place. Novelty here is useful because it refreshes attention. The audience feels that there is still meaningful difference to discover, so it keeps leaning forward instead of settling into passive comprehension.

Weak presenters mishandle this move in two opposite ways. Some explain everything too early because they are afraid of seeming vague. They remove all tension before the audience has felt why the information matters. Others go the other direction and treat intrigue like theater. They hide simple facts behind drama, vagueness, or flashy mystery. Both errors waste trust. One kills attention by flattening the sequence; the other kills trust by making curiosity feel manipulated. The point is not to confuse the room. The point is to keep the room mentally active while meaning is still gathering force.

That boundary matters because intrigue can be abused more easily than story. A presenter can start believing that holding back information is persuasive by itself. It is not. Intrigue is only useful when the delayed piece actually sharpens understanding once it arrives. If the reveal adds no consequence, no difference, and no deeper logic, then the suspense was just a tax on the audience. Real intrigue pays off. It makes the offer feel larger, sharper, or more urgent when the next piece lands.

That is why Chapter 6 sits where it does. Frame gives footing. Story gives sequence. Intrigue keeps the sequence alive with curiosity and novelty. Once the room is leaning forward, the next challenge is not simply to keep attention. It is to convert that attention into value and scarcity. That is where prize comes in.
