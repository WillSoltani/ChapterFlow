Lena feels the meeting turn soft the moment she says she is open to any investor, any structure, and any timeline that keeps the conversation alive. She thinks flexibility makes her easier to like. Instead, it makes the company feel ordinary. The room stops experiencing the deal as something it may need to win and starts experiencing it as something already available on demand. That is the problem Chapter 7 addresses. Once attention is alive, the presenter has to make value feel scarce instead of needy. Klaff's answer is prize.

Prize matters because neediness is corrosive. The faster the presenter signals that any outcome will do, the more the audience assumes the opportunity must not be especially valuable. Status drops first, but value often drops with it. Scarcity does not have to be fake to be powerful. It can come from real standards, real selectivity, real fit, and a clear sense that the presenter is not trying to force the offer into any room that happens to listen. That change in posture alters how the same opportunity is judged.

That is why prize follows intrigue. Intrigue keeps the room leaning in, but prize tells the room what that leaning should feel like. The offer is not just interesting. It is worth winning. A strong presenter does not behave as though the audience is automatically doing them a favor by showing up. The presenter behaves as though both sides are evaluating fit, and that the opportunity has value that cannot be claimed through pressure alone. This is not arrogance. It is disciplined value positioning.

Weak use of this move usually fails in one of two directions. Some presenters keep chasing approval. They overqualify, overexplain terms, and rush to prove that they will say yes to anything. Others hear the idea of prize and turn it into fake aloofness. They posture, withhold basic cooperation, or act as though distance itself creates value. Both errors break trust. One cheapens the offer through neediness. The other cheapens it through theater. Prize works only when selectivity is connected to something real: standards, fit, leverage, or an offer that genuinely should not be treated as common.

That limit matters because false scarcity is easy to perform and easy to detect. The room can feel when the presenter is trying to simulate power instead of holding real value. Strategic distance helps only when it protects the integrity of the opportunity. If the presenter is using distance to hide weak fit, weak substance, or fear of scrutiny, the move turns hollow. But when the presenter is grounded, prize changes the emotional geometry of the pitch. The audience starts feeling that access is earned, not assumed.

That is why this chapter sits before hookpoint. Once the opportunity feels scarce, the pitch needs one concentrated moment where that value hits hardest. Prize prepares the room to feel that peak with more force because the offer no longer sounds common or overeager. It sounds worth winning. That is what hookpoint has to crystallize next.
