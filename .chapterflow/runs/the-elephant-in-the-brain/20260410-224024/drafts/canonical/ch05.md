It is unsettling to think that sincerity might not be the opposite of deception. That is where Chapter 5 begins. After Chapter 4 showed how people cheat under fig leaves, the next move is more intimate. What if the mind sometimes hides truth from itself so that deception becomes easier to carry in public?

The chapter starts by comparing older defensive theories of self-deception with newer evolutionary ones. The older picture says people deceive themselves to avoid pain, shame, anxiety, or inner conflict. That idea still captures something real. Some truths are hard to face. But the chapter argues that this cannot be the whole story if self-deception also helps people navigate social competition.

The newer evolutionary account adds that hidden truths may be concealed from the self in order to mislead other people more effectively. A person who does not fully see the selfish motive behind a claim can often present that claim with more conviction. The signal looks cleaner because the speaker is not constantly managing visible doubt. Self-deception is therefore not only a private defense. It may also be a social advantage.

That does not mean a little inner liar sits at the center of the mind consciously fooling the rest. The chapter avoids that cartoon. Its mechanism depends on the mind being less unified than people often imagine. If mental processes are partly modular, then some parts may carry information that other parts do not fully access. Inconsistent inner processes are no longer a paradox that needs a single mastermind.

This is where self-discretion matters. One part of the mind can remain partly ignorant of what another part is doing or wanting. The result is not total chaos. It is a structured kind of opacity. People can feel sincere while still lacking clean access to the motives shaping their behavior. The hidden motive is not gone. It is simply not equally available across the whole mind.

That helps explain why self-deception can be socially useful. Doubt, guilt, ambition, vanity, and selfish calculation become harder for other people to detect when they are also harder for the actor to detect clearly. The face, voice, and explanation can therefore look less like deliberate manipulation. What outsiders read as honesty may sometimes be produced by inner concealment rather than by full transparency.

The chapter's hard edge is that self-knowledge now becomes less trustworthy without becoming pointless. People are not always consciously fabricating themselves, but they are not always in direct contact with the causes of what they are doing either. The self is partly interpreter and partly participant in a system that can keep truths unevenly distributed inside the mind.

That is why the next chapter turns to counterfeit reasons. If motives can be hidden from the self, then explanation itself becomes unstable. The mind may not only fail to see the real cause. It may also generate a cleaner story after the fact, one that sounds sincere because the speaker lacks full access to what actually drove the action.
