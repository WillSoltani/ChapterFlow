Religion is often described as if it were mainly a matter of private belief. The chapter begins by arguing that this picture is incomplete. Religious life also helps groups bond, display loyalty, and enforce norms in ways that become visible through shared practice.

Its first major frame is bonding. Religion can pull people together through common rituals, repeated gatherings, and synchronized behavior. The point is not only that members think similar thoughts, but that they move, listen, and participate in ways that make belonging feel real and mutual.

This is why loyalty display matters. Sacrifice, public participation, and visible commitment can show that someone is serious about the group. Costly actions are especially legible because they are harder to fake. In that sense, religion can make allegiance publicly readable rather than leaving it as a private claim.

The chapter also highlights norm enforcement. Badges, sermons, and other visible markers can remind members what the group values and who stands inside it. Supernatural enforcement extends that pressure by making norms feel backed by more than immediate human observation.

That does not require sincere belief to disappear. The chapter does not need religion to become pure manipulation. Its stronger claim is that belief, bonding, loyalty signaling, and norm enforcement can all operate together inside the same religious life.

The tension becomes clearest when sacrifice and synchrony do double duty. A ritual can genuinely deepen commitment and also publicly display who is willing to align, obey, and incur costs for the group. The act is then doing more than one job at once.

That keeps the chapter from collapsing into either pure faith or pure control. Religion can bind people together while also signaling loyalty and reinforcing norms. Once that layered structure is visible, the move to politics becomes easier to understand: strong emotions, low information, and sacrifice will again fit a group-loyalty account.
