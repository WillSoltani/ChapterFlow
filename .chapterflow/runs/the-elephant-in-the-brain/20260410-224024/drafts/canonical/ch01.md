The first clue is a monkey with its hands in another monkey's fur. From a distance the scene looks practical, even tender. One animal is cleaning another. Dirt comes off. Parasites come off. The surface story almost writes itself. But the behavior keeps showing up in patterns that make hygiene look too small. Grooming follows rank, alliances, and social tension. It clusters around the relationships that matter. The act still cleans fur, but its larger job is political.

That shift matters because it changes the kind of answer we think we are allowed to give. A clean public function is easy to notice and easy to say out loud. A social payoff is harder to admit, especially when the behavior still produces some obvious good. If grooming helps with hygiene, then calling it political can sound cynical or needlessly rude. But the more interesting explanation is not that the hygienic story is false. It is that the hygienic story is incomplete. Grooming can be useful and strategic at the same time. It can remove parasites while also buying tolerance, signaling loyalty, and tending the alliances that shape life inside the group.

The Arabian babbler pushes the same lesson into brighter light. These birds are famous for behavior that looks generous: standing guard, feeding others, taking risks that seem to help the group. At first glance, the obvious reading is sacrifice. Here, finally, is a clean case of animals doing something for the common good. But the chapter's point is that public-spirited behavior can itself become a contest. If helping is visible, then helping can rank people. A bird can look brave, useful, and worthy of regard by being the one that gives more.

Now the behavior stops looking like a puzzle and starts looking like a bid. What appears to be altruism can double as prestige jockeying. The group may still benefit. The warning call may still help. The food may still get shared. Yet the actor is not floating above social incentives. The actor is inside them, using a prosocial-looking move to rise in standing. That is what makes the example so useful. It blocks the lazy choice between sainthood and fraud. A behavior can do real work for others and still be powered partly by competitive display.

That mixed-motive structure is the real opening move of the book. The authors do not begin with human institutions because people get defensive fast when the target is school, charity, medicine, or politics. Starting with animals changes the emotional temperature. Few readers feel personally accused by monkey grooming or babbler showmanship. So the chapter can train a habit of explanation before the moral alarms go off. It asks the reader to notice a repeated pattern: the official function of a behavior may be only the visible half of what the behavior is doing.

That habit is useful, but it has a limit, and the chapter is better when that limit stays in view. Once you learn to look for hidden motives, you can start seeing them everywhere. You can become the sort of person who treats every kind act as theater and every useful custom as camouflage. The animal cases do not justify that move. They justify a narrower one. They show that social payoff belongs in the explanation more often than we admit. They do not prove that the social payoff is always the whole explanation, or that useful outcomes stop mattering once status enters the scene.

So the deeper question is not whether a behavior is pure. That is usually the wrong test. The better question is what kind of work the behavior is doing all at once. Grooming can clean and curry favor. Babbler helping can protect the group and advertise the helper. Once those pairings feel natural instead of scandalous, the rest of the book can proceed. Human life is full of polished reasons and respectable surfaces. The chapter's animal cases give you a way to look past the first explanation without pretending that the second one cancels everything good the first one was doing.
