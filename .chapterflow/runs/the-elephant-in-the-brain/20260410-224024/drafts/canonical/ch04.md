Everyone in the room knows something is off, yet nobody quite names it. That is the frustration Chapter 4 opens with. Once norms exist, they do not erase the incentive to break them. They change the game. People still want the gains of selfish behavior, but they now want those gains without paying the full social cost of open violation.

The chapter's first move is to reject the simple picture of cheating as secret rule-breaking done entirely in the dark. Some cheating is hidden, but the more interesting kind happens in broad daylight. Other people can often see enough to suspect what is going on. The problem is that suspicion alone is weaker than enforceable proof. If a violation is visible but wrapped in just enough ambiguity, the group may hesitate to act.

That is where the fig leaf enters. A thin fig leaf is not a strong defense. It is a small piece of cover that makes confrontation harder than observation. The cover does not need to fool everyone completely. It only needs to make the accuser pay a higher cost. If calling out the violation means arguing over intent, tone, deniability, or whether the evidence is really decisive, many observers back off.

Weak norms make that hesitation worse. Some rules only work when everyone not only sees the violation, but also knows that everyone else sees it and is ready to act on that shared knowledge. Without that common knowledge, each observer waits. Nobody wants to be the first to convert a vague suspicion into a public accusation. The result is a strange public secret: many people notice the cheating, but the cheating persists because notice has not turned into coordinated enforcement.

Discreet communication helps keep things in that zone. So do ready-made excuses. A cheater does not need an airtight story. A thin explanation, a private signal, or an ambiguous presentation may be enough to prevent the group from aligning cleanly. The excuse changes the burden. Now anyone who wants enforcement has to push past awkwardness, risk seeming unreasonable, and prove more than the cheater had to prove in the first place.

This means the chapter is not really about monsters and saints. It is about structure. Norms create pressure, and pressure creates incentives to cheat around them. People may bend rules openly because the social cost of enforcement is often higher than the private cost of violating the rule. Observers may care, yet still prefer not to spend attention, status, or goodwill turning suspicion into action.

That is why laziness matters in the chapter. Laziness here is not only moral weakness. It is ordinary reluctance to create conflict, gather evidence, test common knowledge, and bear the emotional cost of prosecution. A rule can therefore fail even when the group broadly endorses it. What fails is the path from suspicion to shared action.

The bridge to the next chapter is clear. If cheating survives not only by hiding from others but also by hiding inside ambiguity, then a deeper trick becomes possible. The mind itself may start helping with the concealment. Chapter 5 turns toward self-deception, where motive does not merely dodge public enforcement but becomes harder for the actor to see clearly too.
