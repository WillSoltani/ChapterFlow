School is usually defended as a place where people go to learn useful material. The chapter begins by arguing that this picture is too narrow. School often looks like it is also rewarding endurance, compliance, and credential completion in ways that go beyond the direct transfer of skills.

The chapter's first explanation is signaling. School may function as evidence of intelligence, conscientiousness, and conformity. Finishing assignments, showing up, enduring deadlines, and moving through a long institution can all signal traits that matter socially even when the specific content learned is not what others care about most.

This helps explain why schooling can matter as a credential. A diploma or school record may say something about cognitive ability, but it can also say that a person is disciplined, persistent, and able to fit inside institutional rules. In that sense, school becomes a public filter and signal, not only a place of instruction.

The chapter widens the picture further with other functions. School can act as babysitting by occupying young people and structuring their time. It can act as networking by bringing people into contact with peers and institutions. It can also serve indoctrination and domestication functions by shaping habits, expectations, and forms of social obedience.

That does not mean school never teaches. The chapter does not need learning to disappear. Its stronger claim is that real instruction sits alongside these other functions rather than exhausting what schooling is for. Education can produce knowledge while also acting as a signal of ability and fit.

The chapter's tension is therefore mixed motive again. School can genuinely develop students while also certifying intelligence, conscientiousness, and conformity and serving wider social-management roles. Once that is visible, the move to medicine becomes easier to follow: treatment too may combine real function with visible signals, this time through caring and sacrifice.
