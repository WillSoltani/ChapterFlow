If self-deception hides motive from the self, then explanation becomes a dangerous surface. That is where Chapter 6 begins. People do not only miss their motives. They often produce reasons that sound clean, coherent, and sincere even when those reasons are not the real cause of what they have done.

The chapter first frames this as strategic ignorance. The mind is not always simply confused by accident. Limited access to motive can be useful. When a person does not know the full cause of an action, the explanation offered afterward may come from a thinner and more socially manageable story.

That is why the chapter turns to confabulation. Confabulation is not just a technical oddity. It names the mind's willingness to generate an explanation after the fact when direct access to cause is missing. The explanation may sound reasonable, and the speaker may believe it. The counterfeit part is not in the tone. It is in the gap between the polished story and the real machinery.

Split-brain evidence matters because it shows that explanation can be assembled from partial information. When the system does not have full access to what produced an action, it can still rush to narrate the action as if understanding were complete. The mind prefers an answerable story over a visible gap.

This is where the chapter's image of the ego becomes sharp. The ego behaves more like a press secretary than a central executive. A press secretary explains, manages appearances, and offers the best available story to the audience. That is different from being the true command center that fully knows and directs every cause.

The chapter is not saying reasons never matter. It is saying that the reasons people report are often less authoritative than they feel. Explanation may be a public-facing surface built after action rather than a transparent window into motive. The self can therefore sound sincere and still be reporting from incomplete files.

That changes how the rest of the book has to be read. Hidden motives are no longer only secret drives under visible behavior. They are also hidden because the mind itself keeps narrating cleaner stories than the causal picture really supports. Once that is in place, everyday behavior can be read not as pure information exchange but as signaling through surfaces the body and mind only partly understand.

That is why the next chapter turns to body language. If verbal reasons are often manufactured after the fact, then honest signals may sometimes show up more clearly in channels that are harder to fake and harder for the self to fully script.
