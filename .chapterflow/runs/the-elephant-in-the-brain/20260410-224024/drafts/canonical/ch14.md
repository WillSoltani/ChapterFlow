Medicine is often described as if its only job were to improve health. The chapter begins by arguing that this story is incomplete. Some treatments feel reassuring not only because of their medical effect, but because they visibly show that someone is being cared for.

The chapter's core frame is conspicuous caring. Medicine may function partly as a public signal of concern. Time, pain, expense, effort, and ritual around treatment can all make care visible. In that sense, treatment can communicate that a patient is valued and attended to, not only that a condition is being addressed.

This helps explain why visible sacrifice matters. A burdensome treatment, a visible appointment, or an obvious caregiving effort can make concern socially legible. Public signals of care can shape how treatment is experienced and valued even when direct health claims are still important.

That does not mean medicine never heals. The chapter does not need health benefits to disappear. Its stronger claim is that treatment choices and medical rituals can be shaped by care signaling alongside medical effectiveness. The visible form of the act matters because it shows concern in a way other people can read.

The tension becomes clearest when health claims and care signals point in the same direction. A treatment may help physically and also communicate that someone is trying hard, sacrificing, or taking the condition seriously. The act is then doing more than one job at once.

That keeps the chapter from collapsing into either pure science or pure theater. Medicine can produce real benefit while also functioning partly as conspicuous caring. Once that overlap is visible, the move to religion becomes easier to understand: sacrifice and visible loyalty will matter there too, but at the level of group bonding and norm enforcement.
