If Chapter 2 is right, then human life should be unstable. A species pushed by sex, status, and politics ought to spend most of its time tearing itself apart. Chapter 3 begins with that problem. Humans compete, but they also live in groups that need enough order to keep functioning. The chapter's answer is norms: rules that groups create and enforce in order to suppress selfishness and hold cooperation together.

That answer matters because the chapter does not treat norms as noble slogans floating above daily life. Norms work when groups can actually enforce them. In the chapter's hunter-gatherer frame, people lived in small, nomadic, roughly egalitarian groups. Those groups did not stay egalitarian because everyone was gentle. They stayed egalitarian because stronger individuals could be checked by coalitions of weaker ones. Weapons mattered here because they changed the balance of force. Once a group of weaker members could threaten even the strongest member, enforcement became possible.

That already makes norms look less sentimental than modern moral language often suggests. A rule is not just an idea about how people should behave. It is a coordinated willingness to make selfish behavior costly. The chapter's point is not that humans suddenly became selfless. It is that groups learned how to restrain self-interest enough to preserve common life.

Some of that enforcement is obvious. Much of it is not. Gossip and reputation carry a surprising amount of the load. A person who violates group expectations does not only risk punishment in the dramatic sense. They risk becoming known as someone who cannot be trusted, someone who curries favor too openly, someone who boasts too much, or someone whose selfishness is no longer easy to ignore. In a small group, reputation is not decoration. It shapes who gets backed, who gets watched, and who gets quietly excluded.

That helps explain why the chapter pays attention to subtle norms rather than only to blunt ones. Rules against bragging, favor-currying, subgroup politics, and visible selfish motives are not trivial etiquette. They are ways of controlling how competition shows up inside a cooperative group. The group may tolerate ambition, but it often punishes ambition that becomes too legible. It may tolerate self-interest, but it punishes self-interest that refuses to hide behind a socially acceptable face.

That is what makes norms more interesting than a simple story about morality winning over selfishness. Norms are partly protective. They help groups suppress open predation and stabilize cooperation. But norms are also political tools. They decide which behaviors count as offensively selfish, which displays of self-advantage will be tolerated, and who gets to enforce the line. A group can use norms to preserve fairness, and it can use them to discipline anyone who makes the struggle for advantage too visible.

So the chapter does not really cancel the competitive games from before. It contains them. Norms are a partial escape from open conflict, not an exit from motive altogether. They let humans cooperate more successfully by forcing many selfish maneuvers underground or into subtler forms. That sets up the next chapter cleanly. If people still want the gains of selfish behavior while living under norm enforcement, then cheating will not usually look like open rebellion. It will look like rule-breaking under cover.
