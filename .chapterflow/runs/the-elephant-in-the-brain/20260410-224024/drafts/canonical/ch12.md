Charity is easy to describe as pure concern for outcomes. Yet the pattern of giving often looks more social than that simple picture suggests. People donate where others can see, respond to recognition, and seem drawn to forms of giving that communicate something about them as givers. The chapter begins from that discomfort and argues that helping cannot be understood through effectiveness alone.

The proposed explanation is that charity often signals wealth, empathy, and prosocial orientation. A visible gift can show that the giver has resources to spare. It can also show that the giver is the kind of person who cares, notices suffering, and wants to be seen as aligned with others' welfare. In that sense, charity functions partly as a moral and social signal.

This helps explain why visibility matters. Giving patterns often fit social-display motives more than a pure outcome-maximizing model would predict. Recognition, public association, and the visible form of sacrifice can shape what kinds of help people choose. The act is not only about what it does for the recipient. It is also about what it says about the giver.

That does not mean charity never helps. The chapter does not need giving to be fake in order to say that display matters. Real benefits can still be produced. The stronger claim is that helping behavior is often guided by reputational and social incentives alongside concern for impact.

The tension becomes clearest when effectiveness enters the picture. If pure impact were the only motive, giving patterns would not follow recognition and social legibility so strongly. The chapter therefore treats visible charity as another mixed-motive domain, one where moral orientation and public image often travel together.

That keeps the chapter from collapsing into either praise or cynicism. Giving can be genuinely beneficial while still signaling wealth, empathy, and prosocial orientation. Once that overlap is clear, the move to education makes sense: schooling may also mix real function with visible signals about intelligence, conscientiousness, and conformity.
