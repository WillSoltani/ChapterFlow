Words are often treated as the serious part of communication, but people do not always trust them most. That is the opening pressure of Chapter 7. After the last chapter weakened the authority of spoken reasons, the book turns to body language and asks why nonverbal behavior often seems more credible than the story people tell.

The chapter's answer is that much body language works as honest signaling. These cues are not mere decoration hanging off the real message. They are part of the message. In many situations, posture, tension, gaze, timing, distance, and tone help communicate what someone is ready for, how they feel, and what kind of social move they are making.

What makes body language important is not that it always tells the whole truth. The chapter is not building a magical lie detector. Its stronger claim is that many nonverbal cues are harder to fake cleanly than spoken reasons. They can therefore function as more honest signals, especially in situations where social stakes are high and direct verbal clarity would be costly.

That matters because body language helps coordinate socially sensitive behavior. In sex, politics, and status, a lot of interaction depends on signals that are legible without forcing everything into explicit speech. The body can indicate interest, hesitation, confidence, submission, alignment, discomfort, and threat in ways that let other people respond without requiring a fully stated proposition.

This coordination function helps explain why body language can matter so much when words are ambiguous or strategically polished. A room may watch who leans in, who retreats, whose smile stays tight, who looks at whom before speaking, and who appears relaxed under pressure. These are not trivial extras. They help people decide whether to advance, retreat, trust, challenge, flirt, back down, or shift coalitions.

The chapter does not claim that body language escapes strategy. Nonverbal behavior is still part of social competition. The point is subtler: some signals remain more constrained by the body and by context than verbal explanation does. That makes them informative without making them perfectly transparent.

So the body becomes a channel where hidden motives can show up with less verbal management. It is not pure leakage, and it is not pure theater either. It is a socially useful signaling system that lets people manage sensitive interactions when full verbal openness would be too blunt, too risky, or too easy to fake.

That is why the next chapter turns to laughter. If body language helps coordinate delicate social behavior, laughter shows how a signal can do that while also softening danger and preserving deniability at the same time.
