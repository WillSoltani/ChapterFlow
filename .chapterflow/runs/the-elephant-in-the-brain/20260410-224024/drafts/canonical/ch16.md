Politics is often described as if citizens mainly gather information and then choose the best policies. The chapter begins by arguing that this picture is incomplete. Political participation often looks hotter, costlier, and less informed than a pure policy-optimization model would predict.

Its first major frame is group loyalty. Democratic participation may function partly as a way of showing visible allegiance to a side. Voting, arguing, displaying symbols, and taking part in public conflict can all communicate which coalition someone stands with, not only what policies they have carefully optimized.

This helps explain why strong emotions matter. Anger, enthusiasm, outrage, and pride can make political alignment vivid and socially legible. Emotion is not just noise around reason; it can be part of how group commitment is displayed and reinforced.

Low information also fits the chapter's argument. If participation were mainly about fine-grained policy optimization, people might be expected to track details much more carefully. But if politics is also about side-taking, then visible loyalty can remain strong even when policy knowledge stays thin.

The chapter also highlights sacrifice. Time, risk, and public effort can all function as costly signs that someone is committed to the group. In that sense, political participation can show allegiance through burden, not only through policy analysis.

That does not mean politics never concerns real outcomes. The chapter does not need policy goals to disappear. Its stronger claim is that civic behavior can pursue collective aims while also serving as a display of loyalty under low-information, high-emotion conditions.

That keeps the chapter from collapsing into either pure rationalism or pure tribal theater. Politics can involve genuine concern for outcomes while still being shaped by group loyalty, sacrifice, and visible side-taking. Once that overlap is visible, the conclusion's call for humility and clearer self-understanding lands with more force.
