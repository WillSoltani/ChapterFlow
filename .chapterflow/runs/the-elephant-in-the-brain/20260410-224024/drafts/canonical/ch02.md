People do not only struggle against weather, hunger, and disease. They also struggle against each other inside the same group, while still needing that group to survive. That pressure is what gives Chapter 2 its shape. The authors argue that human behavior was sculpted not just by the outside environment, but by repeated competition among people who had to watch one another closely.

The first game is sex. Here the competition is for mates, which means the real contest is not only over desire, but over what makes someone look like a good partner. People signal traits that matter in that market. They try to look attractive, capable, stable, interesting, fertile, loyal, or strong, depending on what the audience values. The point is not that romance is fake. The point is that mating pressure makes display unavoidable. Once other people are choosing among possible partners, behavior starts carrying signal value whether anyone says so out loud or not.

The second game is status. This one can sound softer than mating, but the chapter treats it as just as serious. People do not only compete for lovers. They compete for friends, allies, and social regard. Being well-liked is not only pleasant. It changes who helps you, who includes you, who forgives you, and who backs you when conflict arrives. Status is therefore not a decorative prize floating above daily life. It affects access, protection, and influence inside the group.

That helps explain why people keep doing things whose literal payoff seems small while their social payoff is large. Wit, taste, bravery, generosity, and poise all matter because they are read by other people as clues. The chapter's deeper move is to say that these are not random clues. They are signals in a partner market. They help answer a question other people are always asking: what kind of friend, ally, or companion would you be to have around?

The third game is politics. Here the competition is not just for admiration or attraction, but for power through coalition. Human beings do not usually dominate alone. They recruit, coordinate, align, and choose sides. That makes politics broader than elections or formal office. It includes the constant work of finding allies, displaying loyalty, and showing that you can be trusted inside a group fight. Once power depends on coalitions, behavior starts getting evaluated for what it says about loyalty, coordination, and usefulness under shared pressure.

These three games differ in audience and stakes, but the chapter says they share one mechanism. All of them involve sending and receiving signals about partner quality. In sex, the question is what kind of mate you would be. In status, it is what kind of friend or companion you would be. In politics, it is what kind of ally you would be. The signal changes with the game, but the pressure to be legible does not.

That is what makes the chapter more than a list. It is offering a background condition for the rest of the book. If people are constantly being read as possible partners in these overlapping games, then a lot of everyday behavior will contain more display than it first admits. That does not make cooperation disappear. It makes cooperation more strategic, because being helpful, attractive, impressive, or loyal can all be ways of competing without announcing that competition directly.

The tension is that human groups cannot function on competition alone. People still need trust, norms, and real coordination. The chapter does not deny that. It claims something narrower and sharper: cooperation often unfolds inside competitive games rather than outside them. The same act can support the group and improve your position in it. That is why the next chapter needs to talk about norms. Once competition is this central, some force has to keep it from tearing the group apart.
