Charity is often described as pure concern for others, but patterns of giving can look more social than that. The chapter argues that helping behavior cannot be understood through effectiveness alone.

Its main claim is that charity often signals wealth, empathy, and prosocial orientation. A visible donation can show resources, caring, and a public willingness to align with others' welfare.

This helps explain why recognition and visibility matter so much. Giving patterns often fit social-display motives better than a narrow outcome-maximizing model would predict. The act can help others while also communicating something about the giver.

That does not make charity fake. Real benefits can still be produced. The stronger point is that helping is often shaped by reputational and moral-signaling incentives alongside concern for impact.

The hard lesson is mixed motive. Charity can be genuinely beneficial while also serving as a visible signal of wealth, empathy, and public-mindedness. That bridge leads into education, where credentials may also combine real function with social display.
