It is unsettling to think that sincerity might not be the opposite of deception. Chapter 5 takes that step. After cheating under fig leaves, the book asks what happens when the mind hides truth from itself so public deception becomes easier to carry.

The chapter first contrasts older defensive theories of self-deception with newer evolutionary ones. The older picture says people deceive themselves to avoid pain, shame, or inner conflict. The chapter keeps that possibility alive, but says it is incomplete if self-deception also helps in social competition.

The newer account says hidden truths may be concealed from the self in order to mislead other people more effectively. If someone does not fully see the selfish motive behind a claim, the claim can come out with more conviction. The person looks sincere partly because visible doubt is reduced at the source.

The chapter does not solve this by inventing a tiny inner liar who knowingly tricks the rest of the mind. Instead it leans on modular minds. Mental processes can be partly separate, so some information is available in one place without being equally available everywhere else.

That is where self-discretion enters. One part of the mind can stay partly ignorant of what another part is doing or wanting. The result is structured opacity rather than chaos. People can feel honest while still lacking clear access to the motives shaping their behavior.

This makes self-deception socially useful. Hidden ambition, vanity, or selfish calculation becomes harder for others to read when it is also harder for the actor to read clearly. The next chapter asks what happens once the mind starts supplying counterfeit reasons on top of that limited access.
