Some art seems more valuable when it looks difficult, wasteful, or impractical. That is the opening clue. The chapter argues that direct function cannot explain all of what people value in art.

Its main framework is costly signaling. Art can signal value because visible effort, burden, or waste can be hard to fake. The handicap principle sharpens that point: what looks inefficient from a narrow practical view can help make the signal credible.

This does not erase genuine appreciation. People can still be moved by art. The stronger claim is that costliness, impracticality, and extrinsic features can help support the value people attach to the work.

Impracticality matters because it separates art from ordinary tool use. Extrinsic features matter because the surrounding signs of cost, effort, or seriousness can shape what the work communicates socially.

The hard point is coexistence. Art can be genuinely appreciated while also carrying value through costly signaling. That bridge leads into charity, where visible sacrifice and display matter again.
