Obvious luxury spending is only the easiest case. The chapter argues that conspicuous consumption is part of a broader consumer-signaling system, not the whole story.

Purchases can signal wealth, but they can also signal conscientiousness, adventurousness, and identity. A buying choice can therefore do more than solve a practical problem. It can also communicate something about character, taste, or group fit.

This does not mean utility disappears. People still buy useful things for practical reasons. The stronger claim is that visible purchases often carry extra public meaning alongside whatever direct use they have.

That is why the chapter expands beyond luxury display. Some purchases look careful and disciplined. Others look bold, exploratory, or identity-marking. Consumer choices can therefore work as layered social signals even when they are not the most expensive option in view.

The hard point is mixed motive. Consumption can be practical and expressive at the same time. That bridge leads into art, where signaling becomes even more visible through costliness and impractical display.
