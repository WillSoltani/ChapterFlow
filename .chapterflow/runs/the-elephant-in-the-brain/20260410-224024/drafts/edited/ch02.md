People do not only struggle against weather, hunger, and disease. They also struggle inside the group they need. Chapter 2 takes that pressure seriously. The claim is that human behavior was shaped not just by the outside environment, but by repeated competition among people watching one another closely.

The first game is sex. The competition is for mates, which means the contest is also over what makes someone look like a good partner. People signal traits the market will reward. They try to look attractive, capable, stable, interesting, loyal, or strong. The point is not that romance is fake. The point is that mate choice makes display unavoidable. Once other people are choosing among possible partners, behavior starts carrying signal value whether anyone admits it or not.

The second game is status. This one can sound lighter than mating, but the chapter treats it as equally serious. People compete for friends, allies, and social regard. Being well-liked does not just feel good. It changes who includes you, who helps you, who trusts you, and who backs you when conflict arrives. Status is not a decorative extra. It affects access and protection inside the group.

That is why people keep doing things whose literal payoff seems modest while their social payoff is large. Wit, bravery, generosity, taste, and poise matter because other people read them as clues. The chapter's sharper move is to say that these clues sit inside a partner market. They help answer a running social question: what kind of friend, ally, or companion would you be to have nearby?

The third game is politics. Here the competition is for power through coalition. Human beings rarely dominate alone. They recruit, coordinate, align, and choose sides. That makes politics broader than government or elections. It includes the daily work of displaying loyalty, reading alliances, and showing that you are useful in a group contest. Once power depends on coalitions, behavior gets judged for what it says about trust, usefulness, and coordination under pressure.

These three games differ in audience and stakes, but they share one mechanism. All of them involve sending and receiving signals about partner quality. In sex, the question is what kind of mate you would be. In status, it is what kind of friend you would be. In politics, it is what kind of ally you would be. The signal changes with the game. The pressure to be legible does not.

That is the chapter's real value. It turns competition into background structure for later chapters instead of leaving it as one topic among many. If people are constantly being read as possible partners in overlapping games, then much of everyday behavior will contain more display than it first admits. Cooperation still matters. It just often unfolds inside competition rather than outside it. The same act can help the group and improve your place in it, which is why the next chapter has to ask how norms hold that pressure in check.
