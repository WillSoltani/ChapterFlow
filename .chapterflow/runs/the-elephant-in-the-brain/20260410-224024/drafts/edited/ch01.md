Two monkeys sit still while one picks through the other's fur. The scene looks hygienic. It also looks gentle. If the act removes dirt and parasites, the explanation seems finished. But grooming keeps showing up where alliances, rank, and tension are being managed. It clusters around relationships that matter. The act still cleans. Its larger job is political.

The chapter's first move is simple. A behavior can have a clean surface function and a social payoff at the same time. Calling grooming political does not mean hygiene is fake. It means hygiene is not the whole story. The same motion can remove parasites while also buying tolerance, signaling loyalty, and tending the bonds that shape life inside the group.

Arabian babblers push the point harder because their behavior looks even nobler. They stand guard. They feed others. They take visible risks. The public story writes itself: sacrifice for the group. But once helping is visible, helping can rank the helpers. A bird can gain standing by being the one that appears bravest, most useful, or most devoted.

Competitive altruism gives the chapter its sharper edge. The behavior may still help the group, but it also works as a prestige bid. The warning call still warns. The food still gets shared. Yet the helper is not outside social incentives. The helper is using a prosocial-looking act to compete inside them. That blocks the easy choice between sainthood and fraud. Useful behavior and status pursuit can occupy the same act.

The animal cases matter because they teach a method before the book turns the light on human beings. Few readers feel accused by monkey grooming or babbler showmanship. That lowers the temperature. It lets the authors train a diagnostic habit: when a behavior has an official function, ask what else it buys inside the group. The hidden motive may not replace the visible one. It may sit underneath it, steering when the behavior appears, who performs it, and who receives it.

That habit becomes corrosive if you use it lazily. Once you learn to spot prestige and alliance payoffs, you can start flattening every kind act into theater. The chapter does not earn that conclusion. It earns a narrower one. Social payoff belongs in the explanation more often than polite accounts admit. It does not follow that status is the only thing happening, or that a useful outcome stops counting once standing is attached to it.

The harder question is explanatory, not moral: when one act serves the group and raises the actor at the same time, which motive should carry the story? The chapter does not settle that problem. It makes it unavoidable. Grooming can clean and curry favor. Babbler helping can protect the group and advertise the helper. Once that mixed structure becomes visible, the rest of the book has somewhere solid to stand.
