Medicine is usually described as health optimization, but the chapter argues that this is too narrow. Some treatments feel valuable partly because they visibly show care.

Its main idea is conspicuous caring. Medicine can function as a public signal of concern through visible effort, sacrifice, time, or burden. Treatment can therefore communicate that someone is being looked after, not only that a condition is being treated.

This does not erase health claims. Real healing can still matter. The stronger point is that visible sacrifice and public care signals can shape how treatment is chosen and understood alongside medical effectiveness.

The chapter keeps both sides alive. Medicine can help physically while also making care publicly legible. That is why treatment can feel reassuring partly because of what it shows about concern, not only because of what it does biologically.

The hard lesson is coexistence. Healing and visible caring can travel together. That bridge leads into religion, where sacrifice and loyalty signals become socially central.
