If Chapter 2 is right, human life should be unstable. A species pushed by sex, status, and politics ought to spend most of its time splitting into conflict. Chapter 3 begins with that problem. Humans compete, but they also depend on groups that need enough order to keep functioning. The chapter's answer is norms: rules groups create and enforce to suppress selfishness and hold cooperation together.

The chapter does not treat norms as noble language floating above daily life. Norms work when groups can enforce them. In the hunter-gatherer frame, people lived in small, nomadic, roughly egalitarian groups. Those groups did not stay egalitarian because everyone was kind. They stayed egalitarian because stronger individuals could be checked by coalitions of weaker ones. Weapons mattered because they changed the balance of force. Once weaker members could threaten even the strongest member together, enforcement became real.

That makes norms look less sentimental than modern moral talk often suggests. A rule is not only an idea about how people should behave. It is a coordinated willingness to make selfish behavior costly. The chapter is not saying humans became selfless. It is saying groups learned how to restrain self-interest enough to preserve common life.

Some enforcement is obvious. Much of it is quiet. Gossip and reputation do a large share of the work. A person who violates group expectations does not only risk punishment in the dramatic sense. They risk becoming known as someone who cannot be trusted, someone who boasts too much, someone who curries favor too openly, or someone whose selfishness has become visible enough to bother other people. In a small group, reputation is not a side issue. It shapes who gets backed, watched, and quietly excluded.

That is why the chapter cares about subtle norms rather than only blunt rules. Norms against bragging, favor-currying, subgroup politics, and visible selfish motives are not trivial etiquette. They regulate how competition can appear inside a cooperative group. Ambition may survive. Overt self-advantage often does not. The group tolerates more selfishness than it tolerates obvious selfishness.

That makes norms morally mixed. They protect the group by suppressing open predation and stabilizing cooperation. They also function as political tools. They decide which displays of self-interest count as intolerable, who gets to draw that line, and whose motives have to stay hidden. The chapter does not cancel competition. It contains it. Norms are a partial escape from open conflict, not an escape from motive itself.

That is why the next step is cheating. Once people still want the gains of selfish behavior while living under norm enforcement, the pressure shifts. Rule-breaking does not have to look like open rebellion. It can survive through cover, ambiguity, and excuses that make enforcement harder than suspicion alone.
