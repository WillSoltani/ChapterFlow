Everyone in the room can tell something is off, yet nobody cleanly names it. That is the frustration Chapter 4 starts with. Norms do not remove the incentive to break rules. They make people want the payoff of selfish behavior without paying the social cost of open violation.

The chapter rejects the simple picture of cheating as secret rule-breaking done entirely in the dark. Some cheating is hidden, but the more interesting kind happens in broad daylight. Other people often see enough to suspect the violation. The problem is that suspicion is weaker than proof the group can act on together.

A thin fig leaf solves that problem for the cheater. It does not need to fool everyone. It only needs to make confrontation awkward and costly. If calling out the violation means arguing over intent, tone, or whether the evidence is strong enough, many observers back off.

Weak norms make that hesitation easier. Some rules only hold when people share common knowledge about the violation and know others are ready to enforce it too. Without that shared certainty, each observer waits for someone else to move first. The result is public cheating that survives because suspicion never becomes coordinated action.

Discreet communication and ready-made excuses help keep violations in that ambiguous zone. A private signal, a partial explanation, or a thin cover story can be enough to block clean enforcement. Anyone who wants to push the issue now has to bear the cost of escalation.

The chapter therefore treats cheating as a structural temptation under enforcement friction, not just as a villain story. Rules can fail because people do not want the conflict, proof work, and social exposure that enforcement demands. The next chapter asks what happens when concealment moves inward and the mind itself helps hide motive.
