If self-deception hides motive from the self, explanation becomes a risky surface. Chapter 6 starts there. People do not only miss their motives. They often produce reasons that sound clean and sincere even when those reasons are not the real cause of what they have done.

The chapter first frames this as strategic ignorance. Limited access to motive is not always an accident. When a person does not know the full cause of an action, the explanation that appears afterward can come from a thinner and more manageable story.

That leads into confabulation. The mind can generate an explanation after the fact when direct access to cause is missing. The explanation may sound reasonable, and the speaker may believe it. What makes it counterfeit is the gap between the polished story and the real machinery.

Split-brain evidence matters because it shows how explanation can be assembled from partial information. The system may not know the full cause, yet it still prefers a coherent answer to an open gap.

This supports the chapter's sharper image of the ego. The ego behaves more like a press secretary than a central executive. It explains, manages appearances, and offers a usable story, but it does not necessarily command or fully understand the whole system it speaks for.

The chapter does not say reasons never matter. It says reported reasons are often less authoritative than they feel. The next chapter moves to body language, where honest signals may reveal more than the stories people tell about themselves.
