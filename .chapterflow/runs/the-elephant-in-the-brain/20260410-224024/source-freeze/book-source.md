## Official Overview

The official site frames the book's core thesis as follows: humans are primates and political animals, and many social behaviors are shaped by hidden motives that are easier to pursue when they stay partly hidden. The book promises to examine both everyday behaviors and large institutions such as art, charity, education, politics, and religion through that lens.

## Official Outline

Introduction:
- explains the thesis that humans are prone to self-deception about their motives
- previews the evidence and the trajectory of the book
- warns against reading the thesis as total contempt for humanity

Part I: Why we hide our motives

1. Animal Behavior
- social grooming among primates looks hygienic but functions politically
- competitive altruism in Arabian babbler birds looks prosocial but is prestige jockeying
- these cases serve as analogues for later human behavior

2. Competition
- human physiology and behavior were shaped partly by intra-group competition
- three competitive games: sex, social status, politics
- all three involve signaling qualities to potential partners, allies, or coalitions

3. Norms
- humans make and enforce rules within groups
- norms suppress selfishness and support collective welfare
- hunter-gatherer egalitarianism relied on weapons, coalition enforcement, gossip, and reputation
- subtle norms also suppress bragging, favor-currying, subgroup politics, and selfish motives

4. Cheating
- humans have incentives to cheat when they think they can get away with it
- many violations happen in broad daylight under thin fig leaves
- weak norms, lazy enforcement, discreet communication, and ready-made excuses help hide motives

5. Self-Deception
- compares older defensive theories of self-deception with newer evolutionary ones
- hidden truths may be concealed from the self in order to mislead other people more effectively
- modular minds allow inconsistent inner processes and self-discretion

6. Counterfeit Reasons
- strategic ignorance about motives
- split-brain and confabulation evidence show that the mind can fabricate explanations
- the ego behaves more like a press secretary than a central executive

Part II: Hidden motives in everyday life

7. Body Language
- much body language works as honest signaling
- it helps coordinate socially sensitive behavior in sex, politics, and status

8. Laughter
- laughter is a play signal
- it softens physical and social danger and enables deniability

9. Conversation
- people talk not just to exchange information but to show off their ability to know and learn
- the chapter connects that theory to news and academic research

10. Consumption
- conspicuous consumption is one signaling pattern inside a broader consumer-signaling landscape
- purchases can signal wealth, conscientiousness, adventurousness, and identity

11. Art
- art can be understood through costly signaling and the handicap principle
- impracticality and extrinsic features help explain how art signals value

12. Charity
- charity often signals wealth, empathy, and prosocial orientation
- giving patterns fit social-display motives more than pure effectiveness motives

13. Education
- school may signal intelligence, conscientiousness, and conformity
- the chapter also raises babysitting, networking, indoctrination, and domestication functions

14. Medicine
- medicine may function partly as conspicuous caring
- visible sacrifice and public signals of care matter alongside health claims

15. Religion
- religion helps groups bond, display loyalty, and enforce norms
- sacrifice, ritual synchrony, badges, sermons, and supernatural enforcement all matter

16. Politics
- democratic participation may express group loyalty as much as policy optimization
- strong emotions, low information, and sacrifice fit the group-loyalty account

Conclusion
- motive awareness should lead to humility, clearer self-understanding, and better institutional design
- readers are warned against flattening the whole picture into despair or total cynicism
