A management problem comes into view as soon as Part 1 ends. If disruptive markets begin where the established business is least prepared to value them, then strong managers can still make the wrong call while doing what looks like serious work. They ask for evidence, forecasts, margins, and customer demand that make sense in the mature business. The new effort fails those tests, so it gets treated as weak before it has had a fair chance to learn.

That is why the book has to pivot. The next question is not whether disruptive change exists. The next question is how it should be managed when its first market does not look like the incumbent's best business. A leader cannot solve that by repeating the old scorecard more loudly.

The first adjustment is conceptual. Managing disruption is not the same as suspending discipline. It is a change in what discipline means. In the established business, managers optimize around known customers, known performance targets, and visible financial logic. In an early disruptive market, the immediate task is often to discover who the first real users are, what job the product can already do, and which signals matter before the market is large enough to look respectable.

That creates a proof mismatch. The incumbent organization is trained to respect clean projections and familiar customers. The disruptive effort often begins with neither. If managers demand mature proof from an immature market, they can kill the initiative for failing a test it could not yet reasonably pass. The error is not laziness. It is the use of the wrong standard at the wrong stage.

The chapter therefore changes the managerial posture. Instead of asking only whether the disruptive effort already fits the old business model, managers need to ask what kind of learning would show whether the effort has a live market at all. A small pilot, a rough first use case, or a modest set of early users may matter more than a polished business case. The goal is not immediate scale. The goal is contact with reality.

That limit matters. The chapter is not arguing that every uncertain idea deserves protection. Managers still have to choose, constrain, and judge. The point is narrower. A disruptive effort should not be dismissed only because it cannot yet satisfy the incumbent business's preferred proof demands. Serious management remains necessary, but the objects of management shift from optimization to discovery.

This is why Part 2 opens here. Once the problem is framed correctly, later chapters can specify what the response requires: where responsibility should sit, how market size changes what a unit can absorb, how emerging markets must be discovered, and how organizational capabilities can become disabilities. Chapter 5 does not finish that argument. It clears the ground for it by naming the management error that would otherwise poison every response.
