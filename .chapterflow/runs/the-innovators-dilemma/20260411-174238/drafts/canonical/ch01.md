The hard disk drive business changed so quickly that it exposed failure in fast motion. One generation of product would create a leader, the next would crown someone else, and firms that had looked disciplined and formidable would vanish from the front rank. Christensen starts there because the pattern is too repeated to dismiss as bad luck. The industry behaves like a compressed business laboratory. You can watch companies make sensible decisions, satisfy real customers, improve real products, and still lose the future.

That opening matters because it strips failure of the easy excuses. The firms in view are not asleep. They are not ignoring engineering. They are not refusing to invest. They are moving, but they are moving along the line that already makes sense to them. The chapter's first distinction appears here: a company can be excellent at improving what its current market rewards and still be poorly positioned for what the next market will want.

Inside that logic, sustaining innovation looks like the responsible path. Established firms listen to their best customers, hear requests for more capacity, more reliability, and better performance, and then fund projects that deliver exactly that. Nothing about the move is irrational. If the people who pay your bills want stronger versions of the existing product, building those stronger versions is what competence looks like. The chapter gains force by refusing to mock that instinct. Good management is not absent. Good management is the setup.

The trouble arrives when a different kind of technology shows up with the wrong early profile. A disruptive approach does not begin by beating the incumbent on the incumbent's preferred measures. It often arrives looking smaller, weaker, cheaper, or less polished. To the incumbent's best customers, it can seem beneath serious attention. That makes the first market for the disruption easy to dismiss. It is not where the leader earns its margins, protects its status, or hears its loudest demands.

This is the chapter's central trap. When managers keep asking the current business what deserves investment, the current business gives a coherent answer. It points toward sustaining improvements that matter now. That answer can strengthen the firm in the short run and still misdirect it in the long run. The company climbs higher on the metrics its customers already value while a new trajectory forms below, in a market segment that looks too small or too undemanding to matter. By the time the new trajectory rises into the core market, the incumbent is no longer deciding from a position of calm.

The disk drive industry makes that pattern hard to ignore because the handoffs happen again and again. Leadership does not merely shift once after a freak invention. It moves across generations. That repetition is what gives Christensen permission to make a stronger claim. He is not describing an isolated error. He is exposing a mechanism. Firms are not only defeated by bad execution. They can be defeated by disciplined execution inside the wrong horizon.

Seen this way, customer focus becomes more complicated than standard business advice allows. Serving customers well is still necessary. The chapter does not argue for contempt toward customers or for random bets detached from demand. It argues that demand has layers. The customers who matter most to your current system may not be the customers who reveal where the market is going next. If a company treats the loudest profitable demand as the whole market, it mistakes a local truth for a total one.

That is why the chapter feels less like a celebration of disruption than a warning about success. A company earns its confidence by learning what good products look like in its existing market. It builds processes, habits, and expectations around that success. Those strengths become filters. A weak-looking entrant is judged by standards it never intended to meet at first. The new technology is not merely underpowered. It is under-read. Incumbents look at it through the eyes of customers who have already moved beyond the conditions that made the entrant useful in the first place.

The deeper sting is that the incumbent can be right about the entrant's weaknesses and wrong about the entrant's future. Early disruptive products often are worse on the dimensions that established buyers prize. The mistake is not mismeasuring the present. The mistake is assuming the present metrics will remain the only ones that count. Once a technology improves enough, the ground shifts. What looked inferior in a fringe market can become good enough for the mainstream, and good enough is often enough to redraw the contest.

That creates a hard managerial contradiction. The firm that stays closest to its best customers can look admirable, disciplined, and rational while it reinforces exactly the allocation pattern that makes it late. Christensen does not resolve the contradiction here. He sharpens it. If smart firms keep failing in patterned ways, the real question is not why they suddenly become foolish. The real question is what kind of market logic keeps turning sensible decisions into strategic blindness.
