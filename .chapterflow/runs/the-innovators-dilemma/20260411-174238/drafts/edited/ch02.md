The same product can look weak in one market and useful in another. Christensen opens Chapter 2 from that disorienting fact because it explains why the firms in Chapter 1 were not simply irrational. A technology does not carry one fixed meaning everywhere. What counts as good enough, worth paying for, or worth attention depends on the network of customers, margins, and performance expectations around it.

That is the work done by a value network. It is not just a customer list or a demographic label. It is the market setting that tells a firm which improvements matter, which buyers are worth chasing, and which projects look too small to deserve real resources. Inside one network, higher performance may be the obvious next step. Inside another, lower cost, simpler use, or easier access may matter more. The chapter's central point is that product quality is always being judged inside a context.

Once that is clear, incumbent behavior becomes easier to read. Established firms innovate toward the customers and margins that already sustain the business. They do not choose blindness for sport. They respond to the rewards built into the network they know. If profitable buyers want better versions of the existing product, then better versions of the existing product will keep rising to the top of the queue. The company's logic is coherent. Its horizon is the problem.

That is where disruption hides. A new product can look unattractive inside the incumbent's value network because it underperforms on the measures the incumbent's best customers already use. Yet the same product can be compelling in a different network where those measures carry less weight. A lower-end buyer, a neglected user group, or a smaller market may care less about elite performance and more about convenience, accessibility, or price. The entrant does not need to win everywhere. It needs one network that sees value before the incumbent does.

This changes the meaning of rejection. When an incumbent turns away a weak-looking product, it may be making the correct call for the network it serves right now. That is why Christensen's argument cuts deeper than a complaint about arrogance. The firm can be right locally and wrong strategically. It is not failing to evaluate. It is evaluating inside a frame that cannot see the whole field.

The value-network idea also clarifies why customer focus alone cannot solve the problem. Customers are not wrong when they ask for better performance on the dimensions they already care about. They are describing the next move inside their world. The trouble is that their world may not be the only one that matters. If a firm mistakes its current network for the market itself, then even honest customer feedback becomes a narrowing force.

The chapter's harder lesson is that a company does not simply need better forecasting. It needs a way to tell the difference between weak fit in its current network and weak fit everywhere. Without that distinction, managers keep reading a new technology as defective when it may only be early and misaligned. The product looks bad by one scorecard and promising by another.

Seen this way, value networks do not make incumbents helpless. They make the real problem more precise. A firm has to serve the network that pays for the business it already has, but it cannot let that network define the limits of what counts as worth noticing. The chapter ends by opening the next test: does this same logic appear outside disk drives, or does the pattern break in another industry?
