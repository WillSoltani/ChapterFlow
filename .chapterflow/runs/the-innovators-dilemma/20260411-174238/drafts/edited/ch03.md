If the argument about disruption works only in disk drives, it is an interesting industry story and not much more. Christensen turns to mechanical excavators to test exactly that weakness. He wants to know whether the same pattern survives when the product, customers, and engineering language all change. The chapter matters because it tries to move the theory from a vivid case into a repeated structure.

In the established excavator market, cable-actuated machines held the main ground. They fit the dominant jobs, the dominant expectations, and the dominant standard of serious performance. That meant incumbent firms were not standing in empty space. They had a product that matched the market they knew how to serve. The old technology was not absurd. It was established for reasons that made sense.

Hydraulic excavators did not first win by beating cable machines everywhere that mattered to mainstream buyers. They found room in another use case, one where different tradeoffs made the new technology good enough to matter. The point of the foothold is crucial. A disruptive product does not need universal approval. It needs a market where its early weaknesses are tolerable and its practical advantages are real.

That is why the excavator chapter strengthens the theory. The same logic from the earlier chapters appears again. Established firms are judging the new product through the standards of the market that already rewards them. Entrants are learning in a smaller network that values the product differently. What looks underpowered in the incumbent's world can still be useful in the entrant's first world.

The chapter's edge gets sharper when incumbents try to respond. Owning or touching the new technology is not enough by itself. If the firm still tries to commercialize the product through the expectations of the old market, the response can stay strategically weak. Christensen is pushing an uncomfortable distinction here: technological participation is not the same thing as market fit.

That distinction keeps the theory from collapsing into a simple tale about stubbornness. Incumbents do not fail only when they refuse the new technology. They can also fail when they approach the new technology from inside the wrong commercial logic. The excavator case shows how a company can move toward the disruption and still drag the old standards along with it.

This is what makes the chapter more than another example. It shows repeated structure across industries without pretending the surface details are identical. Cable excavators are not disk drives, and hydraulic systems are not hard disk form factors. But the same deeper movement appears: a new technology enters through a market the incumbent does not read well, learns there, and later threatens the mainstream position.

That repeated movement opens the next problem. If disruption can gain footholds in neglected or lower-status markets, why are incumbents so ready to keep moving upward while leaving those footholds behind? The next chapter answers that by shifting from repeated pattern to the asymmetry built into profit logic itself.
