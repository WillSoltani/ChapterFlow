Discovering a new market does not answer whether the organization is the right instrument for serving it. Chapter 9 argues that managers must appraise their organization's capabilities and disabilities in context. A capability is not simply a strength that travels everywhere intact. It is a strength relative to a particular task, market, and way of working.

That is the chapter's first correction. Organizations often trust their proven routines because those routines helped create past success. But the same habits, priorities, and processes can become liabilities when the task changes. Excellence in one setting does not automatically create fitness in another.

This is why the chapter is unsettling. A company can be highly capable by its old standards and still be the wrong instrument for the new challenge. The problem is not always lack of talent or effort. It can be that the organization's routines are optimized for a different kind of market and therefore misfit the new one.

That makes self-appraisal more demanding than generic confidence. Managers have to ask not only whether the organization is strong, but whether its strengths match the work required now. A prized process can be highly effective in the core business and still choke a fragile new effort. A disciplined routine can become a disability when the market asks for something else.

The chapter therefore reframes appraisal as a fit question. Capabilities and disabilities are not opposite categories fixed forever. They depend on the relationship between what the organization is built to do and what the situation demands. That is why a strength can reverse into a weakness across contexts.

The limit matters here too. Chapter 9 is not saying every established routine is harmful or that past excellence should be distrusted automatically. The point is narrower. Managers should not confuse proven competence with universal readiness. They need to judge the match between their organization and the new task.

This opens the next question naturally. Once managers understand what their organization can and cannot do well, they still need to know how strategy should actually be formed under those conditions. Chapter 9 clarifies organizational fit. Chapter 10 turns to the strategy development process.
