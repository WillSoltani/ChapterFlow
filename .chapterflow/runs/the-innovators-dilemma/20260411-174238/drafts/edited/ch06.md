Responsibility becomes the next serious question once managers stop grading a disruptive effort by the old business case alone. If the disruptive technology is housed inside a unit whose customers do not need it, that unit has little real reason to make the effort central. Leaders may ask it to care, but its daily priorities will still be shaped by the customers who pay for its current success.

That is the force of Chapter 6. The right home for disruptive work is not automatically the incumbent organization that already dominates the industry. It is the organization whose customers create a live pull for the new technology. Without that pull, the effort can be treated as interesting, strategic, and still secondary in every concrete resource decision.

The mechanism is structural rather than psychological. Organizations learn what matters from the customers they serve. Those customers define what counts as urgent, profitable, and worth improving. If the disruptive technology does not matter to them yet, the incumbent unit will keep finding sensible reasons to delay, trim, or redirect the effort toward the old trajectory.

That is why executive intention is not enough. A senior leader can announce support for the disruptive project, but the unit still has to answer the needs of the customers that justify its budget and attention. The problem is not simply weak commitment. It is organizational misfit. The wrong home keeps translating the disruptive effort into the language of customers who are not asking for it.

The chapter therefore pushes toward separate or autonomous responsibility. The point is not separation for its own sake. The point is to place the effort where success is judged by customers who actually need the technology. That gives the initiative a fairer test and a real reason to be prioritized.

The limit matters here too. The chapter is not saying every disruptive move requires a totally separate company or that organization charts solve the problem by themselves. The narrower claim is that customer fit should govern placement. If responsibility sits in the wrong unit, competence can still produce rational neglect.

This connects directly to the next problem in Part 2. Once the disruptive effort has the right organizational home, managers still have to ask whether that unit is sized appropriately for the market it serves. Chapter 6 clarifies where responsibility belongs. Chapter 7 will sharpen what kind of organization can live on the market's actual scale.
