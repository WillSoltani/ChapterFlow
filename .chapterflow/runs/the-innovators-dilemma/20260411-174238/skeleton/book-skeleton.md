# Book Skeleton

Metadata:
- Book: The Innovator's Dilemma: When New Technologies Cause Great Firms to Fail
- Author: Clayton M. Christensen
- Edition: Harvard Business Review Press 2013 reprint
- Book ID: the-innovators-dilemma
- Variant Family: EMH

Chapter Order And One-Line Intent:
1. How Can Great Firms Fail? Insights from the Hard Disk Drive Industry
Expose the central paradox: strong firms fail not because they ignore customers, but because they follow their best customers too faithfully.
2. Value Networks and the Impetus to Innovate
Show how value networks shape what counts as rational innovation and why incumbents misread disruptive entrants.
3. Disruptive Technological Change in the Mechanical Excavator Industry
Test the disk-drive logic in another industry to prove the pattern is not a one-off.
4. What Goes Up, Can't Go Down
Show how performance oversupply opens room for simpler products to move upward.
5. Managing Disruptive Technological Change
Shift from diagnosis to managerial response.
6. Give Responsibility for Disruptive Technologies to Organizations Whose Customers Need Them
Argue for structural separation from the incumbent customer's pull.
7. Match the Size of the Organization to the Size of the Market
Show why big firms reject markets too small to matter to their growth math.
8. Discovering New and Emerging Markets
Replace forecast confidence with learning-driven discovery.
9. How to Appraise Your Organization's Capabilities and Disabilities
Distinguish resources from processes and values so managers stop overestimating what their firms can actually do.
10. Managing the Strategy Development Process
Show how emergent strategy and bounded experiments outperform rigid top-down prediction in disruptive settings.
11. The Dilemmas of Innovation: A Summary
Compress the recurring dilemmas into a durable decision framework.

Source Richness By Chapter:
- Ch01: high, with strong case anchor and broad secondary support
- Ch02: medium-high, title is explicit and secondary support is solid
- Ch03: medium, anchored by excavator case
- Ch04: medium, concept-heavy and more vulnerable to abstraction drift
- Ch05: medium-high, transition chapter with managerial framing
- Ch06: high, clear prescription and strong secondary support
- Ch07: high, clear market-size logic and examples
- Ch08: high, discovery-driven planning arc is well supported
- Ch09: medium, concept chapter needs careful narrowing
- Ch10: medium, process chapter likely thinner in frozen bundle
- Ch11: medium, synthesis chapter must avoid generic summary drift

Concept Density Estimate:
- Highest density: Ch02, Ch08, Ch09, Ch10, Ch11
- Highest case pressure: Ch01, Ch03, Ch06, Ch07

Moral Complexity Flags:
- Keep the book descriptive rather than triumphalist about disruption.
- Avoid treating customer focus as stupidity; the tension is that good management can produce failure.
- Treat autonomous spin-outs and market experiments as strategic tradeoffs, not universal commandments.

Rotation Plan For Examples:
- Work-heavy examples are natural, so school and personal scenarios must be used deliberately to prevent business monoculture in support surfaces.
- Keep at least two scenarios per chapter outside corporate settings.

School-Setting Plan:
- Use debate team, student startup club, lab section, history seminar, robotics project, and group-study planning as recurring school anchors.

Motif / Callback Opportunities:
- good management becoming the trap

Thin-Chapter Risk Notes:
- Ch04, Ch09, Ch10, and Ch11 can drift into generic management prose if the mechanism is not kept concrete.
- Ch09 and Ch10 need especially strict support boundaries because the frozen source is thinner than the early case chapters.

Premium-Routing Candidates:
- Ch01 and Ch02 establish the causal logic floor.
- Ch08 through Ch10 carry the most conceptual compression risk and need especially careful hard-depth handling.
