Core Claim:
Chapter 2 explains why incumbent behavior in Chapter 1 was not irrational. Firms innovate inside value networks that tell them what performance improvements matter, which customers count, and what margins make a project worth attention. A disruptive product looks weak when judged inside the incumbent's current network, even if it fits another network perfectly. The chapter turns failure from a moral story into a structural one.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
This is the chapter that makes the paradox legible. Instead of more disk-drive turnover alone, it names the market logic that turns sensible management into a blind spot.

Author Logic Chain:
First the author defines value networks as the market context that gives products their meaning and profitability.
Then the author shows that incumbents allocate resources according to the demands and margins of the network they already serve.
Then the author explains that disruptive entrants win because they fit another network first and only later invade the incumbent's home ground.

Required Anchors:
- The value-network concept itself is the chapter's main anchor; the friction is that the same product can look weak in one network and attractive in another, the consequence is different innovation choices.
- Incumbents prioritize projects that satisfy profitable current customers; the friction is resource allocation shaped by existing market rules, the consequence is rejection of low-end disruptions.
- Disruptive technologies begin where established performance measures matter less; the friction is mismatch with incumbent expectations, the consequence is late recognition.
