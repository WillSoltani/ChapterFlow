Core Claim:
Chapter 3 tests whether the disruption pattern from disk drives survives outside the original case. Christensen uses the mechanical excavator industry to show that hydraulic excavators gained traction in a different value network before moving upward toward the mainstream market long dominated by cable machines. The point is not that incumbents never responded. It is that their native market and standards made the new technology hard to commercialize on the terms that mattered first. The chapter strengthens the theory by showing the same structure in another industry.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
This chapter is the theory's stress test. Its identity depends on the cable-versus-hydraulic transition and the question of whether disruption is repeating or collapsing outside disk drives.

Author Logic Chain:
First the author shifts into the excavator industry to test the theory in a new setting.
Then the author shows that hydraulic technology found value in a smaller network with different needs and standards.
Then the author concludes that incumbents can enter the new technology and still fail if they keep trying to commercialize it through the old network.

Required Anchors:
- Cable-actuated excavators dominate the established market; the friction is that the old system fits the mainstream job well, the consequence is incumbent confidence.
- Hydraulic excavators gain foothold in smaller applications such as residential sewage construction; the friction is weak fit for mainstream buyers, the consequence is protection for the entrant's learning.
- Incumbents try hybrids or responses inside their native network; the friction is commercialization through the wrong standards, the consequence is strategic lateness.
