Core Claim:
Chapter 4 asks why leading firms can migrate upward into more demanding, profitable markets so easily while struggling to move downward into the low-end markets where disruptive technologies first matter. Christensen argues that the asymmetry is structural. Upmarket moves promise better margins and fit incumbent cost structures, while downmarket moves look small, poorly defined, and financially unattractive. The steel minimill example sharpens the point: entrants can use neglected low-end segments as footholds precisely because incumbents are happy to leave them behind.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
This chapter is about asymmetry, not just disruption in general. Its identity comes from the upmarket-versus-downmarket mobility problem and the minimill foothold in rebar.

Author Logic Chain:
First the author asks why firms move upmarket readily but resist moving downmarket.
Then the author shows that profit logic, customer migration, and cost structures make low-end markets hard to justify.
Then the author uses minimills to show how the abandoned low-end becomes the entrant's ladder upward.

Required Anchors:
- Upmarket movement is attractive because margins and financial logic reward it; the friction is that it looks responsible, the consequence is structural neglect of the low end.
- Downmarket entry looks small, vague, and low-profit; the friction is weak business justification, the consequence is incumbent reluctance.
- Minimills begin in low-value rebar and climb upward; the friction is low-end foothold and cost-structure advantage, the consequence is an entrant path into the mainstream.
