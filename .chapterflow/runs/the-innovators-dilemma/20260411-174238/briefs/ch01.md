Core Claim:
Christensen opens with a paradox, not a slogan. Leading firms in the disk-drive industry often failed even while they listened to customers, improved products, and followed the logic that had made them successful. The chapter argues that strong management can become the trap when new technologies first look too small or too weak for the incumbent's current market. The lesson is not that customers are useless. It is that customer-led improvement can keep a company climbing the wrong hill.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
This chapter is inseparable from the disk-drive industry and the pattern of leadership handoffs across generations of drives. Its force comes from showing that failure can emerge from rational, disciplined decisions rather than obvious incompetence.

Author Logic Chain:
First the author uses the hard disk drive industry as a compressed laboratory for watching technological generations overturn one another.
Then the author shows that incumbents often excel at sustaining innovations while missing disruptive ones that begin in smaller or weaker markets.
Then the author concludes that good management and close customer listening can actively delay the moves a firm needs to survive disruption.

Required Anchors:
- The disk-drive industry acts as the core laboratory because rapid product generations make leadership turnover visible; the friction is repeated technological transitions, the consequence is recurring incumbent failure.
- Established firms keep serving their best customers with sustaining improvements; the friction is rational resource allocation inside the current business, the consequence is blindness to weaker-looking entrants.
- Disruptive technologies begin in fringe or lower-end markets and move upward over time; the friction is early unattractiveness, the consequence is a late competitive shock.

Frameworks or Terms Introduced:
- sustaining innovation: improvement that matters inside the incumbent's current market and customer logic
- disruptive innovation: initially weaker-seeming approach that grows in neglected markets before moving upward
- incumbent trap: success logic that rewards the wrong response to an emerging disruption

Specific Applications:
- testing whether a product only helps current best customers
- checking whether low-end or fringe adoption is being dismissed too early
- separating sound current-business logic from long-term survival logic

Common Misreadings:

- disruptive entrants win only because incumbents ignore customers

Counterarguments or Limits:
