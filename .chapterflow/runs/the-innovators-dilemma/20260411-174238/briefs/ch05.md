Core Claim:
Once disruptive change is understood as structural, the management task changes. The problem is not to force a disruptive effort to satisfy the incumbent business immediately. The problem is to manage it in a way that lets it find a real market, learn under live conditions, and avoid being killed by the old business's standards before it can become viable.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
This chapter is about the managerial pivot from explanation to response. Its identity is the move from "why incumbents fail" to "what kind of management approach can keep the old business from misgrading the new one."

Author Logic Chain:
First the author closes Part 1's diagnosis by showing that ordinary incumbent logic will keep misjudging disruptive efforts.
Then the author reframes management as protecting learning rather than demanding instant proof under the old business model.
Then the author points toward Part 2's responses: organization design, market-size fit, discovery, and capability appraisal.

Required Anchors:
- The old business's standards are poorly matched to the early disruptive market; the friction is incumbent proof demands, the consequence is premature rejection.
- Managing disruption means creating room for market learning rather than demanding deterministic certainty too early; the friction is ambiguity, the consequence is a different management posture.
- Part 2 will unpack the response into separate organizational choices; the friction is not solved by slogans, the consequence is structured managerial design.
