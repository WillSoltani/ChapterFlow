Core Claim:
If a disruptive technology is managed inside an organization whose existing customers do not need it, that organization will rationally underfund, delay, or misgrade the effort. Responsibility should sit with a unit whose customers actually want the disruptive offering, because customer demand determines what the organization can seriously prioritize.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
This chapter is about organizational placement through customer fit. Its identity is the rule that the right home for disruptive work is not the incumbent unit by default, but the unit whose customers create a real reason to care.

Author Logic Chain:
First the author extends Chapter 5's proof-mismatch problem into an organizational problem.
Then the author argues that customer demand shapes priorities more strongly than executive intention alone.
Then the author points toward autonomous or separate responsibility as a way to keep disruptive work from being judged entirely inside the old customer logic.

Required Anchors:
- Existing customers of the incumbent unit may not need the disruptive technology; the friction is absent demand, the consequence is weak organizational commitment.
- Organizations respond to customer pull, revenue logic, and priority systems; the friction is structural misfit, the consequence is rational neglect.
- Responsibility should be placed where the disruptive technology has a live constituency; the friction is organizational separation, the consequence is a fairer chance to grow.
