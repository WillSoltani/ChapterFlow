Someone describes a moral problem as if the complaint should be obvious to everyone in the room. The unfairness is clear, the harm is plain, and the discussion feels finished before it starts. Then another person reacts as if the real issue is respect, duty, or contamination of a shared standard, and the first speaker suddenly treats that reaction as confusion rather than morality. Chapter 5 opens the door at exactly that moment.

Haidt's point is that many modern readers carry a narrow moral picture without realizing it. The book calls that narrowing WEIRD: a moral outlook shaped by Western, educated, industrialized, rich, democratic settings. Inside that frame, harm and fairness feel so central that they begin to look like morality itself. Chapter 5 argues that this confidence is culturally narrow, not universal.

That correction matters because a narrow moral lens can misread disagreement before the disagreement is even understood. If one group assumes morality is mainly about protecting individuals from harm and securing fair treatment, then concerns about loyalty, authority, respect, or purity-like boundaries can look irrational, primitive, or merely political. The chapter says that reaction is too fast. Many people moralize more than harm and fairness, and they do so sincerely rather than by accident.

The point is not that harm and fairness stop mattering. It is that they do not exhaust the field. Chapter 5 widens the map. It asks readers to stop treating one culturally familiar cluster of concerns as the whole moral territory. Once that widening begins, a lot of conflict looks different. What first sounded like ignorance may instead be a dispute about which moral concern deserves the center of attention.

This is where the chapter has to stay careful. Widening the map is not the same thing as declaring every norm equally wise or every tradition equally admirable. Haidt is not offering a lazy relativism in which all criticism dissolves. He is making a descriptive correction first. Before people decide what should count morally, they need a better account of what often does count morally across different communities and settings. Chapter 5 is about improving description before rushing to verdict.

That is why the chapter also resists caricature. It should not be read as a broad attack on modern liberal morality, nor as a romantic celebration of whatever falls outside it. The sharper claim is that WEIRD populations often universalize their own priorities too quickly. When that happens, they mistake a local moral emphasis for the full architecture of human moral life. Chapter 5 breaks that habit by widening the field.

Placed after the chapter on public persuasion, this move lands with force. Chapter 4 showed that arguments change shape with audiences and alliances. Chapter 5 asks a deeper question: why do different audiences treat different moral concerns as salient in the first place? That is the beginning of Part II. The chapter widens the moral map enough for the next step, which is to make those multiple concerns more concrete and memorable. Chapter 6 will do that by giving the reader a taste-buds style framework for why different moral triggers matter so differently to different people.
