People can be calculating, selfish, and status-seeking, yet they can also become loyal, sacrificial, and deeply cooperative inside a group. That double fact is the puzzle Chapter 9 reopens. Haidt asks why human beings are so groupish in addition to being self-interested, and he argues that morality cannot be understood only as a set of private judgments inside isolated minds. It also has to be understood as something that helps groups bind together.

The chapter matters because the previous political chapters could still tempt readers to think in terms of competing individuals or competing ideologies alone. Chapter 9 widens the frame again. Human beings do not merely reason, persuade, and vote. They also join, cohere, defend, and sometimes lose themselves inside collective life. Haidt uses the tension between selfishness and groupishness to explain why moral life can be both socially binding and socially dangerous.

This is where multilevel selection enters the chapter as a framework. The idea is not that people stop being self-interested. The idea is that selection pressure can make sense of a creature that competes within groups yet also helps groups compete against other groups. That framing gives readers a way to think about why cooperation, loyalty, and discipline can become powerful without pretending that all collective behavior is automatically noble.

Once readers see the problem this way, morality looks different. It is not only a matter of protecting individuals from harm or making fair judgments between persons. It is also part of the equipment by which groups stabilize themselves, reward insiders, punish defectors, and maintain shared purpose. That does not make every cohesive group good. It explains why moral systems can feel both elevating from the inside and narrowing from the outside.

The chapter therefore has to preserve a difficult tension. Group binding can create trust, sacrifice, coordination, and meaning, but it can also produce blindness toward outsiders and overconfidence about the group's own virtue. Haidt is not asking readers to romanticize cohesion. He is asking them to see that morality helps solve group-level problems as well as individual-level ones. The same force that binds can also blind.

Placed here in the book, Chapter 9 turns the argument toward a deeper account of social life. After explaining different moral foundations and political asymmetries, Haidt now asks why human beings are capable of group cohesion in the first place. That question prepares the next chapter, where the book will explain the hive switch and the conditions under which people can move from ordinary self-focus into a more collective mode.

Chapter 9 is therefore a pivot chapter in the strongest sense. It does not leave morality behind. It relocates morality inside the tension between selfishness and shared life. Readers come away with a more unsettling but more realistic picture: people are not just rational individuals or partisan signalers. They are creatures built for both competition and cohesion, and moral life makes much more sense once both sides of that design are kept in view.
