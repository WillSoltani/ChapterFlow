Two people watch the same event and come away morally struck by different parts of it. One cannot get past the unfairness. Another cannot get past the disrespect. A third keeps reacting to what feels contaminating or disloyal. The scene can look chaotic until Chapter 6 offers a better way to hold it. Haidt says the moral mind may be less like a single sensor and more like a set of taste buds: several kinds of moral trigger, each capable of lighting up under the right conditions.

The metaphor matters because it turns Chapter 5's widening move into something readers can actually use. The earlier chapter argued that harm and fairness are not the whole of morality. Chapter 6 makes that claim concrete. If morality has several taste receptors, then disagreement does not always mean one side is blind or evil. It can mean that different people are especially responsive to different moral cues in the same situation.

That does not make the metaphor literal biology. Haidt is not saying morality works exactly like the tongue, nor is he pretending a neat scientific diagram settles every dispute. The point is explanatory. Taste buds give readers a way to picture plurality without collapsing back into a single favored concern. The metaphor helps explain why several moral concerns can coexist, why one can feel especially salient in one setting, and why another can dominate in the next.

Once that framework is in place, a lot of disagreement becomes more legible. A workplace conflict can look mainly unfair to one person and mainly disrespectful to another. A family dispute can center care for one relative and duty for another. A classroom debate can split because one student hears the vulnerable party while another hears a broken standard. Chapter 6 does not say all these reactions are equally wise. It says they can all be morally structured rather than random.

This is where the chapter earns its place in the book. After Chapter 5 corrected a WEIRD narrowing, Chapter 6 gives readers a model of multiple moral saliences. The question stops being whether people care morally at all and becomes which moral trigger is strongest for them here. That is a sharper and more useful question. It widens interpretation before politics arrives.

The chapter still has to resist overreach. If readers turn the taste-buds metaphor into strict biology, they will mistake a heuristic for a laboratory result. If they treat the framework as a final moral verdict, they will ask it to do a job it was not built to do. Chapter 6 is organizing perception, not ending argument. It helps readers see the field of moral concerns more clearly so later judgments and disputes can be read with greater precision.

That precision matters because the next chapters will move into politics. Once readers understand that several moral triggers can be live at once, it becomes easier to ask why different groups reliably respond to different ones. Chapter 6 is therefore not the political chapter itself. It is the conceptual bridge that makes later political disagreement intelligible without reducing it to ignorance, bad faith, or a single moral axis. The framework widens the map and gives the reader a way to carry it.
