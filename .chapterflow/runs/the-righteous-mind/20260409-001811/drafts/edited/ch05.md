Someone raises a moral complaint as if the answer should be obvious to everyone in the room. The unfairness is clear. The harm is plain. The discussion feels settled before it starts. Then another person reacts as if the real issue is respect, duty, loyalty, or some boundary that should not be crossed, and the first speaker treats that reaction as confusion instead of morality. Chapter 5 begins by challenging that reflex.

Haidt argues that many modern readers carry a narrow moral map without noticing it. The book calls that narrowing WEIRD: a moral outlook shaped by Western, educated, industrialized, rich, democratic settings. Inside that frame, harm and fairness feel so central that they begin to look like morality itself. Chapter 5 says that confidence is not universal. It is culturally specific.

That correction matters because a narrow moral lens can misread disagreement before the disagreement is even understood. If one group assumes morality is mainly about preventing harm and securing fairness, then concerns about loyalty, authority, respect, or purity-like boundaries can look irrational, backward, or merely tribal. The chapter says that dismissal is too quick. Many people moralize more than harm and fairness, and they do so sincerely rather than accidentally.

The point is not that harm and fairness stop mattering. The point is that they do not exhaust the field. Chapter 5 widens the map. It asks readers to stop treating one culturally familiar cluster of concerns as the whole moral territory. Once that widening begins, a lot of conflict starts looking different. What first sounded like ignorance may instead be a disagreement about which moral concern should carry the most weight.

This is where the chapter has to remain disciplined. Widening the map is not the same thing as saying every norm is equally good or every cultural pattern equally defensible. Haidt is not offering a blank check for relativism. He is making a descriptive correction first. Before readers decide what should count morally, they need a better account of what often does count morally across different communities and settings. Chapter 5 improves the description before it encourages judgment.

That is also why the chapter should not be flattened into an attack on modern liberal morality. Its sharper claim is that WEIRD populations often universalize their own priorities too quickly. When that happens, they mistake a local moral emphasis for the full architecture of human moral life. Chapter 5 breaks that habit. It widens the field without pretending every moral tradition deserves equal praise.

Placed after the chapter on public persuasion, the argument deepens the book's movement. Chapter 4 showed that arguments shift with audiences and alliances. Chapter 5 asks why different audiences find different moral concerns salient to begin with. That is the beginning of Part II. The chapter clears space for a broader moral framework, and Chapter 6 will make that widened field more concrete with a taste-buds metaphor that helps explain why different moral triggers matter so differently to different people.
