Two people can hear the same political argument and feel that entirely different moral stakes are on the line. One hears protection for the vulnerable. Another hears disrespect for institutions. A third hears betrayal of a shared group. The disagreement can look like a fight between morality and immorality until Chapter 7 applies the earlier framework to politics. Haidt's point is that political conflict becomes more intelligible once readers assume that different moral concerns can dominate different listeners.

That move matters because Chapter 6 was a framework chapter. It gave readers the taste-buds image and argued that several moral triggers can be live at once. Chapter 7 carries that image into ideological life. If people answer to different moral signals in ordinary disputes, then political disagreement should not be read as if one side alone has moral seriousness. It may reflect a patterned difference in which concerns feel most urgent.

The chapter's force comes from making politics morally legible without making it morally simple. A policy fight can be heard by one audience mainly through care and fairness, while another audience hears duty, loyalty, or respect for order. That does not mean every position is equally wise. It means that readers gain a better diagnostic tool if they ask which moral concern is firing first before they jump to explanations about stupidity or bad faith.

Once readers make that shift, a lot of partisan confusion looks less mysterious. A campus protest response, a workplace diversity statement, or a local ballot fight can divide because participants are not ranking the same moral stakes in the same order. One person thinks the obvious issue is protection from harm. Another thinks the obvious issue is respect for inherited norms or obligations. Chapter 7 says those collisions are structured rather than random.

The chapter therefore asks for a change in interpretation more than a truce. Explaining a political opponent is not the same as excusing that opponent. Haidt is not arguing that all moral emphases are equally defensible in every case. He is arguing that readers will misread politics if they assume their own favored concern is the whole of morality. Diagnosis has to widen before judgment can sharpen.

That is why the chapter sits where it does. Chapter 5 widened morality beyond a WEIRD narrowing. Chapter 6 gave readers a model of multiple moral triggers. Chapter 7 now uses that model to explain why political camps so often talk past one another. The clash is not always between moral concern and moral emptiness. It is often between competing moral saliences that shape what each side hears as obvious.

The chapter still stops short of its next step. It begins mapping moral foundations into politics, but it does not yet make the sharper comparative claim that follows. Instead it prepares readers to understand why one political camp might sometimes speak across more moral registers than another. Chapter 7 is the diagnostic bridge. It moves from moral plurality to ideological conflict and makes the coming asymmetry argument possible.
