Core Claim:
Chapter 2 turns the pressure from Chapter 1 into a stronger operating claim: moral reasoning usually follows intuition rather than leading it. Haidt draws on Hume and the broader return of emotion and automaticity research to argue that reasoning often works as a servant of judgment, persuasion, and reputation defense. The chapter's force comes from reversing the flattering picture people hold of their own rational control.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
It is the chapter that most cleanly flips the hierarchy between intuition and reasoning and gives Part I its governing operational claim.

Author Logic Chain:
First the author reframes the old reason-versus-passion debate through Hume and related thinkers.
Then he shows why the common picture of reason in charge does not fit how moral judgment actually behaves.
Then he recasts reasoning as a socially useful servant that explains, persuades, and protects after intuition moves first.

Required Anchors:
- Hume as the cleanest philosophical support for passion or intuition leading
- the intuitive-dog image as the inversion of the usual reason-first picture
- reasoning as post-hoc justification and social persuasion rather than neutral discovery

Counterarguments or Limits:
The chapter lowers the prestige of reasoning, but it does not eliminate it. Reason still matters for persuasion, self-management, and later correction, even if it is rarely first on the scene.
