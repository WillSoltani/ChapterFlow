The next leadership failure in the book is less personal than ego and more structural than attitude. A group can have talented people, solid intentions, and even decent local discipline, yet still fail because each unit treats its own lane like a self-contained world. That is the problem this chapter targets. The issue is not whether every subgroup works hard. The issue is whether each subgroup understands that its work depends on the others and that the mission collapses when support across boundaries stops.

That matters because isolated excellence can still produce collective failure. One team can hit its numbers, protect its process, and stay proud of its standards while the larger mission slips. A support desk can answer tickets quickly while the product team keeps shipping changes that create the same customer pain. A logistics team can keep its own schedule tidy while the handoff point stays broken for everyone downstream. The chapter argues that local success is not enough when the mission is shared. If your team is performing in a way that leaves adjacent teams exposed, then your team is not actually succeeding.

The chapter's central principle is that teams have to cover and move for one another. The phrase points to mutual dependence, not vague friendliness. One element supports another because the mission requires it, not because support is a sentimental extra. The logic is practical: if one lane is pinned down, delayed, or overloaded, the other lanes cannot pretend they are unaffected. Every element's freedom to execute depends on the other elements staying usable. Once leaders see that clearly, they stop treating assistance as charity and start treating it as part of how the mission works.

That shift changes what leaders watch. A narrow leader asks whether their own team is working. A stronger leader asks where the next bottleneck is forming and what support would keep the whole operation moving. That could mean product leaders sitting with support to see what customers are actually fighting through. It could mean a lab subgroup changing its pace because another subgroup cannot finish the experiment without the earlier measurements arriving on time. It could mean a family or volunteer team covering the weak point of the day instead of defending the fairness of fixed roles while the shared job falls apart. The chapter keeps pushing toward the same conclusion: your lane is real, but it never exists alone.

That does not erase ownership. The chapter needs that limit to stay intact or else the principle goes soft. Cover and move is not endless rescue, permanent overfunctioning, or a permission slip for weak execution. Mutual support works only when people still own their responsibilities clearly enough to notice where support is needed and when it should stop. If everyone starts doing a little of everything without standards, then the team has traded silos for confusion. The chapter is arguing for aggressive interdependence with accountability, not for fuzzy responsibility where nobody can tell who is answerable for what.

This is why silo thinking is more dangerous than it first appears. It does not only slow cooperation. It distorts judgment. A siloed team starts protecting local metrics, local preferences, and local grievances even when the larger mission is plainly asking for adjustment. People begin speaking as if the problem belongs to someone else because the failure appears just outside their formal boundary. The chapter rejects that logic. If the adjacent team is failing in a way that will damage the mission, then that problem is already partly yours. Not because you own everything, but because shared missions create shared exposure.

The pattern transfers easily outside combat because almost every real effort contains interdependence. A campus publication staff can miss deadline quality when writers, editors, and layout treat each delay as someone else's problem. A household can spiral into repeated stress when one person protects a preferred routine while the visible weak point keeps getting ignored. A volunteer crew can resent each other for weeks because nobody wants to shift resources toward the part of the work that is currently overloaded. In each case, the team does not need more slogans about togetherness. It needs a sharper understanding that supporting the exposed point is often the most direct way to protect the mission.

That creates the harder question the chapter leaves behind. How do leaders build strong mutual support without letting the whole operation become complicated, overcoordinated, and hard to execute under pressure? The answer is not to retreat back into silos. It is to keep support tied to the mission, ownership, and the simplest workable picture of what each unit owes the others. That makes the bridge to the next chapter natural. Once teams accept that they must cover and move together, they still have to communicate and plan in ways that survive stress. Otherwise even well-intended interdependence can collapse under its own complexity.
