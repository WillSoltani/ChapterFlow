Core Claim:
The ego default distorts judgment when a person treats threats to status, self-worth, or identity as if they were threats to survival. The supported chapter lane is narrow: Parrish names ego as one of the four defaults, and the frozen bundle supports territory, hierarchy, self-preservation, and status threat as the upstream mechanics. The chapter should show how people stop asking what is true or useful once protecting self-image becomes the first priority.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
Chapter 2 was about emotional possession and hot-state reaction. Chapter 3 is about the quieter but equally damaging need to defend identity, rank, and competence. The chapter should feel status-sensitive, pride-sensitive, and defensiveness-sensitive rather than hot, bodily, or purely social.

Author Logic Chain:
First the author names ego as a default rather than a virtue.
Then he connects ego distortion to hierarchy, territory, and self-preservation pressures already established in Chapter 1.
Then he shows what happens when criticism, disagreement, correction, or loss of status gets treated like an attack on the self.
Then he shows the practical cost: people stop learning, over-defend weak positions, and damage judgment by protecting image.
Then he points forward to the strengths chapters, where self-accountability and self-knowledge begin repairing this weakness.

Frameworks or Terms Introduced:
Ego default: reacting to perceived threats to self-image, competence, territory, or rank.
Identity defense: protecting the story you want to believe about yourself even when it damages judgment.
Status threat: a moment where correction, disagreement, or comparison feels like a reduction in standing.
Defensiveness cost: the loss of learning, accuracy, and trust created by protecting image first.

Specific Applications:
- taking feedback as insult instead of usable information
- doubling down in meetings because being wrong feels too expensive
- making decisions to protect image rather than outcome
- confusing self-respect with refusal to revise

Common Misreadings:
- making ego sound identical to emotion
- turning the chapter into generic confidence advice
- treating ego only as arrogance when insecurity and over-defensiveness matter just as much

Counterarguments or Limits:
