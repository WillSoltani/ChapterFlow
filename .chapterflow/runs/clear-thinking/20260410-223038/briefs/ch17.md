Core Claim:
Defining the problem matters because better decisions fail early when the wrong problem gets framed, owned, or solved. The frozen support here is medium and the heading-index lane is clear: problem framing, obstacles, responsibility, and root cause. The chapter should therefore stay practical. It is not about abstract diagnosis theater. It is about asking what the real problem is, what obstacle is actually in the way, and who or what is responsible for changing it.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
Chapter 16 was about learning from a miss after it happens. Chapter 17 asks how that learning sharpens the next problem before action starts. Its texture should feel clarifying, diagnostic, and responsibility-aware rather than vague or overly analytical.

Frameworks or Terms Introduced:
Problem framing: defining what is actually wrong and what decision must really be made.
Obstacle: the real condition preventing progress instead of the most visible annoyance.
Responsibility: the part of the situation the person can actually own or influence.
Root cause: the underlying condition that keeps producing the visible symptom.

Specific Applications:
- separating the real decision from the most emotionally vivid complaint
- identifying the obstacle that keeps repeating under different surface events
- clarifying what is owned, what is influenced, and what is merely observed
- avoiding solution search before the real problem has been named

Common Misreadings:
- making root cause sound like infinite analysis
- framing the problem so broadly that action disappears
- confusing blame with responsibility

Counterarguments or Limits:
The chapter should not imply that every problem has one perfect hidden cause or that more analysis is always better. Keep it narrower and more practical: problem framing matters because weak framing sends effort toward the wrong obstacle. Do not invent formal strategy frameworks, diagnosis trees, or consulting models absent from the frozen bundle.
