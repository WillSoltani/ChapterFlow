People often imagine ego as swagger, vanity, or loud self-importance. Parrish's chapter works on a subtler and more dangerous level. The ego default appears whenever a threat to self-image starts defining the moment before judgment does. A correction becomes an insult. A disagreement becomes disrespect. A revision becomes humiliation. The person is no longer asking what is true, useful, or likely to work. They are asking what protects the version of themselves they most want to preserve. That is why ego can ruin decisions without looking theatrical. It often arrives dressed as dignity, standards, or justified resistance.

The earlier biology frame helps explain why this happens so fast. Territory, hierarchy, and self-preservation do not vanish because the setting is modern and polite. They still shape what the body treats as urgent. If criticism feels like a loss of standing, the body can answer as if status itself were under attack. That answer may not look explosive. It can sound calm, measured, and articulate while still being defensive at the core. A person explains too quickly, pushes back too hard, or rejects a useful correction because accepting it would feel like stepping down in rank. Ego makes image protection feel practical even when it is quietly pulling the person away from a better result.

That distortion changes the purpose of learning moments. Feedback is useful because it exposes something you could not yet see alone. The ego default turns that exposure into a threat. Once that happens, the goal shifts from understanding to self-protection. People defend weak ideas because being wrong feels too expensive. They interrupt before hearing the full point. They double down on positions they would have revised if no audience were present. They choose the line that preserves face rather than the one that improves reality. The cost is not only social friction. The deeper cost is that learning shuts down exactly when it is most needed.

This is why the chapter cannot be reduced to advice about humility in the abstract. The issue is not that confidence is bad or that self-respect should disappear. A stable sense of self is useful. Without it, people become fragile in other ways. The problem begins when self-respect gets tied to being uncorrected, unimproved, or undefeated. Then every disagreement starts carrying extra weight. The question is no longer whether the feedback is right. The question becomes what accepting it would mean about you. When that interpretation takes over, truth has to fight its way through identity before it can do any work.

The practical move is the same one supported earlier in the book: create space between stimulus and response. In this chapter that space matters because self-image is trying to answer first. A pause lets you ask a better question. What, exactly, is being threatened here: the outcome, or my picture of myself? That question does not erase the sting of correction, but it separates dignity from defensiveness. Dignity can survive being wrong. In fact, it often becomes more visible there. Defensiveness cannot survive that moment, so it starts arguing immediately. Clear thinking depends on learning which voice is talking.

That distinction also prepares the ground for the next default. Ego asks how the moment affects my image, rank, or competence. The social default asks how the moment affects my standing with the group. Those pressures overlap, but they are not identical. A person can stop defending the self and still surrender to the room. Parrish's sequence matters because it moves from private self-protection to public conformity. Before the crowd can do its work, the self usually tries to do its own. If you cannot spot that first move, independent judgment will keep arriving late.
