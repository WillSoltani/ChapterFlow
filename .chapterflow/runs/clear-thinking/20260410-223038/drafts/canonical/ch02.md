Sonny Corleone never had a thinking problem first. He had an emotional-speed problem. Parrish opens there because Sonny's weakness is not subtle. He feels, then acts. Vito, by contrast, can stay patient long enough for the situation to reveal itself. That contrast gives the chapter its first hard line: when emotion takes over the clock, judgment does not merely weaken. It often disappears. You are no longer deciding what serves the outcome. You are obeying the demand of the feeling that reached you first.

That is why Parrish says emotional mistakes look obvious in hindsight. The mistake was often visible all along, just not to the person inside the surge. Anger wants release. Fear wants immediate safety. Outrage wants payback. Urgency wants action before reflection. Each state pushes toward movement that feels justified because it promises relief right now. But the chapter keeps forcing the same question: does that move help you get what you want, or does it only satisfy the emotion that currently wants control?

The answer is usually brutal. Anger at a rival pushes you to say the thing that cannot be unsaid. Fear of missing an opportunity makes you commit before the facts settle. Outrage at criticism turns a useful correction into a fight about dignity. Parrish is not saying emotions are fake. He is saying they are dangerous rulers. The feeling can contain information, but once it starts governing action, it can turn every other advantage you built into wasted effort.

That is why the chapter carries the Steve story with so much force. One final conversation with his parents went badly. On their way home, they were hit head-on by a drunk driver. There would be no later apology, no repaired tone, no second chance to reframe the moment. Parrish uses the story carefully but decisively. Emotional reaction is not only embarrassing. Sometimes it becomes permanent. A single hot moment can multiply years of work, love, or discipline by zero if it controls the wrong scene at the wrong time.

The chapter gets stronger when it explains how ordinary this vulnerability is. Emotional hijack is not just about bad temperament. It gets easier when the body is already compromised. Hunger, fatigue, sleep deprivation, distraction, feeling rushed, stress, unfamiliar environments: these do not create wisdom tests you should feel proud to pass. They are hazard conditions. In those states, the emotion default needs less force to seize the wheel because your judgment is already running on less margin than you think.

That shift matters because it turns the chapter from moral lecture into operational warning. If you know a state makes you easier to hijack, then the problem is not only the feeling in the moment. The problem is letting yourself enter consequential situations while emotionally exposed and pretending your judgment is still clean. Parrish does not ask for emotional numbness. He asks for respect for the conditions under which emotion stops being data and starts becoming command.

That leaves the chapter with a live tension. Feeling is not worthless. Fear can warn you. Anger can point to violation. Grief can tell you what matters. But none of that means the first act under emotion deserves trust. The useful move is not to become colder. It is to become slower at the moment when heat tries to appoint itself ruler. Once emotion is in charge, all your reasoning tools arrive too late to do more than explain the wreckage.

So the chapter's lesson is harsher than "calm down." It is that emotional possession compresses time until the future disappears. You stop thinking about what the action creates and start thinking only about what the feeling demands. That is why the chapter belongs so early in the book. Before ego, before social pressure, before habit, Parrish wants the reader to see the first common hijacker clearly. If you cannot catch the surge before it spends your judgment, the rest of the system never gets a fair chance.
