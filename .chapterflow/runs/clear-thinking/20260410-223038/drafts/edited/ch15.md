Knowing your weaknesses is useful, but it is not enough by itself. A person can see where judgment repeatedly breaks down and still lose in the same place if nothing blocks the path back into that failure. That is why Protecting Yourself with Safeguards follows next. Parrish uses this chapter to move from diagnosis into protection. Clear thinking improves when the wiser move is supported by friction, guardrails, and advance protection instead of being left to outrun pressure every time.

The chapter should stay narrower than generic planning advice. The heading index gives it a practical lane: rules, guardrails, premortem, and friction. That means a safeguard is not fearfulness and it is not distrust of the self. It is structure built around a known weak point so that a bad path becomes harder to enter before the live moment speeds up. A guardrail matters because it keeps one avoidable mistake from having to be resisted from scratch each time it reappears.

That is why friction matters. People often assume the answer to weakness is more willpower, but willpower usually arrives inside the same pressured environment that caused the failure in the first place. A small pause, barrier, checklist, second review, or extra step can do something willpower often cannot: it can slow the weak move long enough for better judgment to return. The problem is not always ignorance. Often it is that the person knows the better move and still reaches it too late.

Premortem thinking makes the protection sharper. A person asks how the plan, conversation, purchase, handoff, or commitment is most likely to go wrong before it does. The chapter becomes practical when safeguards are tied to known exposure instead of being spread everywhere in a vague attempt at control. That shift matters because it keeps protection proportionate. The right safeguard is not the one that makes life most rigid. It is the one that makes the known weak path less easy to repeat.

That tension matters too. Safeguards should not sound like panic, bureaucracy, or self-imprisonment. Parrish's chapter should not imply that mature judgment means living behind walls. The point is not to eliminate flexibility. The point is to stop pretending that awareness alone is enough where failure is already predictable. Protection is wise when it is built around real exposure and kept light enough that judgment still has room to work.

The bridge into mistakes follows naturally from there. Even good safeguards do not hold every line forever. Something still slips through, some failure still gets made, and the next question becomes how to respond well once the miss is real. Protecting Yourself with Safeguards explains how to reduce avoidable failure before it happens. How to Handle Mistakes asks what disciplined recovery looks like when protection still proves incomplete.
