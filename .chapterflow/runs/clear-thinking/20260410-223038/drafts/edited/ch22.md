Protecting action with a margin of safety makes the decision more durable, but durability alone does not guarantee improvement. A person can act, survive uncertainty, and still fail to learn anything useful if the result is never examined carefully. That is why Learn from Your Decisions follows next. Parrish uses this chapter to move from protected action into reflection. Clear thinking improves when the reader stops assuming that experience teaches automatically and starts making the process and outcome legible enough to guide the next decision.

The chapter should stay practical rather than sounding like self-help reflection language. The heading index gives it a narrow lane: feedback, postmortem, reflection, and improvement. That means learning here is not rumination and it is not self-criticism for its own sake. It is the work of understanding what happened, what part of the process held or failed, and what reality is actually saying back to the decision that was made.

That is why experience alone is not enough. People often imagine that simply living through an outcome will make them wiser. The chapter pushes against that assumption. Experience is noisy. A good process can still meet a bad result, and a weak process can still get rescued by luck. Reflection matters because it helps separate process from outcome instead of letting the visible result become the whole lesson.

Postmortem thinking makes that separation more useful. The chapter should ask the reader to look back in a structured way: what was the decision, what information was available, what assumptions were made, what happened, and what part of the result belongs to judgment versus noise. Feedback becomes useful when it is treated as information rather than as praise, blame, or an excuse to rewrite the past.

Improvement matters because a lesson only counts if it changes something later. The point is not to admire the review or to punish the self. It is to carry a clearer understanding into the next decision. Parrish is not promising that every outcome yields a neat lesson. He is arguing that without reflection, even rich experience can remain wasted.

The bridge into the late-book shift follows naturally from there. Once a person can think more clearly, act with protection, and learn from the results, the next question is no longer only how to decide. It becomes what kind of outcomes are worth wanting in the first place.
