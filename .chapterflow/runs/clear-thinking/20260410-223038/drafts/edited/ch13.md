Setting a standard solves only part of the problem. A wiser rule can be perfectly clear on paper and still feel weak when pressure arrives. That is why Exemplars + Practice follows next. Parrish uses this chapter to show that standards become more usable when a person can see them embodied and rehearse them deliberately. A rule matters more when it stops being only a sentence you agree with and starts becoming something you can recognize, trust, and repeat.

The chapter should stay narrower than generic self-improvement talk. The heading index gives it a practical lane: role models, practice, calibration, and measurement. That means exemplars are not celebrities to admire from a distance. They are visible models that calibrate what better judgment looks like in motion. A person often thinks they understand a standard until they watch someone apply it with steadiness, timing, restraint, or clarity that exposes the gap between vague approval and usable understanding.

That is why exemplars matter. Good models make judgment concrete. They show what the standard sounds like in conversation, what it looks like in timing, and how it survives ordinary pressure without becoming rigid or theatrical. The value is not imitation for its own sake. The value is calibration. A person can stop guessing what stronger judgment looks like because they have seen a more disciplined version of it carried by someone else in lived form.

Practice matters for the same reason. Seeing the line is not the same as being able to use it. A person may know the right response and still fail to reach it quickly when the room gets tense, the deadline gets close, or an old habit starts pulling. Rehearsal narrows that gap. Repeated attempts make the standard easier to access under ordinary pressure because the person is no longer asking the live moment to build the behavior from zero.

Measurement belongs here too, but only in a narrow way. Parrish's chapter should not turn into vanity scoring or self-optimization theater. The point of feedback is calibration. A simple tally, review, or observed result can show whether practice is making the standard more usable or whether the person is still flattering themselves with theory. That is also why exemplars should not become hero worship. Copying a model blindly can be as distortive as refusing to learn from one. The chapter works best when models, practice, and feedback sharpen independent judgment rather than replace it.

The bridge into weaknesses follows naturally from there. Even a practiced standard still fails somewhere. A person can admire the right model, rehearse the better response, and still break down in certain exposures they do not yet understand. Exemplars + Practice explains how standards become steadier through reinforcement. Knowing Your Weaknesses asks where that reinforcement still fails and what those failures reveal.
