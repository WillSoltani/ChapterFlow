Problems and pursuits look different once time stops feeling abstract. A conflict that seemed consuming can shrink. A delay that felt harmless can start to look expensive. Parrish turns to Memento Mori here because hindsight and regret can still remain too soft until mortality enters the frame. When life is experienced as finite rather than endless, judgment changes. The issue is not to become gloomy. It is to see more clearly what deserves the life that is being spent.

Death awareness restores proportion. Much of what feels urgent borrows its force from forgetfulness about finitude. Petty status contests, noisy demands, and reflexive busyness grow large when time is treated as replenishable. Mortality interrupts that inflation. It asks whether the thing in front of the reader actually deserves the hours, attention, and emotional energy it is taking. Some concerns survive that question. Many do not.

That is why mortality sharpens clarity. If time is limited, then choice is not a casual exercise. What receives commitment, delay, sacrifice, or avoidance starts to reveal itself more honestly. Clarity here does not mean dramatic certainty. It means cleaner proportion. Real urgency becomes easier to distinguish from frantic immediacy. A meaningful obligation may remain urgent under mortality. Much noise turns out to be loud rather than important.

This pressure also shrinks triviality. A person can spend enormous energy protecting image, winning minor conflicts, or feeding routines that only look necessary because they are familiar. Death awareness does not erase responsibility, but it does expose when a pursuit is consuming life without earning that cost. The chapter should make clear that the question is not whether something matters at all. It is whether it matters enough to justify finite life.

The chapter still needs a firm boundary against melodrama, fatalism, and doctrine drift. Parrish is not telling the reader to romanticize death, perform stoic posture, or panic into constant action. Mortality should sharpen judgment, not distort it. Urgency is not hustle. Perspective is not despair. The point is to let finitude strip false size away from what was only pretending to deserve so much life.

That prepares the final turn. Once mortality has clarified proportion and urgency, the last question is broader and calmer: what does death teach about living well? Memento Mori narrows the field. The closing chapter asks what kind of life remains worthy once that narrowing has been done.
