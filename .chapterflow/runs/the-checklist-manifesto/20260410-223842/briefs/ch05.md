Core Claim:
The chapter follows the first serious attempt to build and use a surgical checklist and shows that a promising idea still fails on first contact unless it is revised for real human use. Gawande's point is not that the initial checklist was worthless. It is that practical adoption depends on iteration, local credibility, and visible adjustment when the first version feels awkward, excessive, or out of rhythm with real work. The chapter turns the checklist from a design principle into an implementation problem.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
Unlike Chapter 4, which explains the idea in structural terms, Chapter 5 shows the first real attempt to make that idea usable. Its pressure is procedural and social: good intentions are not enough, and early versions must survive resistance, revision, and the practical intelligence of working professionals.

Author Logic Chain:
First the author moves from the abstract checklist idea into an early pilot effort.
Then the author shows that the first version meets resistance because real professionals experience friction, awkward timing, and doubts about fit.
Then the author shows that revision and refinement are part of the method, not evidence that the checklist idea failed.

Frameworks or Terms Introduced:
- first-version friction: the resistance that appears when a tool meets real workflow
- implementation credibility: trust earned when a checklist proves it respects the room's actual constraints
- iterative refinement: revising wording, timing, and scope so a checklist becomes usable rather than merely admirable

Specific Applications:
- surgical teams testing a new pause-and-check routine
- project teams piloting a coordination tool that initially feels clumsy
- any high-skill setting where rollout depends on revision rather than decree
