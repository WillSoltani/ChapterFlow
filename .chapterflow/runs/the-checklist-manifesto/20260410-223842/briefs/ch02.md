Core Claim:
Checklists work in complex environments because they protect against two ordinary but dangerous human failures: forgetting routine critical steps and choosing to skip them when pressure makes them seem optional. Gawande uses the B-17 story and Pronovost's central-line work to show that expertise does not remove those risks. A good checklist does not replace skill. It catches the minimum necessary actions that are easiest to lose under strain. The chapter therefore turns the book from diagnosis toward a concrete tool.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
This is the book's direct argument for checklists as a practical response to predictable omission. Unlike Chapter 1, it is less about systemic complexity in the abstract and more about why a short list can outperform unaided memory in a high-skill setting.

Author Logic Chain:
First the author shows that even elite performers can miss obvious steps in a complicated process.
Then the author identifies memory lapse and deliberate skipping as the two recurring failure modes.
Then the author shows that a short, well-targeted checklist can protect against those misses and save lives.

Required Anchors:
- the B-17 crash / flight-test story that shows one forgotten step defeating a sophisticated machine and expert pilot
- Peter Pronovost's central-line checklist in Michigan ICUs
- the Keystone Initiative results showing that a short checklist plus disciplined use sharply reduced infections and saved lives

Counterarguments or Limits:
The chapter should not oversell the checklist as magic. It works when the steps are few, vital, and usable in live conditions. Keep the chapter from drifting into later checklist-design doctrine or global rollout evidence that belongs downstream.
