Chapter 5 begins where Chapter 4 leaves off: with a good design idea meeting real professional use for the first time. If a checklist is supposed to support distributed judgment without swallowing it, then the first serious question is not whether the theory sounds clean. It is whether actual teams can use the thing without feeling slowed, insulted, or trapped inside somebody else's abstraction. Gawande turns to an early WHO surgical-checklist pilot to show that implementation starts in friction, not in applause.

That friction matters because the first version of a tool is rarely the real version. A checklist can look persuasive on paper and still feel awkward once it enters a live workflow. Timing may be wrong. Wording may sound unnatural. A step that seems obviously useful in design may feel clumsy in the room. The chapter's first practical lesson is that rollout exposes problems that argument alone cannot see.

This is where resistance changes meaning. Gawande does not present pushback as proof that professionals are too proud to improve. The resistance matters because it can reveal where the checklist fails to match the pace, sequence, or social reality of the work. A room that hesitates may be defending ego, but it may also be noticing that the draft tool is too long, badly timed, or out of rhythm with the job it claims to help.

That is why revision becomes part of the method rather than a sign of embarrassment. The first try is useful precisely because it gives designers something honest to react to. Once people see where the checklist interrupts flow, creates confusion, or feels performative, they can start trimming, retiming, and clarifying. The point is not to abandon the disciplined pause. The point is to shape that pause so professionals can actually carry it without losing the reason it exists.

The chapter therefore widens the meaning of credibility. A checklist does not gain authority because someone high-status announced it. It gains authority when the people using it can see that it respects the work's real constraints while still protecting a few essential checks. Credibility grows when the tool becomes usable enough to survive contact with the room and sharp enough to keep its core purpose alive.

That is what makes Chapter 5 more than a story about an awkward beginning. It shows that implementation is not a final step after design. It is where design becomes real. A checklist worthy of serious professionals has to be revised in public, under pressure, until it is short enough to live inside the workflow and sturdy enough to improve it. That is the bridge to the next chapter, where the book asks what good checklist builders actually have to learn.
