Chapter Promise:
Explain why strong checklists in complex systems decentralize action while tightening communication.

Opening Move:
Open on the contrast between trapped central control and fast local action so the reader feels the design problem before the checklist answer appears.

Anchor Allocation:
- anchor 1 used in paragraph(s): P1-P2
- anchor 2 used in paragraph(s): P2-P4
- anchor 3 used in paragraph(s): P4-P6

Paragraph Job Map:
- P1: frame the central problem of large systems that wait on the center
- P2: use Katrina as the image of breakdown under centralized response
- P3: use Wal-Mart as the image of decentralized local action
- P4: explain why local action only works when communication stays tight
- P5: define the checklist idea as a coordination device rather than a command script
- P6: bridge to practical early checklist building in the next chapter

Required Distinction or Mechanism:
The chapter must distinguish decentralization from chaos. Its mechanism is local authority plus shared communication checks, not local freedom by itself.

Hard-Depth Tension:
Keep alive the danger that decentralized systems can drift unless communication is structured and visible.

Threshold Question:
How do you move authority outward without losing the alignment that keeps many separate actions from pulling apart?

Takeaway Count Lock:
- Easy: 3 (always)
- Medium: 5
- Hard: 5

Hard Takeaway Topics:
- Takeaway 1: centralized response can fail when field conditions change faster than the center can react
- Takeaway 2: decentralization works only when local actors share information in disciplined ways
- Takeaway 3: Katrina and Wal-Mart form a structural contrast, not just a moral contrast
- Takeaway 4: a good checklist coordinates communication rather than replacing judgment
- Takeaway 5: the design challenge is distributing action without inviting drift

Scenario Lesson Map:
- Scenario 1 (inner_monologue, work): teaches how a field lead notices that waiting for central permission is slowing action
- Scenario 2 (contrast, school): teaches how students compare centralized and decentralized response models
- Scenario 3 (dialogue, personal): teaches why a household emergency plan needs both local initiative and quick updates
- Scenario 4 (decision_point, work): teaches how to add a shared communication check instead of a long control script
- Scenario 5 (dilemma, school): teaches why decentralization without communication can still fail
- Scenario 6 (vignette, personal): teaches how a simple family checklist can coordinate fast action without trapping every decision at the center

Scenario Vividness Targets:
- Scenario 1: supply room, radio chatter, and delayed approval
- Scenario 2: classroom comparison board with Katrina and Wal-Mart columns
- Scenario 4: operations desk, short status board, and local team updates
- Scenario 6: storm prep checklist taped near a doorway

Preview Constraint:
The preview must point toward practical checklist building, not just repeat the abstract design idea.

Genericity Risks:
- turning the chapter into generic decentralization praise
- losing the checklist role and sliding into public-management talk
- presenting Wal-Mart as magic rather than as a coordination model

Forbidden Drift:
- filler move 1: generic language about empowerment
- filler move 2: vague praise of communication with no mechanism
- invented authority move 1: unsupported Katrina logistics specifics
- brief-language leak 1: threshold question
