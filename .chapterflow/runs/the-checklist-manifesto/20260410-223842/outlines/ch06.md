Chapter Promise:
Show what skilled checklist builders learn about making lists short, well timed, and usable without stripping away what matters.

Opening Move:
Open on the danger of a bloated or mistimed checklist so the reader feels that design quality matters as much as checklist intent.

Anchor Allocation:
- anchor 1 used in paragraph(s): P1-P2
- anchor 2 used in paragraph(s): P2-P4
- anchor 3 used in paragraph(s): P4-P6

Paragraph Job Map:
- P1: frame the difference between a checklist idea and checklist craft
- P2: bring in Dan Boorman and aviation as the design authority
- P3: show how Boeing manuals and simulator learning teach brevity and timing
- P4: distinguish good lists from bad lists
- P5: explain why a checklist must catch likely failure points without bloating the workflow
- P6: bridge to WHO testing and validation in the next chapter

Required Distinction or Mechanism:
The chapter must distinguish checklist completeness from checklist usefulness. Its mechanism is design discipline around brevity, timing, and failure-point selection.

Hard-Depth Tension:
Keep alive the danger that cutting too much weakens the tool while adding too much makes it unusable.

Threshold Question:
How do you decide what belongs on a checklist when every extra item increases drag and every missing item increases risk?

Takeaway Count Lock:
- Easy: 3 (always)
- Medium: 5
- Hard: 5

Hard Takeaway Topics:
- Takeaway 1: good checklist design is a form of craft rather than a random brainstorm
- Takeaway 2: Dan Boorman and aviation show why timing and brevity matter
- Takeaway 3: simulator learning helps reveal whether a list supports or disrupts execution
- Takeaway 4: good lists are focused while bad lists are bloated or vague
- Takeaway 5: design means choosing what failure points are important enough to deserve a place on the list
