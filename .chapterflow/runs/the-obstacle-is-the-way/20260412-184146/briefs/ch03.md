Core Claim:
The chapter argues that emotions become dangerous when they are allowed to run the reading of events and the selection of responses. Its job is to show that control means governing reaction, not becoming numb. The chapter should keep pressure visible and show how unchecked anger, fear, or wounded pride can enlarge an obstacle and spoil the next move. It should stay distinct from Chapter 2 by widening from the first surge to the broader management of emotional response.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
Chapter 2 focused on the opening spike in the nerves. Chapter 3 moves beyond the first spike to the larger discipline of not letting emotion become command. It is not yet objectivity as a whole chapter and not yet tactical action.

Author Logic Chain:
First the author shows that feelings are real but make poor rulers.
Then the author shows how unmanaged emotion distorts judgment and decision quality.
Then the author concludes that control means keeping feelings from owning the response.

Frameworks or Terms Introduced:
- emotional control: governing reaction without denying feeling
- reaction ownership: refusing to let anger, fear, or pride decide the next move

Specific Applications:
- not answering from anger
- not letting shame or fear write the whole meaning of a setback
- keeping the next move aligned with judgment instead of mood

Common Misreadings:

- treating strong feeling as permission for bad judgment

Counterarguments or Limits:
The chapter should acknowledge that emotions carry information. The error is not feeling them. The error is yielding control of interpretation and response to them.
