Chapter: 1 — Sort Out the Three Conversations

Core concepts to test:
- The three conversations as a diagnostic structure (what happened / feelings / identity)
- The identity conversation as the quietest, most powerful, and most often invisible layer
- The failure mode of preparing for / fighting the "what happened" conversation when the identity conversation is doing the real damage
- Suppressed feelings distorting the other two conversations
- The diagnostic vs. prescriptive limit of this chapter (it names the problem, does not fix it)

Bloom ceiling:
- Highest justified level for this chapter: analyze (some evaluate)
- Ch1 is diagnostic — it builds recognition and application, not prescriptive skill. Deeper questions should test whether readers can identify the right layer in a scenario, not what to do about it.

Question plan:
- q01: Basic orientation — what are the three conversations? (remember/understand)
- q02: Identity conversation comprehension — what makes it distinct from the other two? (understand)
- q03: Application — which conversation is being fought in a described scenario? (apply)
- q04: Named scenario (Naomi/Derek) — what layer is doing the most damage? (apply)
- q05: Distinction / trap — feelings vs. identity conversation confusion (analyze)
- q06: Limit / misreading — naming the three conversations is NOT itself a solution (understand/analyze)
- q07: Transfer — recognize the identity conversation in a personal/family context (apply/analyze)
- q08: Scenario judgment (Theo/dilemma) — which conversation is making a decision hard? (analyze)
- q09: Synthesis — what does it mean that difficult conversations almost always involve all three simultaneously? (evaluate)
- q10: Previous chapter / threshold connection — Ch1 is chapter 1, so q10 should connect to the book's broader claim and hold the threshold question: what is the limitation of the three-conversation diagnostic alone? (evaluate/create)

Distractor notes:
- Tempting wrong answer type 1: "The facts conversation is usually the most important because it is the most visible" — plausible but inverts the chapter's main point about identity being more powerful
- Tempting wrong answer type 2: "Naming the three conversations to yourself is the same as naming them to the other person during the conversation" — blurs the diagnostic/prescriptive distinction and the complexity of the identity conversation when surfaced aloud
- Tempting wrong answer type 3 (for scenario questions): "The person should address their feelings first" — correct instinct from later chapters (Ch5) but not what Ch1 teaches; Ch1 is about naming the layers, not prescribing the order

Explanation diversity notes:

- Each direct explanation must begin differently
- q01–q03 explanations should be shorter (recall-oriented)
- q04–q08 explanations should teach the mechanism through the scenario
- q09–q10 explanations should model deeper analytical thinking
