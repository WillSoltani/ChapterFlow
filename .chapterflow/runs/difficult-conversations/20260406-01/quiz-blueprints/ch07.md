Chapter: 7 — What's Your Purpose? When to Raise It, When to Let Go

Core concepts to test:
- The purpose check: asking what outcome you are trying to produce before entering a difficult conversation
- The three legitimate purposes: learning, expressing, problem-solving — what each serves and why each holds up
- The three warning-sign purposes: making the other person suffer, winning an argument, changing who they are — why each fails structurally
- Mixed purposes: legitimate and warning-sign in the same conversation; the move is to separate and redesign, not cancel
- When to let go: the honest inventory test; some grievances belong somewhere other than a confrontation with the person they concern
- Letting go vs. suppression: the structural distinction between deliberate release and unprocessed redirect
- (Hard) The cost of always raising: over-processing as a relational pattern; the framework used as cover for a warning-sign purpose

Bloom ceiling:
- Highest justified level: evaluate
- The chapter's sharpest distinction — letting go vs. suppression — requires readers to assess whether a described behavior constitutes deliberate release or underground redirect. The warning-sign purpose embedded in a legitimate one (Kasim's situation) also supports evaluate-level questions: readers must assess whether a described purpose is legitimate, warning-sign, or mixed. Questions at the evaluate level should present realistic scenarios and ask the reader to diagnose what kind of purpose is operating, or whether a described letting-go is genuinely deliberate.

Distractor notes:
- For legitimate purpose questions:
  - Tempting wrong 1: "making sure the other person understands your position" — close to expressing, but the legitimate purpose is the telling itself, not ensuring comprehension; the distractor conflates expressing with persuading
  - Tempting wrong 2: "clearing the air so the relationship can move forward" — sounds good but is too vague; it is not one of the three named purposes and can mask a warning-sign purpose (making the other person feel the weight of something)
  - Tempting wrong 3: "getting closure" — sounds legitimate but is often a warning-sign purpose in disguise; closure is not something a conversation with the other person can reliably produce

- For warning-sign purpose questions:
  - Tempting wrong 1: "expressing something important to you" — this is a legitimate purpose; the distractor tests whether readers distinguish expressing from making someone suffer
  - Tempting wrong 2: "solving a specific problem together" — legitimate purpose; tests whether readers know the line between problem-solving and winning
  - Tempting wrong 3: "letting the other person know how you feel" — close to expressing, which is legitimate; the distractor tests whether readers distinguish feeling-expression from wanting the other person to suffer the weight of a feeling

- For letting-go vs. suppression questions:
  - Tempting wrong 1: "both involve deciding not to say something to the other person" — true of both in one sense, but this collapses the distinction the chapter makes; the chapter's claim is about what happens to the grievance after the decision
  - Tempting wrong 2: "letting go means the grievance no longer matters" — incorrect; the chapter specifies that the grievance is real and deserves to be processed, just not in a confrontation with the person it concerns
  - Tempting wrong 3: "suppression is when you decide not to raise something; letting go is when you simply forget about it" — neither is accurate; suppression is unprocessed and underground, letting go is deliberate and processed elsewhere
