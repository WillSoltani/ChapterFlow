# Problem-Solving: Take the Lead

Farah and Miles had finally stopped having the wrong argument. For weeks the hiring conversation in their business had been a personality fight in disguise. Miles sounded reckless to Farah. Farah sounded fearful to Miles. Now, after enough real listening, each could say the other person's concern back without contempt. Payroll risk was real. Burnout and missed growth were real. The fight was quieter. Nothing was solved.

That is a distinct stage in a difficult conversation. Sometimes understanding is the work. Sometimes expression is the work. But there are also moments when the question has to change from "what is true here?" to "what do we do about it?" If no one takes the lead then, the conversation peters out into relief without change. Both people leave feeling as though something happened. A week later the same problem is still sitting where it was.

The opposite failure is just as common. One person takes over the problem-solving and presents an answer. The tone may sound collaborative, but the structure is unilateral. The other person is left with two roles: endorse it or resist it. Many people endorse it on the surface and undermine it later in practice because they had no hand in designing it.

The useful move is to take the lead on process without taking control of the outcome. That means proposing a joint inquiry into solutions rather than a solution. Not "here is what we should do," but "here is one thing I am thinking might help; what am I missing, and what would you add?" The point is shared design. A plan made together has a chance of being lived with. A plan handed down usually has to be enforced.

Farah and Miles only got unstuck when they stopped treating the first option named as the side to defend. Hire now had become Miles's side. Wait had become Farah's. The shift was to invent options before evaluating any one of them. A contractor for three months. A full-time hire with a conservative offer. A deferred hire triggered by a specific revenue number. Once there were several possibilities on the table, the conversation stopped being "your way versus my way" and became design again.

Generating options is only half the move. The next step is to ask what standard both people would accept in advance. What would a reasonable hiring policy look like for a business at their size? What level of payroll exposure would be responsible? What level of owner exhaustion would be irresponsible? Those questions move the discussion off preference and onto criteria both can inspect.

The same logic applies when the other person's idea arrives first. The easy mistake is to treat their proposal as the new enemy. Better to treat it as raw material. "What I like in your idea is that it protects cash. The part I want to change is that it leaves the workload untouched." Building on a proposal is not politeness. It is how joint inquiry stays joint. Rejection freezes design too early.

Then the conversation has to become action. "We should revisit this soon" feels responsible and usually means nothing. A useful close names what will happen, who owns it, and when it will be checked. Farah will call two contractors. Miles will model the cash impact. They will compare those answers on Monday morning. Specificity is the bridge between insight and behavior.

But specificity has a hard edge. Mira sees it in a meeting with Professor Rowan about recurring teaching-section chaos. Rowan wants a clean finish: Mira will confirm room coverage and revised section assignments by next Wednesday. The problem is that Mira does not control room assignments or late enrollment shifts. She can promise to send the roster she has, flag conflicts early, and notify Rowan when the department changes the schedule. She cannot honestly promise stability she does not control. A specific commitment that outruns real agency is not stronger problem-solving. It is false closure.

That limit matters because solution talk can become another way of avoiding reality. It can arrive too early, before enough listening has happened, and it can arrive too hard, pushing people into promises that make the meeting feel finished while setting them up to fail later. When a plan is needed, it has to be jointly designed and tied to what the people in the room can actually do. That is what lets a difficult conversation turn into action instead of just another version of pressure.
