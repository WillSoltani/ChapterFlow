# Don't Assume They Meant It

Andre has written three responses to Soleil's message. All three start the same way: "I didn't mean..." He has not sent any of them. Not because they are wrong — he genuinely did not mean anything by the joke — but because each draft feels like it is missing something he cannot name. He is correct about his intention. He is also, somehow, moving further from where he needs to be.

The problem is not what he meant. The problem is that he is treating what he meant as the conversation that needs to happen — when what Soleil needs is a different conversation first.

Intent and impact are not the same thing. Most people treat them as if they are. We judge ourselves by what we intended, and we judge others by what their actions produced. Both judgments assume that intent and impact are the same event read two different ways. They are not. They are separate things that can run in any combination: good intent with real harm, harmful intent with minimal impact, intent unknown and impact undeniable. The confusion produces two failure modes that show up in almost every difficult conversation.

The first is on the receiving end. When someone does something that hurts us, we quickly assemble a story about why. The intention we construct is almost always worse than the real one. We react to the constructed version as if it were confirmed. The other person, unaware that they have been assigned an intention, is defending against something they did not know was on the table. The conversation cannot move because both people are arguing about different things: one about what happened, and the other about what they meant — which was never contested.

The second failure mode is on the delivering end, and it causes more damage. When something we have said or done has caused harm, the instinct is to lead with the intent defense: "I didn't mean it that way." That may be true. It is also useless for the person who was hurt, because it does not address what happened to them. It closes the impact conversation before it has opened. Rodrigo had canceled plans with Celia three times in five weeks. When she said it felt like he did not want to spend time with her, his first response was to explain the work situation: the project, the deadlines, how overwhelmed he had been. She had not asked about the project. She had said something about what his behavior had meant to her. He spent four sentences explaining something she had not contested. The conversation ended worse than it started.

The move is to separate intent and impact rather than run them together. On the receiving end: notice when you are inventing the other person's intention rather than knowing it. Ask what you actually know about what they meant versus what you have assembled. On the delivering end: acknowledge the impact before you explain the intent. Not instead of — before. "I can see that it landed badly" is a different sentence from "I can see that landed badly, but I want you to know..." The second sentence uses the acknowledgment as a runway to re-deliver the same defense. The first one leaves the impact in the room as something real.

This is harder than it sounds. The reason most people lead with the intent defense is not carelessness — it is that they are trying to make the impact less real. If the harm was unintentional, it feels like it should count for less. The other person is experiencing it as real regardless. Acknowledging the impact without immediately qualifying it with the intent is the specific move that keeps the conversation open.

What Andre needed was not a fourth draft starting with "I didn't mean." It was something that could let Soleil's experience be real before it explained his. "I can hear that it made you uncomfortable, and I want to understand what happened. Can we talk about it?" That sentence does not require him to accept blame for bad intent. It requires him to let the impact exist first.

This move has a harder version that the chapter does not fully resolve: what happens when the intent was genuinely bad — when the person meant it the way it landed? The disentangle move still applies, but now the deliverer-side job changes. Denying the intent would be dishonest. Acknowledging it is the uncomfortable version of the same move: "I did mean it that way, and I want to talk about that." That is a harder conversation than the accidental harm case. It is also the one the framework points toward.

Once intent and impact are separated, a different question opens: not what each person meant, but what each person has been doing that keeps producing this kind of situation. That is what the next chapter is about.
