# Have Your Feelings (Or They Will Have You)

Tara has a meeting in five minutes. She has a printed agenda. The questions are professional and appropriate: what was the business reasoning, what alternatives were considered, what the timeline looked like from the executive team's perspective. She knows the questions are wrong — not factually wrong, but wrong in the sense that they are not what this meeting is actually for. What the meeting is actually for, she has spent three days telling herself is not appropriate, not professional, not something she can bring into a room with her CEO. So she will ask the business questions.

She is grieving a project she worked on for eight months. She is scared about what the decision means for her standing. She is furious that no one consulted her. She has not named any of these things, even to herself. They are underground.

Underground is not safe. Feelings that are suppressed do not disappear — they redirect. They redirect into tone: a coldness in the voice the speaker denies but everyone can hear. They redirect into contempt: a slight dismissiveness toward someone who has already been decided not worth full engagement. They redirect into avoidance: the email that does not get answered, the subject that is never raised. They redirect into sudden escalation: the moment when a conversation about something small blows up in a way that seems disproportionate, because it was not about the small thing. The feelings conversation is already happening. The question is whether it is happening above or below ground.

Tara's meeting will go nowhere, because the conversation she needs to have is not the one she scheduled. The CEO will answer the business questions carefully, and every answer will land wrong, because the questions were not about business reasoning. Both people will leave confused about why the other person seemed unsatisfied.

Most people under-identify what they are feeling. Under "fine" or "frustrated" there is usually a specific mix — grief, fear, anger, relief, shame, embarrassment, hope — and the specific feeling is more useful than the generic. Tara's feelings are not just "upset." They are grief (the work is gone), fear (what does this mean for her position?), and anger (she was not consulted). Each points to a different stake. The grief wants acknowledgment. The fear wants information. The anger wants its legitimacy recognized. A meeting about business reasoning cannot address any of them. The first move is to find what you are actually feeling — not the labeled version, but the specific one.

Rourke has been performing cheerfulness in his friend group for three months, since something Zoe said at a group dinner that made him feel dismissed and that he decided was not worth raising. He has been showing up with maximum energy, supporting Zoe's ideas in group texts, doing everything right on the surface. But the performance is not invisible. The other people in the group can feel something is off, and they have quietly adjusted — slightly less open, slightly more careful. Rourke's suppression is his, but the cost is distributed. What he is sitting on is affecting the whole room.

Once you have found the feeling, the second move is negotiating with it. Feelings are produced by the stories you tell, and examining the story can revise the feeling. This is not suppression — it is inspection. If Tara is catastrophizing about her standing, examining that story ("am I actually at risk, or is this a worst-case I am constructing?") might revise panic to concern. If concern is more accurate than panic, the conversation she needs changes. The negotiation does not eliminate the feeling. It makes it more accurate.

The third move is not letting the feeling drive. The feeling is a signal about what matters; it is not a script for what to say. When feelings drive, they produce reactions calibrated to the feeling rather than the situation — the business meeting that blows up, the group gathering where something small triggers three months of accumulated suppression. The move is to use the feeling as information: "I am scared about my standing. What do I actually need to know?"

The fourth move is sharing feelings in their direct form. There is a structural difference between "I'm hurt" and "I feel like you don't care." The first is a report about yourself. The second is a verdict about the other person, framed as a feeling. It has no feeling word in it — it has a claim about someone else's internal state. Feelings-shaped accusations produce defensiveness because they are accusations. Direct feelings statements are more likely to produce acknowledgment because they are reports about yourself.

Ben has a 1:1 with Sadie, who has been making more mistakes than usual. He asks how things are going. She says: "I'm fine, just a lot going on." He accepts this and moves to the work items. The meeting ends with nothing surfaced, and both feel worse than when they started. He took the "fine" at face value because that is the polite move in that context. A different move — "I can hear you're managing a lot; if anything shifts, I want to make sure there's space" — is not forcing disclosure. It is acknowledging that the feeling is real and leaving a door open.

The fifth move is acknowledging the other person's feelings before problem-solving. When someone expresses a feeling — even imperfectly, even as a feelings-shaped accusation — skipping to the fix misses it. "I'm sorry you feel that way, here's what we can do" moves past the feeling without letting it be seen. The right first move is to let it be in the room: "I can hear that this has been hard." Not because you agree with the story producing the feeling, not because you accept responsibility for causing it — but because the feeling is real and it needs acknowledgment before anything else can land.

Tara does not need to tell her CEO she is grieving and scared and furious. But she needs to have found those feelings before she walked in. If she had, the questions she asks would be different — not "what was the business reasoning," but something that gets her closer to what she actually needs: to know whether the decision was about the project or about her, and to have its impact acknowledged. She can do that without performing emotion. But she cannot do it without knowing what she is actually asking about.

The feelings conversation is not optional. Avoiding it is usually what made the original conversation difficult in the first place. Bringing it above ground does not mean expressing everything. It means not letting suppression redirect it into tone and avoidance and everything that makes conversations harder than they need to be.

When feelings run hot in a difficult conversation, they are often signaling something beyond the surface emotion — something about what the situation means for the person's sense of themselves: their competence, their worth, their standing. That signal points to a deeper layer. That layer has its own name, and it is what the next chapter is about.
