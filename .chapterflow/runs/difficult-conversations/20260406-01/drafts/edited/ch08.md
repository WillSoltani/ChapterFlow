# Begin from the Third Story

Soren had written three versions of the message and could not tell what was wrong with any of them. The concern was real: requirements on his project had been changing mid-sprint without warning, and twice in the past month it had cost his team a full day of rework. The drafts were reasonable, professional — and each one slightly more pointed than the problem required. Each started from the same place: here is what has been happening, here is why it is a problem, here is what needs to change. He could not see that all three had the same structure until he tried to imagine Claudia reading them, and understood what she would feel at the first sentence.

Most difficult conversations go wrong before the first substantive exchange. The problem is not tone — it is position. When you open from your own story, you treat your version of events as the baseline: here is what happened, here is what it means, now respond. Before the other person has said a word, they have been placed in the role of defendant. Their options are to confirm your account, dispute it, or defend against it. None of those is inquiry.

This is a structural problem, not a tonal one. A gentler first-story opener is still a first-story opener. The defenses come up not because of what the speaker sounds like, but because of the implicit premise: I have already assessed this situation, and you are being asked to respond to my assessment.

There is a different way to begin. The authors call it the third story — the account that belongs to neither party. Not your story, not the other person's story, but the story a thoughtful observer would give: two people working from different assumptions, different information, or different readings of the same events, with no verdict yet about which view is closer to right. The third story names the disagreement without assigning it to anyone's failure.

The shift does three things. It does not assume the speaker is already right — the third-story frame acknowledges that the speaker's version is partial, not because it is wrong but because the other person holds context and interpretation not yet heard. It invites joint inquiry rather than message delivery: here is a gap between our views, let's look at it together. And it lowers the identity stakes — when the opening assigns no fault, neither party is positioned as the one who got it wrong, and the conversation can be about the situation rather than anyone's competence or character.

Soren's first draft had read: "I need to flag a concern about how mid-sprint requirements changes are being handled — it's been creating significant rework and I think we need a different process." He rewrote it: "I think we've been working from different assumptions about what should trigger a mid-sprint change, and I'd like to compare notes so we're starting from the same place." The first message treats Claudia as someone who needs to receive a concern. The second treats her as someone whose view Soren has not yet heard. That is the operational difference.

The third-story opener closes with an invitation. Tsega, a first-year graduate student, had been wanting to raise a concern with Professor Huang about her chapter feedback sitting for six weeks. Her original version: "I feel like my draft has been waiting too long." She rewrote it: "I'm not sure we're on the same page about what the feedback timeline is supposed to look like — I'd like to get clearer on that so we're both expecting the same thing." The closing move matters. "I'd like to get clearer on that" signals Tsega has something to learn from the conversation — that hearing Professor Huang's view is part of why she is raising it. A neutrally framed opener that closes with "I need you to understand why this is a problem" has not made the full move. The frame and the invitation belong together.

The invitation is only genuine when the speaker means it. A third-story opener delivered while remaining privately certain you are right uses the form without the stance. The other person can usually feel this — a technically correct framing without genuine openness can function as manipulation, more corrosive than an honest first-story opener, because it obscures the speaker's actual intent while appearing fair-minded. If writing the neutral opener feels dishonest — if it feels like pretending not to know something you already know — the question is not how to phrase the opening. It is whether the learning stance is actually in place.

There are things the third-story opener cannot fix. The first: when acknowledgment needs to come before inquiry. Some conversations involve a genuine need to feel heard before exploration is possible. A third-story opener in those situations can feel evasive — procedurally correct but relationally wrong, more interested in being fair-minded than in understanding what the other person has been through. The second: the structural power gap. When significant authority differentials exist, "joint inquiry" can be nominal in a way both parties sense. The opener frames the conversation as collaborative; the underlying structure may determine the outcome regardless. The third-story opener cannot reframe a dynamic more powerful than any conversational framing.

The third story gets the conversation started. What happens next — how to hear what the other person is saying once both parties are in the room — is a different question.
