# What's Your Purpose? When to Raise It, When to Let Go

Kasim had written three versions of the meeting invite and deleted each one. The conversation with Lena was clear enough: the handoff process between their teams had been broken for a year and a half. The drafts were professional, reasonable — and every one was slightly more pointed than the problem required. When he read the third aloud, he understood what the tone was telling him. He did not just want to fix the process. He wanted Lena to understand — in some uncomfortable way — what the friction had cost him.

Before you prepare how to say something, ask what the conversation is for. Not what you plan to say — what you need it to produce. Three purposes justify raising a difficult conversation. Three others predict it will not work.

The three that hold up do so because a conversation can deliver them. Learning: there is something about how the other person sees the situation that you do not know and need to know. Expressing: the telling is itself the outcome — something needs to be said, and the saying is the point. Problem-solving: there is a specific change both parties need and that neither can make alone.

The others cannot be delivered. Making the other person suffer: a conversation cannot reliably produce guilt in someone who has not already chosen to feel it. Winning an argument: genuine concession requires genuine movement — a conversation designed for winning treats the other person as a target, and targets do not concede sincerely. Changing who the other person is: people change themselves. A conversation can name a pattern; it cannot produce the transformation.

Kasim's situation is common. He had a legitimate purpose — problem-solving — but mixed in with it was something else: wanting Lena to feel the weight of what the friction had cost him. That was not a reason to cancel the meeting. It was information. The accusatory tone in his drafts was pointing at something real — unacknowledged cost that part of him wanted the conversation to settle. That account cannot be settled in a meeting about process design. Once he could see the two purposes separately, he could see which one belonged in the meeting. He rewrote the invite.

Some grievances cannot be served by a conversation with the person they concern. The test: would this person hear it the way it needs to be heard? Would it change anything? Would something genuinely be resolved? If the answer is probably not, the grievance belongs somewhere other than a confrontation with them. The question is not whether the grievance is real. It is where it can actually land.

Fleur had rehearsed the conversation with her brother Adil six times. At a family gathering three months earlier, he had dismissed her career — offhandedly, in the way that sometimes lands worse than cruelty because it costs nothing. Each rehearsal ended the same way: she was not satisfied; he did not fully understand. When she asked honestly whether any version would produce what she needed, the answer was probably no. What she needed was something he had not been able to give in the original moment, and a confrontation months later was unlikely to change that. The grievance said something about what she had needed from him that he was not in a position to provide. That was worth knowing. It did not require Adil to receive it.

What she did instead was not suppression. Suppression is unprocessed — the grievance goes underground and resurfaces as friction, as distance, as irritability that seems unconnected to its source. Letting go is different. Fleur took the grievance to a friend who could receive it, named to herself what she had needed and why she did not get it. The grievance was processed. It simply did not belong with Adil. That distinction — between a grievance taken deliberately elsewhere and one buried — is not visible from the outside. From the inside, it is decisive.

The framework does not often say this plainly: the cost of always raising. A person who processes every grievance in confrontation — who treats every friction as a conversation that needs to happen — is not practicing well. They are over-processing, and it exhausts the people around them. Worse: the vocabulary can be used as cover. "I just need to be honest with you" is hard to argue against. But if the honesty is in service of making the other person reckon with something, or winning a point that has been festering, the problem-solving language is a wrapper around a purpose that would not survive the inventory.

In the other direction: letting go can be avoidance with better vocabulary. There are grievances that need to be raised — where the pattern or the stakes mean the conversation must happen. The framework does not give you a rule for telling the difference between deliberate release and quiet self-silencing. It gives you better tools for the inventory. The honesty of that inventory is on you.

Once you know what the conversation is for, the question becomes how to begin it.
