# Abandon Blame

Dara has written three versions of a message to HR. The first was titled "Complaint." She changed it to "Concern." Then she deleted the draft entirely. She is not sure what she is trying to report. Her manager Felix is not doing anything wrong in any way she can name precisely — he is checking in more often, asking more questions, adding her to Slack threads she does not need to be on. It is too much, and it is getting worse. She knows who she is angry at. She does not know what to do about it.

She also knows, somewhere underneath the certainty, that she stopped talking to Felix three months ago. She stopped offering context, stopped volunteering updates, started turning in work without commentary and waiting to be asked. She had reasons. But she is not thinking about the reasons right now. She is thinking about the email to HR.

Most difficult conversations get stuck in blame for a specific reason: blame is often accurate. Felix's oversight is real. Dara's frustration is justified. The problem is not that blame gets the facts wrong. The problem is that blame asks the wrong question. It asks who deserves punishment. That question has an answer sometimes, but the answer does not change anything. The oversight continues. The silence continues. The cycle continues. Blame is retrospective in a situation that is entirely about what happens next.

Contribution asks a different question: what is each person doing right now that keeps this going, and what would it take for each to do something different? Contribution is not a softer version of blame, and it is not an attempt to distribute blame equally. Contributions are almost never equal — most situations are more like 70/30 or 90/10. Contribution does not pretend otherwise. What it does differently is aim at the system producing the outcome rather than the villain producing it.

Dara and Felix are each doing something that guarantees the other person will keep doing what they are doing. Dara's silence contributed to Felix's anxiety. Her withdrawal created the information gap that his oversight is attempting to fill. And his oversight contributed to her withdrawal — it confirmed the story she was already telling herself about not being trusted. Her withdrawal confirmed his story about a performance concern. Both are right about what the other person is doing. Neither can see what their own behavior produces. That is not a character flaw in either person. It is a system, and both are in it.

The same structure runs in longer patterns. Marta has been managing her family's holiday logistics for a decade. She is good at it, and she resents it. Every year she raises it with her brother Sione; every year he defends the current arrangement; every year both people leave with the same roles intact. What Marta cannot see from inside her resentment: her competence at logistics has invisibly guaranteed that Sione never had to develop any logistics capacity. The more seamlessly she handles everything, the less he has to care. What Sione cannot see from inside his defensiveness: his conflict-avoidance has let Marta carry the burden for a decade. His non-role is itself a role. The conversation that could change things is not the one where Marta tells Sione he never helps — she has had that conversation, and it does not change anything. The conversation that could change things is the one where each of them names their contribution to the pattern. That conversation has never happened.

There are four recognizable patterns in how people contribute to difficult conversations. The first is avoiding until now: not raising an issue when it is small, so that the issue calcifies, and the delay becomes part of the problem. The second is being unapproachable: creating conditions — through defensiveness, volatility, or consistent dismissal — that make it hard for the other person to raise something. The person who is unapproachable rarely knows it. The third is style intersections: two people with different work styles or relational preferences that produce friction in combination without either style being wrong individually. Yemi delivers work early and rough; Kwame delivers late and comprehensive. Neither style is wrong on its own. The intersection is what the chapter is describing. The fourth is problematic role assumptions: both people settle into fixed roles that guarantee the same result each time — the organizer and the disengaged, the critic and the defender, the pursuer and the avoider.

The reason contribution is harder than blame is not the analysis. The contribution analysis is often clear once you look. The reason it is harder is that naming your own contribution requires holding something uncomfortable: that whatever the split is — 90/10 or something else — there is a piece of it that belongs to you, and that piece is what you can actually change. The other person's contribution is not in your direct control. Yours is.

Dara closes the HR draft. She opens a new message to Felix: "I've been less communicative than usual, and I think that's made things harder for both of us. Can we talk?" That message is not a concession. It is not an admission that Felix's oversight is appropriate. It is a contribution named in service of a different outcome — one where the system can shift because one person decided to move differently in it.

Once contribution is mapped and each person can see their role in the pattern, something surfaces: not just understanding, but feeling. The recognition of one's own contribution to harm can produce guilt, grief, or something closer to relief. Those feelings need somewhere to go before they derail the next conversation. That is what the next chapter addresses.
