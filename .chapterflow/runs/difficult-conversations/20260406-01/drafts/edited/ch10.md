# Speak for Yourself with Clarity and Power

Soraya had rewritten the note to Emil four times by Thursday night. The task-force announcement had gone out on Monday morning, and her name was not on it. In one draft she nearly disappeared: "It's probably not a big deal, but I just wondered about the list." In another she swung to indictment: "I've been invisible here for years, and this proves it." Both were false in different ways. One asked to be ignored. The other asked to be argued with. The honest version was harder to find than either extreme.

Once you have listened well, the next difficulty is saying what is true for you without turning the truth into a weapon. Most people slide toward one of two failures. They hold back because the real point feels dangerous, or they overcorrect and speak in a way that puts the other person on trial. Clean expression asks for something narrower: report your experience accurately enough that it can be discussed.

That report has three parts. First, the observation. What happened, described cleanly enough that a camera could have recorded it. Not "you dismissed me." The observation is the list that went out without your name, the comment the other person actually made, the meeting where your idea was passed over. If the first sentence already contains your verdict on motive or character, the rest of the conversation begins inside that verdict.

Second, the internal response. What you felt, owned as yours. "I felt shut out." "I felt more rattled than I expected." Third, the story you started telling yourself. This is the meaning your mind rushed to assign to what happened: my work is less visible than I thought; you do not take me seriously; this project is not safe in your hands. Most people skip this part or smuggle it in as fact. Naming it as a story keeps it checkable. It says: this is where my mind went, not what I know for certain.

That is why the story matters. Offered as your story, it invites correction. Offered as reality, it invites defense. "The story I started telling myself was that my work is less visible here than I thought" lands differently from "you have been overlooking me." The fear may be similar. What changes is whether the other person is being asked to examine the meaning with you or answer for it.

Soraya's useful sentence only appeared after she wrote the bad versions first. The underdone version was too small: "It is probably not a big deal." The overdone version was too large: "This proves I have been invisible here for years." The honest version sat between them: "When I saw the announcement and realized I was not on the list, I felt more thrown than I expected. The story I started telling myself was that my work is less visible here than I thought. I want to check that story with you." The observation is specific. The feeling is owned. The story is offered as a story.

Saying it cleanly also means saying it early. Long runways rarely soften the point. They mostly make the other person brace for what is coming. Better to say the hard thing first and let the conversation build around it. Better, too, to resist the familiar distortions. Exaggeration makes the point easy to dismiss. Minimization makes it impossible to address. The true sentence usually sits in the middle, with less drama than anger wants and more weight than fear wants.

Then comes the moment that undoes many good starts. The other person flinches. Emil might reach too quickly for reassurance, or he might stiffen and challenge Soraya's reading. At that point the temptation is immediate retreat: "Forget it. I probably read it wrong. It is fine." But it is not fine, and now the conversation has to pretend not to know what both people just heard. Holding the point does not mean sharpening it. It means leaving it on the table long enough for it to become discussable. "I may be wrong about the story. I do want to check it. But that is where my head went." That is steady without becoming aggressive.

Marisol faced a faster version of the same problem in seminar. Gavin said her paper's central claim felt bigger than the evidence she had offered. She could defend the whole project at once, or she could say something precise: "When you said the claim felt bigger than the evidence, I felt myself get defensive. The story I started telling was that you thought the whole project was careless. Is that what you meant?" That sentence does not surrender the paper. It also does not accuse Gavin of contempt. It gives him something he can answer clearly.

Ownership language still does not make every sentence safe. "The story I told myself" can still sound accusatory if the observation is already blame-soaked, if the story is really a conclusion in disguise, or if the other person has not been heard enough for your expression to land as reciprocal. "Do not take it back" can also sour into stubbornness if it starts meaning "do not revise anything." The point is to keep the truth available for discussion, not to make it sacred. Once both people can do that, the conversation is ready to turn toward what to do next.
