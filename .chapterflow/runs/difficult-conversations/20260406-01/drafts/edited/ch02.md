# Stop Arguing About Who's Right

Reza has explained the roadmap case four times. He has the data, the user research, the competitive analysis. He has restructured the argument each time based on the pushback, looking for the framing that will finally land. Yuki has done the same. Same spreadsheet between them, no new information in the room for a month, and neither can understand why the other person keeps not getting it.

This is the most common version of a difficult conversation: two people with coherent versions of what is true, each pushing harder, each interpreting the other's resistance as a failure to listen or reason. The harder each pushes, the more the other digs in. Both are frustrated for the same reason — they think the problem is the other person's capacity to receive information.

The problem is the stance.

Most people enter a difficult conversation with an unexamined assumption about what the conversation is for: I already have a version of what happened, and the purpose of this conversation is to get you to accept it. This is the message delivery stance. It is also the reason the conversation cannot move.

Message delivery fails for a structural reason. The other person has their own version of events — internally coherent, not because they are unreasonable, but because they have a different vantage point, different information, and a different set of experiences producing a different story. They are not waiting to be corrected. They are in message delivery mode too. Two people simultaneously trying to deliver reality to each other, neither making room for the other's version to be plausible. The conversation escalates without going anywhere.

The alternative is not capitulation. It is a different goal for the conversation. Instead of entering to establish that your version is correct, enter to understand what the other person is seeing that you are not. What is their version, and how does it make sense from where they stand? What might you be missing? This is the learning stance — and it requires something most people find genuinely difficult: treating your own version of events as one perspective among possible perspectives rather than as the obvious truth the other person is inexplicably refusing to accept.

Your version is informed by your experience, your position, and the information you have access to. It is almost certainly incomplete in some way you cannot currently see. The learning stance does not ask you to abandon your view. It asks you to hold it as a view.

The design team lead who has delivered the same feedback three times — as a question, as a direct statement, as a company policy reference — has been solving a delivery problem when the problem is a stance problem. The feedback is accurate. The framings have been thoughtful. And the senior team member keeps defending her position with the same energy each time, because each new framing is still arriving with the same underlying assumption: I know what is true here, and this conversation is for getting you to accept it. She can feel that assumption in every version, even when the words are different.

What actually changes things is harder: noticing that you are trying to deliver a message, and asking instead what the other person is seeing that you are not. Not as a rhetorical question — those are just message delivery wearing a question mark — but as genuine inquiry. What are Yuki's constraints that Reza has not asked about? What does the product data look like from Yuki's position? What would it mean to Yuki if Reza's version were right, and what is that reaction trying to protect?

The learning stance is harder than it sounds because it requires genuine willingness to be changed by what you hear. Most people can perform openness for a few exchanges before reverting to delivery mode. The other person can feel the reversion — questions that are waiting for a particular answer, or "yes, and" moves that absorb information without considering it. Performed openness is not the same as being open, and the difference between them determines whether the conversation produces anything new.

The learning stance does not guarantee agreement. Understanding what Yuki sees does not mean Reza has to change his position. It means he knows more than he did. The next conversation starts from a different place. That is a narrower promise than most people want from a stance shift — and it is the honest one.

Inside the "what happened" conversation, once you stop trying to win it, a specific confusion becomes available to see: the assumptions you are making about the other person. Not about their position — about their intentions. About why they did what they did and what they were trying to accomplish. Most people do not realize they have assembled a story about the other person's intentions before the conversation even starts. What happens when that story is wrong — and what happens when it is right — is what the next chapter addresses.
