# Sort Out the Three Conversations

Naomi has been rewriting the same opening sentence for forty minutes. She knows what she wants to say. She has the data — the three missed deadlines, the two reschedules, the work that came in at 60% of where it needed to be. She has been in this role for 18 months and she has learned, from her own managers, that good feedback is specific. So she made it specific. And yet she keeps hitting delete.

She does not have a word for what is wrong with the sentence. The problem is not the facts. The problem is that she is also preparing for a conversation she has not quite admitted she is preparing for: the one where she has to be the kind of manager who can say this without becoming someone's enemy. The one where Derek, who has been at this company for 14 years and who has bailed out more critical projects than she has shipped in her career, does not walk out of the room feeling erased.

Naomi thinks she is preparing for one conversation. She is actually preparing for three.

This is the central observation behind everything that follows in this book: what people call a "difficult conversation" is never one conversation. It is always three, running simultaneously, with different subject matter and different stakes. Most of the time, the people inside the conversation are only aware of one of the three. And it is almost never the most important one.

The first conversation is the one everyone shows up to: the conversation about what happened. What was said, what was done, who was right, who was wrong, who is to blame. This is the conversation Naomi scripted. It is also the one where most difficult conversations stall. Both people have a version of events that is internally coherent from where they stand. Both are trying to get the other person to accept their version. Neither can, because the other person has different information, a different vantage point, and their own internally coherent story. The more each person pushes their version, the less either person can hear the other. The conversation escalates without going anywhere.

The second conversation is the one most people pretend is not happening: the conversation about feelings. What each person is actually experiencing, and whether those feelings have any right to be in the room. Professional cultures train people to set feelings aside and discuss things "on the merits." What this actually accomplishes is that the feelings go underground, not away. A person who is carrying grief or anger or humiliation while pretending not to be will still have those feelings, but now they will come out sideways, as a sharper tone than the words warrant, as contempt inside a calm sentence, as a collapse in generosity at the moment generosity would have helped. The feelings do not vanish. They ventriloquize.

The third conversation is the quietest one, and it is usually the one doing the most damage. It is the conversation each person is having inside their own head about what the situation says about who they are. Am I competent? Am I a good person? Am I worthy of this relationship, this job, this family? When the situation threatens to answer those questions in the negative, the person's behavior in the room stops making sense by any ordinary logic, because the real stakes are no longer about the topic. They are about the self.

Carmen called her brother Miguel because the texts had stopped making sense. They had been arguing for four days about who would take the next weekend visit to their father, who has been diagnosed with early-stage dementia and can no longer be left alone for long. The logistics argument had filled 47 messages. The word "logistics" appeared seven times. Neither sibling noticed that they were not arguing about the weekend. Carmen was carrying grief about her father's decline and fifteen years of resentment about being the one who always picks up the slack. Miguel was carrying guilt about living three hours away and defensiveness about every implication — stated or not — that his distance made him a bad son. The identity conversation: Am I the kind of daughter who abandons her parent when it gets hard? Am I the kind of son who lets his sister handle everything? No one said any of that. They argued about the weekend.

Back to Naomi. The reason she keeps deleting the first sentence is that she is trying to write the facts conversation in a way that will also somehow resolve the identity conversation — hers and Derek's. She needs to feel like a fair leader. He needs to feel like someone whose 14 years meant something. A list of bullet points about missed deadlines is not going to do either job, no matter how specific it is. She is not writing the wrong sentences. She is trying to carry too many conversations in one container.

This is what the three-conversation frame actually gives you: not a solution, but a different kind of vision. You can see what you are actually dealing with. You can stop being confused about why a conversation that should be about a simple operational problem feels like it might end something. The feelings conversation is real. The identity conversation is real. And until you can see all three clearly, you will keep preparing for the wrong one.

What to do about them is another question. The first move, the one that makes all the other moves possible, is to see them at all.
