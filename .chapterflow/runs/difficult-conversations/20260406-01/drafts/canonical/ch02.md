# Stop Arguing About Who's Right

Reza has explained the roadmap case four times now. He has the data, the user research, the competitive analysis. He has restructured the argument each time based on the pushback, looking for the framing that will finally get through. Yuki has done the same. They have the same spreadsheet between them, no new information has entered the room in a month, and neither of them can understand why the other person keeps not getting it.

This is the most common version of a difficult conversation: two people, each with a coherent version of what is true, each pushing that version harder, each interpreting the other's resistance as a failure to listen or a failure to reason. The harder each person pushes, the more the other person digs in. The conversation is not going anywhere, and both people are frustrated for the same reason — they think the problem is the other person's capacity to receive information.

The problem is the stance.

Most people enter a difficult conversation with a specific, usually unexamined assumption about what the conversation is for. The assumption: I already have a version of what happened, and the purpose of this conversation is to get you to accept it. Call this the message delivery stance. It is the default. It is also structural to why the conversation cannot move.

Message delivery fails for a specific reason. The other person also has a version of what happened, and it is also internally coherent — not because they are stupid or unreasonable but because they have a different vantage point, different information, and a different set of experiences that produce a different story. They are not waiting to be corrected. They are in message delivery mode too. The result is two people trying to deliver versions of reality to each other simultaneously, neither making room for the other's version to be plausible, neither learning anything. The conversation escalates without moving.

The alternative is not to capitulate. It is to change what the conversation is for. Instead of entering to establish that your version is correct, enter to understand what the other person is seeing that you are not. What is their version, and how does it make sense from where they stand? What might you be missing? What might they be missing? What could both of you see more clearly once neither of you is trying to win?

This is the learning stance, and it requires something most people find genuinely difficult: holding your own version of events as one perspective among possible perspectives rather than as the obvious truth that the other person is inexplicably refusing to accept. Your version is informed by your experience, your position, and the information you have access to. It is almost certainly incomplete in some way you cannot currently see, because incompleteness is how perspective works. The learning stance does not ask you to abandon your view — it asks you to treat it as a view.

The design team lead who has delivered the same feedback three times in three different framings — as a question, as a direct statement, as a reference to policy — has been solving a delivery problem when the problem is a stance problem. The feedback is accurate. The framing has been thoughtful. And the senior designer keeps defending the scope with the same energy each time, because each new framing is still being delivered with the same underlying assumption: I know what is true here, and the conversation is for getting you to accept it. The designer can feel that assumption in every version of the feedback, even when the words are different.

What is much harder, and what actually changes things, is when someone in a conversation notices that they are trying to deliver a message and asks instead: what is the other person seeing that I'm not? Not as a rhetorical question — those are just message delivery wearing a question mark — but as a genuine inquiry. What are Yuki's constraints that Reza has not asked about? What does the product data look like from Yuki's vantage point? What would it mean to Yuki if Reza's version were correct, and what is that response trying to protect?

The learning stance is harder than it sounds because it requires genuine willingness to be changed by what you hear. Most people can perform openness for two or three exchanges before reverting to delivery mode. The other person can feel this reversion — it usually reads as questions that are waiting for a particular answer, or as "yes, and" moves that absorb the information without actually considering it. Performed openness is not the same as being open, and the gap between them is what determines whether the conversation produces anything new.

Reza and Yuki's roadmap fight can end two ways. In one version, one of them finds a better argument and the other finally accepts it. In the other version, one of them asks what the other is actually afraid will happen if their version wins — and hears something that was not in the spreadsheet. The second conversation is rarer and more useful.

What it does not guarantee is agreement. Understanding what Yuki sees does not mean Reza has to change his position. It means he now knows more than he did, and the next version of the conversation starts from a different place. That is a narrower promise than most people want from a single shift in approach. It is also the honest one.
