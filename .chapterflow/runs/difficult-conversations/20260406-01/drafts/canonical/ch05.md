# Have Your Feelings (Or They Will Have You)

Tara has a meeting in five minutes. She has a printed agenda. She prepared the questions carefully: what was the business reasoning behind the decision, what alternatives were considered, what the timeline looked like from the executive team's perspective. She knows the questions are professional and appropriate. She also knows they are wrong — not wrong as in incorrect, but wrong as in not what this meeting is actually for. What the meeting is actually for, she has spent three days telling herself is not appropriate, not professional, not something she can bring into a room with her CEO. So she will ask the business questions.

She is grieving a project she worked on for eight months. She is scared about what the decision means for her standing at the company. She is furious that no one consulted her before announcing it. She has named none of these things to herself. They are underground.

Underground is not safe. Feelings that are suppressed do not disappear — they redirect. They redirect into tone: a coldness or impatience in the voice that the speaker denies but everyone can hear. They redirect into contempt: the slight dismissiveness of someone who has already decided the other person is not worth engaging with fully. They redirect into avoidance: the email that does not get answered, the meeting that does not get scheduled, the subject that is never raised. They redirect into sudden escalation: the moment when a conversation about something small blows up in a way that seems disproportionate, because it was not actually about the small thing — it was about everything that had not been said for months.

Tara's meeting will go nowhere because the conversation she needs to have is not the one she scheduled. The CEO will answer the business questions carefully and sincerely, and every answer will land wrong, because the questions were not about business reasoning. Both of them will leave the meeting confused about why the other person seemed unsatisfied.

The feelings conversation is not optional. Avoiding it was usually what made the original conversation difficult in the first place. But bringing feelings into a conversation does not mean flooding the other person with them. It means finding them first.

Most people under-identify what they are feeling. Under "fine" or "frustrated" or "upset" there is usually a specific mix — grief, fear, anger, relief, shame, loneliness, embarrassment, hope — and the mix is more useful than the generic. Tara's underground feelings are not just "upset about the project." They are grief (the work is gone), fear (what does this mean for her position?), and anger (she was not consulted). These three feelings point to different stakes and different conversations. The grief wants acknowledgment. The fear wants information or reassurance. The anger wants to be seen as legitimate. A meeting about business reasoning cannot address any of them.

Rourke has been performing cheerfulness in his friend group for three months, since a falling-out with Zoe that he has told himself was not worth raising. He has been showing up to gatherings with maximum energy, supporting Zoe's ideas in group texts, doing everything right. The performance is not invisible. The other people in the group can feel that something is off, and they have adjusted — they are slightly less open with Rourke than they used to be, because his performed cheerfulness makes it hard to be real. The suppression is his, but the cost is distributed. Rourke's underground feelings are affecting everyone in the room.

Finding the feeling is the first move. The second is negotiating with it. Feelings are produced by stories, and examining the story can sometimes revise the feeling. This is not suppression — it is inspection. If Tara is catastrophizing about her professional standing, examining that story ("am I actually at risk here, or am I imagining a worst case?") might revise the panic to a concern. If the concern is more accurate than the panic, the conversation she needs is different. The negotiation does not eliminate the feeling. It makes the feeling more accurate.

The third move is not letting the feeling drive. The feeling is a signal about what matters; it is not an instruction for what to say. When feelings drive, they produce disproportionate reactions — the meeting about business reasoning that blows up, the group gathering where something small triggers the three months of suppressed frustration. Using the feeling as information rather than as a script is the difference between "I notice I'm scared about my standing — what do I actually need to know?" and saying the first scared thing that surfaces.

The fourth move is sharing feelings on their own terms. There is a structural difference between a direct feelings statement and what sounds like a feelings statement but is not. "I'm feeling hurt" is a report about yourself. "I feel like you don't care about this project" is a verdict about the other person, rendered in feelings language. The second form contains no feeling — it contains a judgment. "I feel like you don't care" has no feeling word in it; it has a claim about someone else's internal state dressed up as self-report. This distinction matters because feelings-shaped accusations produce defensiveness. The direct statement produces something closer to acknowledgment, because it is a report about you rather than a verdict about the other person.

Ben has a 1:1 with Sadie, who has been making more mistakes than usual. He opens by asking how things are going. She says: "I'm fine, just a lot going on." He accepts this and moves to the work items. The meeting ends with no issues surfaced, and both people feel worse than when they started. He took the "fine" at face value because the polite move in that context is to accept what someone offers. A different move is not to force disclosure but to leave a door open: "I can hear you're managing a lot. If anything shifts and you want to talk about it, I want to make sure there's space." That is not therapy. It is acknowledgment that the feeling is real, which is sometimes the only thing required for the feeling to find its way into the room.

The fifth move is acknowledging the other person's feelings before trying to problem-solve. When someone expresses a feeling — even imperfectly, even as a feelings-shaped accusation — the wrong response is to skip directly to the fix. "I'm sorry you feel that way, here's what we can do" moves past the feeling without acknowledging it, and the person expressing it knows it. The right first move is to let the feeling be seen: "I can hear that this has been hard." Not because you agree with the story producing the feeling, not because you are accepting responsibility for causing it — but because the feeling is legitimate material for the conversation, and it needs to be in the room before anything else can happen.

The moves matter in professional contexts because the stakes are real. In the meeting she is about to have, Tara does not need to tell her CEO she is grieving and scared and furious. But she needs to have found those feelings before she walked in. If she had, the questions she would ask would be different. Not "what was the business reasoning," but something that gets her closer to what she actually needs: to know whether the decision was about the project or about her, and to have its impact acknowledged. She can ask that without performing emotion. But she cannot ask it without knowing what she is actually asking about.

The feelings conversation is one of the three conversations that every difficult conversation contains. Bringing it above ground does not mean expressing everything you feel. It means not letting suppression redirect it into tone, avoidance, and everything else that makes conversations harder than they need to be.

When feelings are intense in a difficult conversation, they are often doing more than signaling an emotion. They are often signaling that something about the person's sense of themselves is at stake — their competence, their worth, their goodness. That signal points to the next layer. That layer has its own name.
