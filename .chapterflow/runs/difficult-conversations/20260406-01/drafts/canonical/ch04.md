# Abandon Blame

Dara has written three versions of a message to HR. The first was titled "Complaint." She changed it to "Concern." Then she deleted the draft entirely. She is not sure what she is trying to report. Her manager Felix is not doing anything wrong in any way she can name precisely — he is checking in more often, asking more questions, adding her to Slack threads she does not need to be on. It is too much, and it is getting worse. She knows who she is angry at. She does not know what to do about it.

She also knows, somewhere underneath the certainty, that she stopped talking to Felix three months ago. She stopped offering context. She stopped volunteering updates. She started turning in work without commentary and waiting to be asked. She had reasons. But she is not thinking about the reasons. She is thinking about the email to HR.

Most difficult conversations get stuck in blame for a reason: blame is accurate. Felix's oversight is real. Dara's frustration is justified. The problem is not that blame gets the facts wrong — it often gets the facts right. The problem is that blame asks the wrong question. It asks who deserves punishment. That question has an answer, sometimes, but the answer does not change anything. The oversight continues. The silence continues. The cycle continues. Blame is retrospective in a situation that is entirely about what happens next.

Contribution asks a different question: what is each person doing right now that keeps this going, and what would it take for each of them to do something different? Contribution is not the same as blame. It is not a softer version of blame or an attempt to distribute blame equally. Contributions are almost never equal — in most situations they are more like 70/30 or 90/10. Contribution does not pretend otherwise. What it does differently is aim at the system producing the outcome rather than the villain producing it.

Dara and Felix are each doing something that guarantees the other person will keep doing what they are doing. Dara's silence contributed to Felix's anxiety. Her withdrawal created the information gap that Felix's oversight is attempting to fill. And Felix's oversight contributed to Dara's withdrawal. His monitoring confirmed the story she was already telling — that she was not trusted — and her withdrawal confirmed his. Both are right about what the other person is doing. Neither can see what their own behavior is producing. That is the pattern. It is not a character flaw in either person. It is a system, and both people are in it.

The same system runs in longer patterns. Marta has been handling her family's holiday logistics for a decade. She is good at it, and she resents it. Every year she raises it with her brother Sione; every year he defends the current arrangement; every year both people leave the conversation with the same roles intact. What Marta cannot see from inside her resentment: her competence at logistics has invisibly guaranteed that Sione never had to develop any logistics capacity. The more seamlessly she handles everything, the less he has to care. Her contribution to the pattern is her competence itself — not wrong on its own, but wrong in the combination. What Sione cannot see from inside his defensiveness: his conflict-avoidance has let Marta carry the burden for a decade. His non-role is itself a role, and it has a cost.

The conversation that could change things is not the one where Marta tells Sione he never helps. She has had that conversation. It does not change anything because it aims at the individual, not the system. The conversation that could change things is the one where Marta names her contribution to the system — "I have been so competent at this that you have never had to be" — and Sione names his. That conversation has never happened.

There are four recognizable patterns in how people contribute to the situations that produce difficult conversations. The first is avoiding until now: someone does not raise an issue when it is small, and the issue calcifies. When they finally raise it months later, the delay is itself part of the pattern. The second is being unapproachable: someone's defensiveness, volatility, or consistent dismissal of concerns makes it hard for the other person to bring something up. The person who is unapproachable rarely knows it. The third is style intersections: two people have work styles or relational preferences that are not wrong individually but produce friction in combination. Yemi delivers work early and rough; Kwame delivers late and comprehensive. Neither style is wrong. But neither person named the incompatibility, and neither has asked the other what "done" means. The fourth is problematic role assumptions: both people have settled into fixed roles in their interaction that guarantee the same result. The organizer and the disengaged. The critic and the defender. The more one person plays their role, the more the other person plays theirs.

The reason contribution is harder than blame is not the analysis. The analysis is often clear once you look. The reason it is harder is that naming your own contribution requires holding something most people do not want to hold: that whatever the split is — 90/10, 70/30, something in between — there is a piece of it that belongs to you, and that piece is actually what you can change. The other person's contribution is not in your direct control. Yours is.

What Dara can change is that she stopped communicating. She cannot make Felix's oversight disappear by emailing HR. She can try a different move in the system. She closes the HR draft. She opens a new message. "I've been less communicative than usual, and I think that's made things harder for both of us. Can we talk?"

That message is not a concession or an apology. It is a contribution named in service of a different outcome. It does not say that Felix's oversight is appropriate. It says that Dara's part in the system is available to her to change, and she is changing it.

Once contribution is mapped and each person can name their role in the pattern, something usually surfaces: not just understanding, but feeling. The recognition of one's own contribution to harm can produce guilt, or grief, or something closer to relief. Those feelings need somewhere to go before they derail the next conversation. That is what the next chapter is about.
