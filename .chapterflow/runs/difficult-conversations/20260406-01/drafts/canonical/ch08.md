# Begin from the Third Story

Soren had written three versions of the message and could not tell what was wrong with any of them. The concern was real: requirements on his project had been changing mid-sprint without warning, and twice in the past month it had cost his team a full day of rework. The drafts were reasonable. They were professional. They were — when he read them back — each slightly more pointed than the problem required. Each one started from the same place: here is what has been happening, here is why it is a problem, here is what needs to change. He could not see that all three had the same structure until he tried to imagine Claudia reading them, and understood what she would feel reading the first sentence before she had reached the second.

Most difficult conversations go wrong before the first substantive exchange. The problem is not tone and it is not courage — it is position. When you open from your own story, you are treating your version of events as the baseline: here is what happened, here is what it means, now respond. Before the other person has said a word, they have been placed in the role of defendant. Their options are to confirm your account, dispute it, or defend against it. None of those is inquiry. The conversation is already framed as a verdict waiting to be delivered.

This is a structural problem, not a tonal one. A gentler version of the first-story opener is still a first-story opener. The listener is positioned as defendant regardless of how warmly the opening is delivered. The defenses come up not because of what the speaker sounds like, but because of the implicit premise: I have already assessed this situation, and you are being asked to respond to my assessment.

There is a different way to begin. The authors call it the third story — the account that belongs to neither party. Not your story, not the other person's story, but the story a thoughtful observer would give: two people working from different assumptions, different information, or different readings of the same events, with no verdict yet about which view is closer to right. The third story names the disagreement without assigning it to anyone's failure. It locates the problem in the gap between two views rather than in one person's conduct.

The shift does three things. First, it does not assume the speaker is already right. The third-story frame acknowledges that the speaker's version is incomplete — not because it is wrong, but because it is partial. The other person holds context, history, or interpretation that the speaker has not yet heard. Second, it invites joint inquiry rather than message delivery. Instead of: here is my conclusion, now respond — the third-story opener says: here is a gap between our views, let's look at it together. The conversational move that follows is exploration rather than argument. Third, it lowers the identity stakes. When the opening does not assign fault, neither party is positioned as the one who got it wrong. The conversation can be about the situation rather than about who is a bad colleague or a negligent supervisor. Identity, when threatened, produces defensiveness. Lowering the stakes at the outset gives the conversation a chance to begin before those defenses arrive.

Soren rewrote the message once more. His first draft had read: "I need to flag a concern about how mid-sprint requirements changes are being handled — it's been creating significant rework for my team and I think we need a different process." He rewrote it: "I think we've been working from different assumptions about what should trigger a mid-sprint requirements change, and I'd like to compare notes so we're at least starting from the same place." The difference is not politeness. The first message treats Claudia as someone who needs to receive a concern. The second treats her as someone whose view of the situation Soren has not yet heard. That is the operational difference between a first-story opener and a third-story one.

The third-story opener does not end with the frame — it closes with an invitation. Tsega, a first-year graduate student, had been wanting to raise a concern with Professor Huang about how long her chapter draft had been waiting for feedback. Her original version started: "I feel like my draft has been sitting for six weeks and that's too long." She rewrote it: "I'm not sure we're on the same page about what the feedback timeline is supposed to look like — I'd like to get clearer on that so we're both expecting the same thing." The closing move matters. "I'd like to get clearer on that" is an invitation — it signals that Tsega has something to learn from the conversation, that hearing how Professor Huang is seeing the timeline is part of why she is raising it. An opener that frames the disagreement neutrally and then closes with "I need you to understand why this is a problem" has not made the move. The frame and the invitation belong together.

The invitation is only genuine when the speaker means it. This is the hardest part. A speaker who has composed a third-story opener while remaining privately certain they are right is using the form without holding the stance behind it. The other person can usually feel this — a technically correct framing delivered without genuine openness reads as manipulation, and it can be more corrosive than an honest first-story opener, because it obscures the speaker's actual intent while appearing to be fair-minded. The third-story opener depends on the speaker actually believing their version is incomplete. If writing the neutral opener feels dishonest — if it feels like pretending not to know something you already know — the question is not how to phrase the opening. It is whether the learning stance is genuinely in place.

There are things the third-story opener cannot fix. The first is a situation where acknowledgment needs to come before inquiry. Some conversations involve a genuine and deep need to feel heard — to have the other person register what happened, not to jointly explore whether it happened. A third-story opener in those situations can feel evasive, as though the speaker is more interested in being procedurally fair than in understanding what the other person has been through. The form may be correct while the relational sequence is wrong. The second is a structural power gap. When a significant authority differential exists — an employee approaching a decision-maker, a student approaching a professor with final say — "joint inquiry" can be nominal in a way both parties know. The opener frames the conversation as collaborative; the underlying structure may determine the outcome regardless. The third-story opener cannot reframe a dynamic that is more powerful than any conversational framing.

The third story gets the conversation started. What happens next — how to actually hear what the other person is saying once both parties are in the room together — is a different question, and a different chapter.
