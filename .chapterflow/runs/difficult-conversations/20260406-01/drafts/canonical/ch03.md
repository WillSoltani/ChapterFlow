# Don't Assume They Meant It

Andre has written three responses to Soleil's message. All three start the same way: "I didn't mean..." He has not sent any of them. Not because they are wrong — he genuinely did not mean anything by the joke — but because each draft feels like it is missing the point in a way he cannot quite identify. He is correct about his intention. He is also, somehow, getting further from where he needs to be.

The problem is not what he meant. The problem is that he is treating what he meant as the conversation he needs to have — when what Soleil needs is a different conversation first.

Intent and impact are not the same thing. Most people treat them as if they are. We judge ourselves by what we meant, and we judge others by what they did. Both judgments assume that intent and impact are the same event read two different ways. They are not. They are separate things that can run in any combination: good intent with bad impact, bad intent with good impact, intent unknown and impact real. The confusion produces two specific failure modes, and most difficult conversations contain at least one of them.

The first failure mode is on the receiving end. When someone does something that hurts us, we assemble a story about why — almost immediately, and usually without awareness that we are doing it. The intention we construct for the other person is nearly always worse than the real one. We react to the constructed intention as if it were confirmed. The other person, unaware that they have been assigned an intention, is defending against a charge they did not know had been filed. The conversation cannot move because both people are arguing about different things: one person is arguing about what happened, and the other is arguing about what they meant — which was never on the table to begin with.

The second failure mode is on the delivering end, and it is the one that does more damage. When something we have said or done has caused harm, the instinct is to lead with the intent defense: "I didn't mean it that way." That statement may be true. It is also useless for the person who was hurt, because it does not address what happened to them. It closes the impact conversation before it has opened. The other person does not need to know, first, that the harm was not intended. They need to know that the harm was registered.

Three weeks before this moment, Rodrigo had canceled plans with Celia three times in five weeks. She told him it felt like he did not want to spend time with her. His first response was to explain his work situation: the project, the deadlines, how overwhelmed he had been. All true. She had not asked about the project. She had said something about what his behavior had meant to her. He spent four sentences explaining something she had not contested. The conversation ended in a worse place than it started. When Rodrigo finally stopped explaining and said "I hear that it's felt like I'm pulling away — that makes sense to say" — the conversation changed. Not resolved. Changed.

The move is to separate intent and impact rather than merge them. On the receiving end: notice when you are inventing the other person's intention rather than knowing it. Ask what you actually know about what they meant versus what you have assembled from the gap between what you expected and what happened. On the delivering end: acknowledge the impact before you explain the intent. Not instead of — before. "I can see that landed badly" is different from "I can see that landed badly, but I want you to know..." The second sentence uses the acknowledgment as a runway for the same defense. The first one leaves the impact in the room as something real, not something to explain away.

This is harder than it sounds. The reason most people lead with the intent defense is not carelessness — it is that they are trying to make the impact less real. If the harm was unintentional, it feels like it should count for less. The other person is still experiencing it as real regardless. The act of acknowledging the impact without immediately qualifying it with the intent is the specific move that allows the conversation to stay open.

What Andre needed to send was not a fourth draft starting with "I didn't mean." It was something that could acknowledge, without defense, that Soleil's discomfort was real and that he wanted to understand it. "I can hear that it made you uncomfortable, and I'm sorry it landed that way. Can we talk about what happened?" That sentence does not require him to have intended harm. It requires him to let the impact be real before he explains the intent.

Once intent and impact are separated, a different question becomes available: not who is to blame for what happened, but what each person has been doing that keeps producing this kind of situation. That is the territory the next chapter opens.
