# Problem-Solving: Take the Lead

Farah and Miles had finally stopped having the wrong argument. For weeks the hiring conversation in their business had been a personality fight in disguise. Miles sounded reckless to Farah. Farah sounded fearful to Miles. Now, after enough real listening, each could finally say the other person's concern back in a way that did not feel mocking. Payroll risk was real. Burnout and missed growth were real. The fight was quieter. Nothing was solved.

That is a distinct stage in a difficult conversation. Sometimes understanding is the work. Sometimes expression is the work. But there are also moments when the question has to change from "what is true here?" to "what do we do about it?" If no one takes the lead at that stage, the conversation peters out into relief without change. Both people leave feeling as though something important happened because something honest did happen. A week later the same problem is still sitting exactly where it was.

The opposite failure is just as common. One person, usually the one who raised the issue, takes over the problem-solving and presents an answer. The tone may even sound collaborative. But the structure is unilateral. The proposal arrives already finished, and the other person is left with two roles: endorse it or resist it. Many people endorse it on the surface and undermine it later in practice because they had no hand in designing it. That later resistance often gets misread as laziness or bad faith, when the deeper problem is that agreement was extracted before ownership existed.

The useful move is to take the lead on process without taking control of the outcome. That means proposing a joint inquiry into solutions rather than a solution. The sentence sounds different. Not "here is what we should do," but "here is one thing I am thinking might help; what am I missing, and what would you add?" The point is not false humility. The point is to make design shared from the beginning. A plan made together has a chance of being lived with. A plan handed down usually has to be enforced.

Farah and Miles only got unstuck when they stopped treating the first option named as the side to defend. Hire now had become Miles's side. Wait had become Farah's. As long as those were the only choices, each new sentence strengthened a camp. The problem-solving move was to invent options before evaluating any one of them. A contractor for three months. A full-time hire with a conservative offer. A deferred hire triggered by a specific revenue number. Once there were several possibilities on the table, the conversation stopped being a contest between two identities and became a design problem again. The first option no longer had the power to become the trench.

Generating options is only half the move. If each person simply ranks the options according to preference, the fight returns in new clothing. The next step is to ask what standard both people would accept in advance. What would a reasonable hiring policy look like for a business at their size? What level of payroll exposure would be responsible? What level of owner exhaustion would be irresponsible? Those questions matter because they move the discussion off "your way versus my way" and onto criteria both can inspect. A shared standard does not remove disagreement. It gives disagreement a fairer object.

The same logic applies when the other person's idea arrives first. The easy mistake is to treat their proposal as the new enemy. Better to treat it as raw material. "What I like in your idea is that it protects cash. The part I want to change is that it leaves the workload untouched. What if we keep the revenue trigger but use a contractor until then?" Building on a proposal is not politeness. It is how joint inquiry stays joint. Attack freezes design. Modification keeps it moving.

Then the conversation has to become action. "We should revisit this soon" feels responsible and usually means nothing. A useful close names what will happen, who owns it, and when it will be checked. Farah will call two contractors by Thursday. Miles will model the cash impact of three hiring options by Friday. They will compare numbers on Monday morning and decide whether the revenue-trigger plan still makes the most sense. Specificity is not bureaucracy here. It is the bridge between insight and behavior.

But specificity has a hard edge, and this is where the move can quietly go wrong. Mira sees it in a meeting with Professor Rowan about recurring teaching-section chaos. Rowan wants a clean finish: Mira will confirm room coverage, attendance overflow, and revised discussion-group assignments by next Wednesday. The problem is that Mira does not control room assignments or late enrollment shifts. She can promise to send the updated roster she has, flag conflicts early, and notify Rowan the moment the department changes the schedule. She cannot honestly promise that the schedule itself will stay fixed. A specific commitment that outruns real control is not stronger problem-solving. It is false closure purchased with a weaker person's anxiety.

That limit matters because problem-solving language can become a new way of avoiding reality. The urge to solve can arrive too early, before enough listening has happened, and it can arrive too hard, pressing people into promises that make the meeting feel complete while setting them up to fail later. Good process does not mean every conversation ends in a plan. It means that when a plan is needed, the plan is jointly designed and tied to what the people in the room can actually do.

Once that becomes possible, the whole sequence is finally visible. A difficult conversation can be prepared for, opened, listened through, spoken through, and turned toward action without any one of those stages replacing the others. That is the integration the final move has to hold.
