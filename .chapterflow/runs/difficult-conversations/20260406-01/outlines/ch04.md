Chapter Promise:
By the end of this chapter, the reader can take a situation they have been framing as blame — who is at fault, who deserves to apologize — and reframe it as a contribution map: what is each person doing that keeps this pattern going, and what would it take for each to do something different.

---

Opening Move:
Open on Dara, the remote employee, composing a message to HR. She has been composing it for three days. The subject line keeps changing: first "Complaint," then "Concern," then she deletes the draft entirely. She is not sure whether what is happening to her counts as a problem worth reporting. Her manager Felix is not doing anything wrong exactly — he is just watching her too closely, asking too many questions, adding her to threads that have nothing to do with her work. She does not know how to name what is going on. She knows who she blames. She does not know what to do about it.

---

Anchor Allocation:
- Anchor 1 (Dara / Felix — remote work cycle): P1 (hook), P5 (surfaces the contribution system), P7 (names what changes if both map their contribution)
- Anchor 2 (Marta / Sione — sibling holiday pattern): P4 (extends mechanism to long-running personal roles)
- Anchor 3 (Yemi / Kwame — style intersection): P3 (illustrates how contribution without malice still produces harm)

---

Paragraph Job Map:
- P1: Hook — Dara composing the message to HR. The reader knows this feeling: the certainty about who is to blame and the uncertainty about what to do about it.
- P2: Name the failure of blame. Blame is not wrong — it is often accurate about who did what. It is useless for what comes next, because it asks who deserves punishment rather than what needs to change.
- P3: Introduce contribution. Not "everyone is equally to blame" — contribution does not pretend equality. It asks what each person did that kept the pattern going. Yemi/Kwame: neither is wrong, the combination is wrong.
- P4: Name the four contribution types. Marta/Sione anchor runs through the problematic-role-assumption type as its primary lens. Brief pass through the others.
- P5: Return to Dara/Felix. Both are contributing. Dara's withdrawal triggered Felix's anxiety and produced the oversight. Felix's oversight produced the withdrawal. The system is producing the outcome, not one villain.
- P6: The asymmetry. Contributions are rarely equal. Naming your 10% contribution when the split is 90/10 is the chapter's hardest move, and it does not require pretending the 90% does not exist.
- P7: What changes when Dara maps her contribution. She does not send the message to HR. She sends a message to Felix instead: "I've been less communicative than usual, and I think that's made things harder for both of us. Can we talk?"
- P8: Bridge — once contribution is mapped, the feelings that surface from seeing your own role need to be addressed before they derail the conversation. That is what the next chapter is about.

---

Required Distinction or Mechanism:
The distinction between blame and contribution is not "be nicer" or "stop being angry." It is a temporal and practical shift: blame is about the past (who deserves punishment), contribution is about the present and future (what is each person doing right now that keeps this going, and what can each person change). Contribution is also not symmetry — contributions are often unequal. The move is to name what each person contributed, regardless of proportion, because each person's contribution is something they can actually change.

---

Hard-Depth Tension:
The contribution framing is not universally safe. In high-power-gap situations, asking the less-powerful party to identify their contribution can serve to deflect accountability from the more powerful party. A manager who uses contribution-mapping with someone who raised a legitimate concern may be using the language of the chapter to avoid their own accountability. Hard depth must hold this explicitly: contribution-mapping is a genuine tool AND it has asymmetric risks depending on who is doing it and in what context.

---

Threshold Question:
When one party's contribution is 10% and the other's is 90%, naming the 10% feels like it reduces the 90%. How do you hold both true simultaneously: "I contributed to this" AND "the other person's contribution was much larger" AND "naming mine is still the right move"?

Hard Takeaway Topics:
- T1: Blame asks who deserves punishment; contribution asks what each person can change
- T2: Contributions are rarely equal — contribution-mapping is not the same as assigning 50/50 responsibility
- T3: Four contribution types: avoiding-until-now, unapproachability, style intersections, role assumptions
- T4: The contribution system — both people are playing roles in a pattern that keeps producing the same outcome
- T5: Naming your contribution even when it is small — because your contribution is what you can actually change
- T6 (hard only): When contribution-mapping has asymmetric risk — high-power-gap situations where the move can be used to deflect accountability

Scenario Lesson Map:
- Scenario 1 (decision_point, work): Dara deciding whether to send the message to HR or the message to Felix. The decision: who is the complaint about, or what is the pattern about? Teaches: reframing the target of the conversation changes what the conversation can produce. Ending: decision_reframe.
- Scenario 2 (postmortem, school): A group project that stalled. In the postmortem, the group diagnoses a "free-rider" — Nola did the least work. The hidden cost: no one considers what the group dynamics were that made it easy for Nola to under-contribute, or what each other member did that enabled the pattern. Teaches (hidden_cost): contribution analysis applies in all directions, not just to the most visible contributor.
- Scenario 3 (dialogue, personal): Marta and Sione having the conversation they have never had — Marta naming her contribution (she has been so competent at logistics that Sione never had to develop any logistics capacity), Sione naming his (he has been so conflict-averse that he let the burden stay with Marta). Teaches (relationship_turn): naming contributions without blame opens space for both people to change.
- Scenario 4 (predict_reveal, work): Reader predicts whether Yemi can resolve the tension with Kwame by raising his complaint (Kwame is too slow). Reveal: the complaint is accurate and also misses Kwame's contribution analysis (Yemi's "done" deliverables are rough and require extensive revision, which is why Kwame is careful). Teaches (broader_principle): the complaint that is accurate from one angle may miss the other's contribution.
- Scenario 5 (dilemma, school): Isabel is deciding whether to tell her project group that she thinks the current leader, Sage, contributed to the group's underperformance. Her hesitation: naming Sage's contribution will feel like blame. The dilemma: how to raise contribution without it landing as blame. Teaches (self_diagnosis): the difference between framing as contribution vs. blame is available to the reader to practice.
- Scenario 6 (before_after, personal): Dani and her partner. Before: Dani's framing of the recurring fight is "you never initiate connection." After: she names her own contribution (she has built a life that runs on scheduled meetings, including emotional ones, and her partner cannot connect on a schedule). Teaches (behavior_shift): recognizing your contribution to the pattern shifts what you can ask of the other person.

---

Genericity Risks:
- Risk 1: The chapter becomes "everyone is responsible for everything" — flattening unequal contributions into false parity.
- Risk 2: Contribution is presented as equivalent to apology or blame. The specific distinction is that contribution is forward-looking (what can I change) while blame is backward-looking (who deserves punishment).
- Risk 3: The power-gap asymmetry is omitted, making contribution-mapping sound universally safe and straightforward.

---

Forbidden Drift:
- Suggesting that identifying contribution resolves questions of accountability or harm
- Using intent/impact language (Ch03 territory) in contribution framing — "I didn't mean to contribute" is not the chapter's move
- Identity conversation framing (Ch06 territory)
- Suggesting that naming contribution is the same as forgiving or excusing the other person
- Brief-language leak: no "avoiding-until-now," "unapproachable," "role assumptions" as meta-terms in reader-facing content where they would read as jargon
