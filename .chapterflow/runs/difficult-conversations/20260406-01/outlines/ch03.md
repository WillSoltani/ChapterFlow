Chapter Promise:
By the end of this chapter, the reader can take a situation where something they said landed badly — or something said to them landed badly — and separate what actually happened in each lane (intent, impact) without conflating them.

---

Opening Move:
Open on the manager's scenario — Kai, who made a joke in a team meeting that landed badly, is now in their car after work looking at a message from the engineer, Miriam, on their phone. The message says the comment made her uncomfortable. Kai's first draft response is already almost written in their head: "I didn't mean anything by it." That draft is sincere. And it is also going to make things worse.

Paragraph Job Map:
- P1: Hook — Kai on their phone, "I didn't mean anything by it" already formed. The reader knows this response. They also know how it usually goes.
- P2: The confusion the chapter addresses: people treat intent and impact as the same thing. The two failure modes.
- P3: The receiver-side failure: inventing an intention. Usually worse than the real one. Reacting to the invented version.
- P4: The deliverer-side failure — and why it is harder and more important. "I didn't mean it" is a statement about intent. The engineer was not asking about intent. She was reporting impact. The intent defense does not address the impact, and the other person knows it.
- P5: The partners anchor — same structure in a personal relationship. Each conversation about the cancellations ends with a defense of intent that does not address impact, and the gap widens.
- P6: The disentangle move. Hold both separately. Acknowledge the impact as real without making it evidence for the intent. Name the intent without claiming it resolves the impact.
- P7: Return to Kai. What changes when Kai responds to the impact before explaining the intent.
- P8: Bridge — once intent and impact are separated, the next question becomes: what did each person contribute to producing the situation? That is Ch04.

---

Required Distinction or Mechanism:
The distinction between intent and impact is not just "be aware of how things land." The mechanism is that these are two separately real things that can run in any combination: good intent / bad impact, bad intent / good impact, both good, both bad. Most people treat them as the same thing in two directions: defending yourself by pointing to your intent, and accusing others by pointing to their impact. The disentangle move holds both without using either to cancel the other.

---

Hard-Depth Tension:
The chapter's primary use case is unintentional harm — good intent, bad impact. The disentangle move is designed for that case. Hard depth must address the asymmetric case: what happens when the intent was in fact harmful? The framework still applies — intent and impact are still separate — but now the deliverer's job is to acknowledge the intent rather than defend against it. That is a harder and different conversation. Hard depth holds this without resolving it neatly.

---

Threshold Question:
When taking responsibility for impact — "I can hear that this landed badly, and I'm sorry for the impact" — what prevents that acknowledgment from being heard as an admission of malicious intent? The person offering the acknowledgment knows they meant well. The other person may not know that yet. How do you hold "I take responsibility for the impact" and "my intent was not harmful" simultaneously without either statement canceling the other?

Hard Takeaway Topics:
- T1: Intent and impact are two separate things that can run in any combination
- T2: Receiver-side failure: inventing the worst possible intent and reacting to the invented version
- T3: Deliverer-side failure: using "I didn't mean it" as if it resolved the impact (it does not)
- T4: The disentangle move: acknowledge impact without making it evidence for intent; name intent without using it to close the impact conversation
- T5: Sequence matters: acknowledging the impact before explaining the intent changes what the other person can hear
- T6 (hard only): The asymmetric case — when intent was genuinely harmful, the disentangle move still applies but in the opposite direction: acknowledging intent rather than defending against it

- Scenario 4 (predict_reveal, work): Reader predicts whether Lars can resolve a tense exchange with a client by clarifying what he actually meant. Reveal: the clarification of intent is heard as minimization of impact. Teaches (broader_principle): intent clarification before impact acknowledgment almost always backfires.

Her hesitation: if she says it was hurtful, will the classmate take it as an accusation of bad intent? ---

Genericity Risks:
- Risk 1: The chapter becomes general advice to "be empathetic" or "consider other people's feelings." The specific mechanism is the disentangle move, not emotional sensitivity.
- Risk 2: The deliverer-side move is presented as easy once you understand it. It is not. Taking responsibility for impact while not accepting blame for intent requires holding two claims simultaneously that most people experience as contradictory.
- Risk 3: The asymmetric case (harmful intent) is left out entirely. This would make the chapter feel incomplete for readers whose experience includes genuinely harmful behavior.

---

Forbidden Drift:
- Adding contribution mapping (Ch04)
- Using identity quake or AND/BOTH language (Ch06 territory)
- Suggesting that acknowledging impact requires an apology (acknowledgment and apology are different)
- Framing the disentangle move as a communication technique rather than a genuine internal move
- Brief-language leak: no "receiver-side failure," "deliverer-side failure," "asymmetric case" in reader-facing content
