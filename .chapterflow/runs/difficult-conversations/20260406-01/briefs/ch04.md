Core Claim:
Blame is the language of past judgment — it focuses on who deserves punishment or apology. Contribution is the language of present-tense change — what is each person doing that keeps the situation stuck, and what would it take for each to do something different. Replacing blame with contribution-mapping surfaces the system producing the problem instead of the villain producing it. This makes change possible in ways that blame never does, because blame asks who deserves punishment while contribution asks what each person can do differently starting now.

---

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
The chapter's sharpest insight is not the shift from blame to contribution — it is the asymmetry of contributions. Contributions are almost never equal, and the chapter is explicit about this. The move is not to pretend each person contributed equally (that is its own form of dishonesty) but to look at what each person did, regardless of proportion. A person who contributed 10% is still in the system, and their 10% is still available for them to change. The teaching treatment that flattens this into "you both contributed" misses the chapter's weight.

---

Author Logic Chain:
First, the authors establish why blame fails: it focuses on the past (who deserves punishment) when the real question is the future (what needs to change). It also produces defensiveness in the blamed person and reduces the blamer's own culpability from view.
Then, they introduce the contribution framing: each person is playing a role in a system that keeps producing the same outcome, and each person's contribution is legible if you look.
Then, they name the four common contribution types, each of which is invisible from inside it.
Then, they handle the asymmetric case explicitly: contributions can be 90/10, and naming your 10% does not erase the other person's 90%.

Required Anchors:

Anchor 1 — The remote employee and the micromanaging manager:
A remote employee, Priya, has been going silent on her check-ins for three months — submitting work without comment, not responding to her manager's questions, withdrawing from the team channel. Her manager, Felix, has responded by adding more check-ins, asking more questions, requesting more updates, and adding her to Slack threads she wasn't on before. Both experience the other as the problem: Priya experiences Felix's increasing oversight as controlling; Felix experiences Priya's silence as a performance concern. Both are right about the other's behavior and blind to their own. Priya's withdrawal contributed to Felix's anxiety and triggered the oversight. Felix's oversight contributed to Priya's sense of being treated as untrustworthy and triggered the withdrawal.
- Concrete detail: Priya has stopped speaking in Thursday standups entirely. Felix has added a second weekly 1:1 specifically to ask about her workload.
- Friction: Each interprets the other's escalation as confirmation of the original story.
- Consequence: Three months in, the pattern is worse than it was, and neither has named it.

Anchor 2 — The siblings and the holiday:
Two siblings, Marta and Sione, have the same holiday fight every year. Marta always raises the logistics (who handles travel, who pays for what, who visits their parents, when). Sione always defends the current arrangement as fine. By the end of every conversation, Marta has been cast as the one who always complains and Sione as the one who never helps. Both roles are ones neither chose and both maintain. Marta's role: she has been so competent at logistics for so long that Sione has never had to develop any logistics capacity, which Marta takes as evidence of Sione's apathy. Sione's role: he has been so conflict-averse that he has let Marta carry the weight for years, which Sione takes as "that's just how we've always done it."
- Concrete detail: This year's fight starts in October, before any holidays are even near.
- Friction: Marta's competence invisibly guarantees that Sione never has to care.
- Consequence: The pattern has been running for a decade. Neither person has named it as a pattern.

Anchor 3 — The co-workers and the handoff:
Two teammates, Yemi and Kwame, have been frustrated with each other's work style for a project cycle. Yemi delivers work early but with rough edges and expects Kwame to catch the remaining issues. Kwame delivers comprehensive deliverables but later than expected and expects Yemi to wait. Neither has told the other. They have built stories about each other: Yemi thinks Kwame is slow; Kwame thinks Yemi is careless. The friction point is not malice but the intersection of two work styles that produce incompatible expectations.
- Concrete detail: Last quarter's deliverable was late because Kwame was waiting for Yemi to "finalize" a document Yemi considered done.
- Friction: Each person's style is not wrong on its own; the combination produces the outcome.
- Consequence: They are now assigned to the same project again, and both have requested to the manager (separately) to be reassigned.

Frameworks or Terms Introduced:
1. **Blame vs. contribution** — the core diagnostic: blame looks backward for punishment, contribution looks forward for change.
2. **The four contribution types** — avoiding-until-now, being unapproachable, style intersections, problematic role assumptions. Each is a specific pattern that is usually invisible from inside it.
3. **The contribution system** — the idea that both people are playing roles in a system producing the same outcome, and the system is the target of change, not the villain.

---

Specific Applications:
1. When stuck in a recurring fight: ask what each person keeps doing that guarantees the same result. What is your role in maintaining the pattern?
2. When you are clearly less at fault: acknowledge what you contributed (even if it is much smaller) without treating that acknowledgment as an equalization of the responsibility.
3. When you are clearly more at fault: naming your contribution does not protect you from also needing to acknowledge the impact on the other person. These are separate conversations.

---

Common Misreadings:
1. **Contribution means everyone is equally to blame.** Explicitly not. Contributions are often 90/10. The chapter's move is not to flatten responsibility but to look at what each person can change, regardless of proportion.
2. **Naming your contribution means you accept the blame for what the other person did.** It does not. "I contributed by avoiding the conversation for six months" does not mean "therefore everything that went wrong is my fault."
3. **Contribution mapping is only useful after the fact.** It is most useful as a diagnostic for why a pattern keeps recurring — before the next escalation, not as a postmortem after the blowup.

---

Counterarguments or Limits:
The contribution framing has a real limit: in situations where the power differential is significant, asking the less-powerful party to identify their contribution can become a tool for deflecting accountability from the more powerful party. A manager who uses contribution-mapping with a direct report who raised a harassment concern is not doing the chapter's move — they are using its language to avoid accountability. The hard depth must hold this: contribution-mapping is not a universally safe move; it has asymmetric risks depending on who is doing it and in what context.

Additionally, the "avoiding-until-now" contribution can be read as blaming the person who was afraid to raise an issue. The chapter must be clear that this contribution is named for learning purposes, not as a way of re-assigning fault to the person who was least powerful in the situation.

---

Previous Chapter Bridge: The intent/impact disentangle from Ch03 is the prerequisite for contribution-mapping. A person who leads with their intention as defense cannot map their contribution honestly, because they are protecting a fixed version of themselves. Contribution-mapping requires the learning stance from Ch02: holding your own role as a perspective that might be incomplete.

Next Chapter Bridge: Once contribution is mapped, the feelings conversation often surfaces: the recognition of one's own role in producing harm can produce guilt, shame, grief, or relief. Ch05 addresses what to do when feelings are in the room.

---

Cross-Chapter Tensions:
1. Naming contribution (Ch04) can feel like accepting blame for intent (Ch03). The language matters: "I contributed to this outcome" ≠ "I intended this outcome" ≠ "I am solely to blame."
2. The unapproachability contribution (one person made it hard to raise the issue) connects to the identity conversation from Ch01: the person who is unapproachable is often running an identity conversation about their own competence or goodness that makes criticism feel like an attack.

---

Moral Complexity:
YES. The move of naming your own contribution is ethically complex when the power gap is significant, when the situation involved real harm, and when the contributions are highly unequal. The teaching prose must not flatten this. Naming your 10% contribution in a 90/10 situation is different from taking 50% responsibility. The chapter holds both as simultaneously true: contributions are real AND they are not equal AND naming yours is still the right move.

---

Hard-Depth Minimum:
When contributions are genuinely unequal — when the split is more like 90/10 — naming your 10% is still the chapter's move, but the ethical stakes change. A person who contributed 10% to a situation that caused serious harm must hold two things simultaneously: "I caused some of this" AND "the proportion of cause matters and does not disappear." Hard depth must name this tension explicitly and hold it without resolving it neatly.
