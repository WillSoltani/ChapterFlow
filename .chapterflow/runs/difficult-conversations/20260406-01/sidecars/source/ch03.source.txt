CORE CLAIM (permitted to paraphrase, not quote):
People judge themselves by their intent ("I didn't mean it") and other people by the impact of their actions ("I don't care what you meant — this is how it landed"). Both judgments are wrong in the same way: they treat intent and impact as the same thing when they are two separate things that can run in any combination.

Two failure modes:
1. On the receiving end: inventing an intention that is almost always worse than the real one, then reacting to the invented version as if it were confirmed.
2. On the delivering end: using "I didn't mean it" as if that defense resolved the impact, when it does not address the impact at all.

The move: disentangle the two. Acknowledge the impact as real without using it as evidence for harmful intent. Name the intent without using it to close the impact conversation.

===========================================================
THE TWO FAILURE MODES:

RECEIVER-SIDE FAILURE — INVENTING INTENT:
When someone does something that hurts us, we unconsciously construct a story about why they did it. That story almost always assigns the worst plausible intention. We react to the invented intention as if it were confirmed, when we actually do not know what the other person intended. The correction: ask what you actually know about their intent versus what you are assuming. What would make a reasonable person do this?

DELIVERER-SIDE FAILURE — INTENT AS DEFENSE:
When we have done something that hurt someone, our first response is often "I didn't mean it" or "I didn't intend that." The problem: this is a statement about intent. The other person is reporting an impact. Intent and impact are separate. A defense of intent does not address the impact. The other person has already experienced the impact regardless of what was intended. Saying "I didn't mean it" repeatedly makes the other person feel more dismissed, not less, because the defense keeps talking past the impact.

THE DELIVERER-SIDE MOVE (the harder and more important one):
Acknowledge the impact as real — "I can hear that this landed badly" or "I can see that had a real effect on you" — before or instead of immediately explaining intent. This does not mean:
- Accepting blame for intentional harm (only acknowledging that the impact is real)
- Never explaining your intent (you can explain it — just after, not before, and not as a defense)
- Agreeing that you did something wrong in a moral sense

It means separating two things that are both true: the impact happened AND the intent was not harmful (if that is true). Both of those are real. Neither cancels the other.

SEQUENCE MATTERS:
Intent explanation before impact acknowledgment almost always makes things worse. The other person hears the intent explanation as: you are more interested in defending yourself than in addressing what happened to me. Impact acknowledgment before intent explanation creates the space for intent to be heard: because the other person does not feel their experience is being dismissed.

PERMITTED ANCHORS:

ANCHOR 1 — MANAGER'S OFFHAND COMMENT (fresh):
A manager (Kai) makes a joke in a team meeting about a junior engineer's (Miriam's) accent. Miriam goes quiet for the rest of the meeting and messages Kai that evening saying the comment made her uncomfortable. Kai's first draft response is "I didn't mean anything by it." Kai genuinely means this. The draft response is also going to make things worse, because it does not address Miriam's experience — it dismisses it.

ANCHOR 2 — PARTNER'S CANCELLATIONS (fresh):
One partner (Hayden) has canceled three plans in a row due to work pressure. The other partner (Soleil) says it feels like Hayden does not want to spend time with them. Hayden's response: "That's not true — I do want to spend time with you, I've just been overwhelmed." This is a statement of intent. Soleil asked about impact. The intent statement does not address the impact, and Soleil hears it as another instance of not being heard.

ANCHOR 3 — EMAIL OMISSION (fresh):
A project volunteer accidentally leaves a colleague off an email chain about a decision that affects the colleague's work. The colleague finds out from someone else, at a team meeting, in front of others. The volunteer keeps explaining "it was an honest mistake." The colleague is not questioning whether it was intentional — the colleague is experiencing the impact of being excluded from a decision about their own project.

===========================================================
ASYMMETRIC CASE (hard depth only):
The chapter's primary framework addresses unintentional harm: good intent, bad impact. Hard depth must address what happens when intent was genuinely harmful. The disentangle move still applies — intent and impact are still separate — but now the deliverer's job is to acknowledge the intent rather than defend against it. "You're right that I was trying to hurt you with that comment" is a different conversation than "I didn't mean it." The framework does not collapse, but it becomes more demanding. The chapter does not resolve what the right response to acknowledged harmful intent looks like. That is an honest limit.

TEACHING IMPLICATIONS:
The deliverer-side move is the chapter's hardest and most important. Do not present it as easy once you understand it. Holding "I caused harm AND I did not intend harm" simultaneously runs against the instinct to defend intent. The teaching prose should honor this difficulty.
