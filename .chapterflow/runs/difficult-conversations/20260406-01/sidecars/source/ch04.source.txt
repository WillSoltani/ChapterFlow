PERMITTED CONTENT

Core distinction:
- Blame is the language of past judgment. It focuses on who deserves punishment or apology. It looks backward.
- Contribution is the language of present-tense change. It asks what each person is doing right now that keeps the situation going, and what each could do differently. It looks forward.
- The practical difference: blame asks who was wrong; contribution asks what could change starting today.
- Blame almost always produces defensiveness in the blamed person and removes the blamer's own role from view. It rarely changes anything.

The four contribution types:
1. Avoiding until now — contributing by not raising the issue earlier when it would have been easier. The longer the issue was avoided, the more the pattern had to calcify.
2. Being unapproachable — contributing by making it hard for the other person to raise something. Signals: defensiveness, volatility, history of dismissing concerns, or treating certain topics as off-limits. The person who is unapproachable often does not know it.
3. Style intersections — contributing through work styles, communication patterns, or relational preferences that are not wrong on their own but that produce friction when combined with someone whose style is different. Neither person is the problem; the combination is.
4. Problematic role assumptions — contributing by playing fixed roles in an interaction that guarantee the same outcome. The classic patterns: the helper and the helpless (where the helper's competence keeps the other person from developing capacity), the critic and the defender (where criticism triggers defense which triggers more criticism), the avoider and the pursuer (where one person's withdrawal triggers the other's escalation).

The contribution system:
- Both people are playing roles in a system that keeps producing the same outcome.
- The system, not the person, is the target of change.
- Once you see the system, you can look for the move that disrupts it rather than looking for the villain to hold responsible.

The asymmetric case:
- Contributions are rarely equal. Most situations are more like 70/30 or 90/10.
- Contribution-mapping is not a claim that both people contributed equally.
- Naming your 10% contribution does not erase the other person's 90%. Both can be true simultaneously.
- The reason to name your 10% anyway: it is what you can actually change. The other person's 90% is not in your direct control; yours is.

The avoiding-until-now contribution:
- This is one of the most common but least acknowledged contribution types.
- When someone raises a concern after a long period of not raising it, the delay is itself part of the pattern. The concern that could have been addressed six months ago has now calcified.
- Naming this contribution is not the same as blaming the person for not raising it earlier — it may have been dangerous or impractical to raise it then. It is naming the effect of the delay as part of the contribution system.

PERMITTED ANCHORS

Anchor 1 — Remote employee / manager cycle:
- Employee withdraws (goes quiet in check-ins, stops volunteering information, becomes performatively efficient).
- Manager increases oversight (adds check-ins, sends more questions, adds employee to Slack threads, requests more status updates).
- Employee experiences the oversight as evidence of distrust; withdraws further.
- Manager experiences the silence as a performance concern; adds more oversight.
- Both are right about the other's behavior. Neither is seeing their own contribution to the cycle.
- Withdrawal contribution: creates the conditions for anxiety that produce the oversight.
- Oversight contribution: confirms the distrust the employee was worried about, which produces more withdrawal.
- The system: the same move each time, producing the same outcome each time.

Anchor 2 — Sibling holiday pattern:
- One sibling (call them the organizer) handles all logistics, has always handled all logistics, and carries a mounting resentment about it.
- The other sibling (call them the disengaged) benefits from the organizer's competence and has never had to develop logistics capacity.
- The organizer's contribution: their competence has invisibly enabled the disengagement. The more competent they are, the less the other sibling has to care.
- The disengaged sibling's contribution: their avoidance of conflict has let the burden stay with the organizer. Their non-role is itself a role.
- The pattern: the organizer raises the issue as a complaint, the disengaged sibling defends, both leave with the same roles intact.
- What breaks it: naming the system both people are playing into, not just the person who contributed more.

Anchor 3 — Style intersection:
- One person delivers work early and expects the other to catch remaining issues. They consider the work "done" when it is structurally complete.
- The other person delivers later but comprehensively. They consider work "done" when it requires no further iteration.
- The intersection: the first person's output does not meet the second person's standards for handoff; the second person's timeline does not meet the first person's expectations for completion.
- Neither style is wrong. The combination produces a predictable conflict that neither person recognizes as a contribution pattern.
- They have built separate stories: the fast-and-rough person thinks the slow-and-comprehensive person is a perfectionist; the slow-and-comprehensive person thinks the fast-and-rough person is careless.
- The contribution: both maintained incompatible expectations without naming them.

LIMITS

The writer may not:
- Suggest that naming your contribution is the same as forgiving or excusing the other person's behavior.
- Suggest that contributions are equal or that the chapter argues for false parity.
- Claim that contribution-mapping resolves questions of accountability in high-harm or high-power-gap situations.
- Introduce contribution-mapping as a management or HR technique (too narrow).
- Reference specific academic research by name or citation.
- Use the identity conversation framing from Ch06 — that territory is reserved.
- Use intent/impact language as if it were contribution language — these are different frames.
