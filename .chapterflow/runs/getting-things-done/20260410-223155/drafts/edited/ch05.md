You can tell collection is incomplete by what keeps following you around. The thought about the reimbursement form returns in the grocery line. A receipt is still in your jacket pocket. A sticky note is half-hidden under a folder at work. A school paper is folded into the backpack pocket you always forget to check. None of these items is large on its own. Together they keep proving the same thing: some of your life is still living loose.

That is the pressure Chapter 5 attacks. Allen calls this loose material your stuff, not to be casual, but to stay broad. Stuff includes papers, notes, reminders, mental loops, errands, requests, and all the other incompletes that have not yet entered a trusted place. The chapter's core argument is that partial capture does not solve this problem. If enough material remains scattered, the mind keeps scanning for leftovers and the system never earns full trust.

This is why collection has to be more comprehensive than most people want. Selective capture feels efficient because it lets you grab what seems important and leave the rest for later. Allen's point is that the leftover material does not stay quiet. It keeps leaking attention. A few uncaptured pockets, surfaces, and mental reminders are enough to keep the sense that something is still out there, unresolved, waiting to surprise you later.

Chapter 4 prepared the conditions for this move. Once time, space, and tools are ready, collection becomes a full sweep. The goal is not clever filtering. It is to gather the loose material that has been living in too many places. Physical incompletes matter here, receipts, papers, notes, forms, mail, objects left where they should not be. Mental incompletes matter too, promises to call someone, items to buy, things to check, obligations that keep replaying because they never landed in the system.

That breadth matters because collection is not yet processing. The chapter is not asking the reader to decide what every item means while gathering it. That comes later. Collection does a narrower but harder job: get the material out of hiding and into one trusted capture path. If you start deciding too early, the sweep slows down, attention fragments, and whole categories of loose stuff stay behind. Gathering has to stay gathering.

This is one reason Allen pushes toward a trusted in-basket. Trust does not come only from having a tray or list. It comes from knowing that the loose material has actually entered it. A nearly complete system can still feel incomplete if enough items remain in side pockets, side piles, and side thoughts. The mind keeps a quiet suspicion that something important is still outside the official flow.

The chapter therefore makes a stronger claim than "write things down." It asks for a temporary seriousness about corralling the incompletes. Empty the backpack. Check the drawer. Pull the scraps together. Notice what keeps repeating mentally. Bring it all to one place where it can later be processed cleanly. The relief comes less from any one item than from the shrinking of the unknown remainder.

The failure mode is easy to recognize once you see it. A person collects some items, feels slightly better, and assumes the job is done. But the uncaptured material keeps surfacing in random moments, which means the mind never fully stops guarding against omissions. Partial collection can reduce noise a little while preserving the structure of distraction.

Allen's logic is demanding here because honest gathering often feels messier before it feels cleaner. A real sweep can temporarily enlarge the pile in front of you. That is not regression. It is visibility. The system is finally seeing what the mind was carrying in fragments.

That sets up the next chapter cleanly. Once the stuff is actually in front of you, what do you do with it? Collection gathers the field. Processing has to take it down to empty.
