The system can be current, trustworthy, and still leave one hard question unanswered: what do you do right now? Several actions may be valid. More than one may matter. But the moment itself has limits. You are in a certain place, with a certain amount of time, a certain level of energy, and a certain sense of what matters most. Chapter 9 steps into that live moment.

Allen's point is that doing in GTD is not just having a list and then hoping the top item reveals itself. It is choosing from a trusted system according to present conditions. The system matters because it gives the menu. But the menu still has to be narrowed by what the moment can actually support.

This is why context becomes load-bearing. Some actions fit where you are, and some do not. A phone call, a laptop task, and an errand may all be worth doing, but they are not equally available in every setting. GTD does not remove that reality. It uses it. Context filters the live options so the choice becomes more concrete.

Time matters next because the same list looks different in a ten-minute gap than it does in an open afternoon. A task that makes sense with room to think may be the wrong choice if only a few minutes are available. The chapter pushes against the fantasy that the best action is always the same regardless of the size of the window.

Energy matters for the same reason. A low-energy evening and a sharp morning do not offer the same working conditions. Allen's logic does not treat that as weakness. It treats it as part of the real decision environment. Choosing well means noticing what kind of action the present level of energy can honestly support.

Priority still matters, but inside those real limits. The most important item in the abstract may still not be the best next move in this context, with this time, at this energy level. Chapter 9 does not erase importance. It puts importance into conversation with actual conditions so the choice can be both intelligent and doable.

That is why doing is more practical than people sometimes expect. GTD does not promise one universal action formula floating above life. It offers filters that help narrow the best available move now. Higher horizons may frame why things matter, but the ground game still depends on what can really be done from the current moment.

The failure mode is easy to recognize. A person keeps choosing as if present conditions do not count. They select by abstract importance alone, then stall because the action does not fit where they are, the time they have, or the energy they can bring. The problem is not lack of priorities. It is a refusal to let live conditions shape the choice.

Allen's stronger claim is that a trusted system makes this kind of choice possible without panic. Once the lists are current, the choice does not have to come from vague mood or pressure. It can come from a narrower, more grounded question: given where I am, how much time I have, what energy I have, and what matters, what is the best next move?
