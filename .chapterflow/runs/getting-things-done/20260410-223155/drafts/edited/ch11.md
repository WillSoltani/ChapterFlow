Obligations can crowd attention before any action happens. A call to return, a form to fill out, a book to buy, a school note, a task at work. None of those items may be moving yet, but each still pulls lightly on the mind while it remains uncollected. Chapter 11 explains why the collection habit matters at that earlier stage.

Allen's emphasis is plain and easy to miss. Capture gets things out of your head. The point is not to show commitment by remembering more. The point is to stop using mental space as storage for reminders, obligations, and loose inputs that could live somewhere more reliable. Once they leave the head, attention changes even before the rest of the workflow is complete.

That is where psychic drag becomes a useful description. Internal reminders do not wait quietly. They keep asking for a little energy. They make the mind rehearse, scan, and recheck. The drag may not look dramatic from the outside, but it thickens ordinary experience. Collection reduces that friction by moving open loops into an external place that can be trusted.

The relief matters because it can arrive before clarification, organization, or action. A person may not yet know exactly what to do with an input. It may still need processing later. But the act of catching it somewhere outside the head already changes the feel of the load. The reminder no longer has to stay mentally alive every minute in order not to disappear.

That does not mean collection is the whole method. Allen is not claiming that capture finishes the work. A captured item can still become clutter if it is never clarified or reviewed. The chapter's point is narrower and stronger. Collection has power because it removes a major source of internal friction at the start. It gives later stages a cleaner place to operate.

That is why the habit matters, not just the tool. Anyone can write something down once. The principle becomes powerful when capture is regular enough that the mind begins to trust it. Open loops do not have to stay in memory because there is a dependable path for catching them. The gain is not only logistical order. It is a different quality of attention.

The failure mode is familiar. Someone carries errands, follow-ups, ideas, and obligations in memory because they seem too small to record. The result is not strength. It is lingering drag. Attention keeps getting tapped by reminders that have no stable place to land. The person then wonders why life feels heavier than the visible workload alone would justify.

Chapter 11 turns back to the foundation underneath the practical mechanics. Collection is powerful because it changes the relationship between attention and open loops. When commitments move out of the head and into a trusted capture point, the system begins by reducing strain instead of demanding more mental juggling.

That is the bridge into the next principle. Once collection has cleared space, the question becomes what to decide about what was captured. Chapter 12 moves from the power of getting things out of the head to the power of determining the next action.
