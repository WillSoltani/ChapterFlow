The pile can look better than it feels. Everything is finally gathered in one place, but each time you look at it, you touch a few items, read a few lines, and put them back down without being any clearer about what they are. A note from a meeting stays a note. A flyer stays a flyer. A receipt stays a receipt. Collection has succeeded, but the basket is still mentally heavy because the material has not been decided.

That is the pressure Chapter 6 addresses. Allen shifts from gathering to processing, which means clarifying what each collected item actually represents. The chapter's point is not that the pile needs more attention in general. It needs decisions. Until each input is processed, the in-basket remains a holding area full of unresolved meaning.

This is why the chapter insists on one item at a time. Looking at the whole pile may feel responsible, but it often creates only a vague sense of work. Processing works by taking one paper, one note, one reminder, and asking what it is. Then the next item gets the same treatment. The method slows the eye down so the mind stops skating over inputs without converting them into clear next moves.

The key split inside that move is whether the item is actionable or not. Some inputs point toward action. Others do not. A handout may become reference. A receipt may become trash. A reminder card may need action later or no action at all. The chapter's discipline is not sophisticated sorting for its own sake. It is deciding what category of response the item actually deserves instead of leaving it in a vague maybe state.

That distinction matters because people often mistake rereading for processing. They look through the in-basket, recognize the items, and feel briefly engaged with them. But familiarity is not clarity. If the same note keeps getting touched without a decision, the system has not moved forward. Rehandling creates the sensation of involvement while preserving the burden of indecision.

Allen's standard is stricter than "look at everything regularly." He wants the basket taken to empty. Empty does not mean all work is finished. It means the raw input container is no longer holding undecided material. Each item has been clarified and routed according to what it is. The basket stops being a parking lot for thought and starts functioning as a temporary intake point.

That is why processing comes after collection and before organizing. Collection gathered the field. Processing decides what each item means. Only then can later buckets do their job. If you skip this chapter's discipline, the organizing system gets fed with haze instead of decisions, or the in-basket turns into a familiar stack that is reviewed, shuffled, and trusted less each day.

The failure mode is common because it looks so ordinary. Someone empties pockets, mail, and notes into one place, feels virtuous about the sweep, then keeps returning to the same pile without pushing items through the actionable split. Nothing explodes. The basket just stays quietly dense. The mind keeps sensing unfinished interpretation sitting in front of it.

Processing therefore asks for a different kind of honesty than collection did. Collection admitted that loose material existed. Processing admits that each item now requires a decision. One piece at a time, the pile has to stop being a stack of inputs and become a set of clarified outcomes.

That sets up the next chapter directly. Once each item has been clarified, where does it belong? Organizing will answer that. Processing has to make sure there is something definite to place there.
