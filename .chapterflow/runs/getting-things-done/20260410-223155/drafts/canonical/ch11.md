You can feel crowded by obligations before doing anything about them. A call to return, a form to fill out, a book to buy, a note from school, a task at work. None of those items may be moving yet, but each one still pulls lightly on attention while it stays uncollected. Chapter 11 explains why the collection habit matters at that earlier point.

Allen's emphasis is simple and easy to underestimate. Capture gets things out of your head. The point is not to prove that you care by remembering more. The point is to stop using mental space as storage for commitments, reminders, and loose inputs that could live somewhere more reliable. Once they leave the head, attention changes even before the rest of the workflow is complete.

That is where the idea of psychic drag becomes useful. Internal reminders do not only wait quietly. They keep asking for a little energy. They make the mind rehearse, scan, and recheck. The drag may not look dramatic from the outside, but it thickens ordinary experience. Collection reduces that friction by moving open loops into an external place that can be trusted.

The relief matters because it arrives before clarification, organization, or action. A person may not yet know exactly what to do with an input. It may still need processing later. But the simple act of catching it somewhere outside the head already changes the feel of the load. The reminder stops needing to stay mentally alive every minute in order not to disappear.

That does not mean collection is the whole method. Allen is not saying that capture finishes the work. A captured item can still become clutter if it never gets clarified or reviewed. The chapter's claim is narrower and stronger. Collection has power because it removes one major source of internal friction at the start. It gives later stages a chance to work on cleaner ground.

That is why the habit matters, not just the tool. Anyone can write something down once. The principle becomes powerful when capture is regular enough that the mind begins to trust it. Open loops do not have to stay in memory because there is a dependable path for catching them. The gain is not only logistical order. It is a different quality of attention.

The failure mode is familiar. Someone tries to carry errands, follow-ups, ideas, and obligations in memory because they seem too small to record. The result is not strength. It is lingering drag. Attention keeps getting tapped by reminders that have no stable place to land. The person then wonders why things feel heavier than the visible workload alone would justify.

Chapter 11 turns the reader back to the foundation underneath all the practical mechanics. Collection is powerful because it changes the relationship between attention and open loops. When commitments move out of the head and into a trusted capture point, the system begins by reducing strain instead of adding more mental juggling.

That is the bridge into the next principle. Once collection has cleared space, the question becomes what to decide about what was captured. Chapter 12 moves from the power of getting things out of the head to the power of determining the next action.
