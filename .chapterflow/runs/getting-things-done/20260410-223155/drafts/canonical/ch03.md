The meeting goes sideways in a familiar way. Someone says, "Let's just start throwing out ideas," and within minutes the whiteboard is crowded with fragments. Marketing angle. Vendor option. Timeline concern. Budget issue. Possible venue. New feature. Nobody knows what counts as a good outcome yet, but the room is already producing answers to questions it has not clearly asked.

Allen treats that pattern as backward planning. In Chapter 2, the problem was trying to choose actions before inputs had been clarified. Here the problem is trying to solve a project before the project has been properly imagined. His claim is that the mind already knows how to plan better than that. The natural planning model follows a sequence: purpose and principles, outcome visioning, brainstorming, organizing, and next actions.

Purpose and principles come first because projects need a reason and a standard before they need tactics. Why are we doing this? What has to stay true while we do it? Without those answers, effort becomes noisy fast. People can work hard on a project and still drift because the project has no governing center. Allen's point is not grand philosophy. It is practical containment. Purpose holds the work together when choices start branching.

Then comes outcome visioning. What would done actually look like? What would success feel like in concrete terms? Allen argues that projects become easier to move when the end state is visible enough to guide selection and attention. A vague wish to "improve onboarding" or "plan the trip" does not yet tell the mind what to look for. A clearer picture, the first successful week for a new hire, the flights booked, the hotel confirmed, the itinerary shared, begins narrowing the field. It makes later thinking less random.

Only after that does brainstorming earn its place. Allen is not against idea generation. He is against doing it too early. Brainstorming works when there is already a purpose to serve and an outcome to build toward. Then the flood of ideas has shape. Without that frame, brainstorming often produces one of two bad results: a pile of disconnected thoughts, or a false feeling of progress because the group sounded energetic. Energy is not the same thing as direction.

Organizing is what keeps the brainstorm from staying a pile. Once ideas exist, they need grouping, sequencing, and structure. What belongs together? What is a subcomponent? What has to happen first? What is merely interesting, and what is actually part of the plan? Allen is pushing the project toward legibility here. A project does not become calmer because it has more notes. It becomes calmer because the notes begin forming a shape that can be navigated.

Next actions complete the model by reconnecting planning to movement. This is where Chapter 3 locks back into the rest of GTD. A project can have purpose, a vivid outcome, a strong brainstorm, and a clean structure, and still sit still if nobody has defined the next visible move. Allen's planning model does not replace next-action discipline. It produces better next actions because the project behind them is now clearer.

The dining-out example makes the point almost deceptively simple. A person decides to go out to dinner. That desire supplies purpose. Standards such as convenience or quality supply principles. The evening is imagined. Ideas emerge about time, clothes, transportation, and reservation details. Those ideas get organized. Then someone makes the call or books the ride. Allen uses the example to show that ordinary planning already follows a natural arc when it is not forced out of order.

That contrast with unnatural planning is the chapter's real edge. Many projects go wrong not because people lack ideas, but because they start from the wrong phase. They brainstorm before naming the outcome. They debate tactics before deciding the standard. They admire the vision without translating it into structure. Or they generate a beautiful plan and still fail to name the next action. In every case, the project feels heavier than it needs to feel because it never crossed the right thresholds in the right order.

The promise of the chapter is not creativity for its own sake. It is relaxed control. Allen wants planning to feel both imaginative and grounded, loose enough to generate options, disciplined enough to convert them into movement. The hard limit is already visible: if outcome visioning turns dreamy, or brainstorming arrives before the project has a center, the model collapses into noise. But when the sequence holds, the project stops behaving like one big vague obligation and starts behaving like a series of intelligible moves.
