Core Claim:
Control improves when incoming obligations move through a defined workflow instead of staying as undifferentiated input. Allen's five stages, capture, clarify, organize, reflect, and engage, turn stress into decisions by separating collection from interpretation and action choice. The chapter argues that people often try to decide what to do before they have defined what they have. Weekly review and trusted buckets keep the system from decaying back into piles.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
This is the architecture chapter. It converts Chapter 1's diagnosis into a repeatable workflow and introduces the sorting decisions that make GTD operational rather than inspirational.

Author Logic Chain:
First the author lays out the five stages of workflow mastery.
Then the author shows that every input must be processed item by item into actionable or non-actionable categories.
Then the author concludes that reflection and engagement only work after capture, clarification, and organization have been done honestly.

Required Anchors:
- The five-stage workflow: capture, clarify, organize, reflect, engage
- The actionable split: do it, delegate it, or defer it, with the two-minute rule
- The non-actionable split: trash, incubate, or reference, plus Weekly Review as the maintenance hinge

Counterarguments or Limits:
The workflow can turn rigid if treated like bureaucracy instead of clarity. The chapter should keep the distinction between simple processing discipline and perfectionism. It should also preserve that choosing what to do still depends on judgment after the workflow has defined the landscape.
