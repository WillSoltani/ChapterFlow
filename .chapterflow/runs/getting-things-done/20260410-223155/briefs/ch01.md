Core Claim:
Modern knowledge work creates too many open loops for the mind to track cleanly on its own. Stress rises when commitments remain unresolved in mental RAM instead of being captured in a trusted system. The chapter argues that productivity starts not with bigger goals but with getting commitments out of the head and into visible next actions. Bottom-up control is presented as the practical path toward clearer attention and calmer execution.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
This is the book's problem-setting chapter. It introduces the overload condition, defines open loops, introduces mind like water, and reframes productivity as trusted externalization plus next-action clarity instead of motivation or discipline talk.

Author Logic Chain:
First the author establishes that modern work has become boundaryless and cognitively overloaded.
Then the author shows that unresolved commitments occupy attention as open loops when they are not captured and clarified.
Then the author concludes that a trusted external system and defined next actions are the route toward clear, responsive attention.

Required Anchors:
- The 21st-century knowledge-work overload frame: constant inputs, loose work boundaries, too much to do, not enough clarity
- Open loops clogging short-term attention unless they are stored in a trusted system
- The concrete exercise of choosing a real project and writing the next physical action

Frameworks or Terms Introduced:
- open loops: unresolved commitments that keep consuming attention
- mind like water: a calm, appropriately responsive mental state
- next action: the next visible physical move that advances a project
- bottom-up control: solving ground-level commitments before claiming higher-order clarity

Specific Applications:
- turning a vague project into one visible next step
- reducing background anxiety by externalizing commitments
- noticing that high-level goals do not help when the next move is undefined

Common Misreadings:
- GTD is mainly a motivational system rather than a decision-and-capture system
- more planning at the abstract level will solve overload without ground-level clarification

Counterarguments or Limits:
External capture alone does not solve overload if no next action is defined. Big-picture thinking still matters, but Chapter 1 argues it is not enough when day-to-day commitments remain mentally loose. The chapter should not imply that all stress comes from poor organization rather than real workload.
