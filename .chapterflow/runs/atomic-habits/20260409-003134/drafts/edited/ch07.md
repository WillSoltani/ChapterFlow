The phone is on the bedside table again. The vending machine still waits at the same hallway turn. The distracting tab opens the moment the browser starts. Then the person says, with real frustration, that they need more self-control. Chapter 7 begins by challenging that explanation. Often the problem is not that the person keeps failing some heroic inner test. The problem is that the tempting cue keeps showing up on time, every time.

That is why Clear reframes self-control so sharply. People who seem disciplined are not always better at fighting temptation in the moment. Many of them simply spend less time in situations that keep pulling the same bad habit back into view. The cue appears less often, so the fight appears less often. What looks like strong self-control from the outside may often be better exposure design from the inside.

This matters because cues keep habits active. A bad habit does not need a philosophical argument to survive. It often just needs to be seen again. The glowing screen, the familiar route past the vending machine, the open snack shelf, the app already waiting on the home screen: each cue gives the behavior another chance to wake up. The chapter stays practical by showing that repeated exposure creates repeated struggle.

That is why cue removal and cue avoidance matter so much. If the temptation appears less often, the bad habit has fewer chances to begin. Move the phone away from the bed. Keep the browser from opening on the distracting tab. Change the route that passes the same cue every afternoon. Put the tempting food somewhere that does not keep entering sight. The chapter is not glorifying avoidance as weakness. It is showing that many reliable wins happen earlier than the dramatic moment of resistance people usually admire.

That point gives the chapter its edge. Heroic resistance can look impressive, but a quieter design often works better. A person who never puts the cue in front of themselves may look less dramatic than someone who keeps resisting it face to face, yet the quieter person may be exercising the more stable form of discipline. The chapter is asking the reader to stop confusing visible struggle with superior self-control.

The limit matters, too. Chapter 7 does not say that resistance is imaginary or that agency disappears once structure improves. Temptation can still appear. Choices still have to be made. The narrower claim is that repeated self-control becomes more reliable when repeated temptation is not constantly reintroduced. That makes the chapter stronger, not weaker. It is not denying discipline. It is explaining how discipline is often protected.

This also completes the first law cleanly. Chapter 4 made cues visible. Chapter 5 placed cues deliberately. Chapter 6 arranged environments so the right cue could stand out. Chapter 7 shows why bad cues should often be seen less, not resisted more often at close range. Once that logic is complete, the next question changes. If visibility and exposure explain so much, what explains why some behaviors still feel more desirable than others? That is the opening for Chapter 8.
