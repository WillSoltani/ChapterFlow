Many good habits ask for work now and pay later, while many bad habits offer relief now and send the bill into the future. Chapter 15 opens inside that tension. The chapter argues that repetition is strongly shaped by whatever feels satisfying in the short term. A behavior that pays back immediately has a structural advantage over one that only promises a distant benefit.

That is the force behind the cardinal rule of behavior change. Rewarded behavior gets repeated. The chapter keeps the point narrow and practical. A person does not need a grand theory of discipline to see it. If an action ends with some felt payoff, closure, relief, pride, progress, or pleasure, it becomes easier for the brain to mark that action as worth doing again.

This is why immediate outcomes matter so much. Human behavior is often steered less by what will be best in six months than by what feels good, finished, or relieving in the next few seconds. Chapter 15 does not deny the importance of long-term goals. It explains why long-term goals so often lose in daily competition when they arrive without any immediate sign that the effort was worthwhile.

Bad habits often exploit that asymmetry. Scrolling, snacking, avoiding, and other familiar escapes tend to reward immediately even when they create larger costs later. The pleasure or relief shows up now, so repetition becomes easy to understand. The person may know the trade is bad overall, but the short-term payoff still gives the behavior momentum.

Good habits face the opposite problem. Reading, practicing, saving, training, and other worthwhile routines often deliver their biggest rewards far in the future. Early on, the effort can feel like pure cost. That is why the chapter argues that good habits need some immediate reinforcement if they are going to stick reliably. A completion mark, a visible sign of progress, a satisfying ritual ending, or some other immediate signal can keep the behavior from feeling like a one-sided sacrifice.

The chapter still keeps one limit in view. Satisfaction is not the whole story, and reinforcement does not have to mean shallow bribery. The deeper point is alignment. If the habit can carry even a small immediate payoff while its larger benefits grow in the background, repetition becomes more realistic. The person is no longer being asked to suffer through a long stretch of invisible progress with nothing felt in the present.

That is why Chapter 15 matters after the environment work of Chapter 14. A habit can be easier to start and easier to find, yet still fail to repeat if it never feels rewarding when completed. Setup changes whether the action happens. Satisfaction changes whether the action wants to happen again. That is the bridge into Chapter 16, where visible tracking can turn immediate reinforcement into something repeatable and concrete.
