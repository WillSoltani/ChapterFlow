The cue is visible, but the habit still does not start. The shoes are by the door. The notebook is on the desk. The reading lamp is already on. Yet nothing happens until the routine is linked to something the person actually wants. Chapter 8 begins by showing that visibility is not the whole story. A cue can be obvious and still weak if it does not point toward a desired state.

That is why Clear moves from cue to craving. In the habit loop, the cue introduces the possibility, but craving supplies the pull. People do not act just because something is present in front of them. They act because the cue starts to promise relief, enjoyment, satisfaction, progress, connection, or some other internal change they want. The chapter stays strongest when it keeps that distinction clean. Noticing is one thing. Wanting is another.

This matters because anticipation can make a behavior attractive before the action even begins. A walk starts to feel easier when it is also the time for a favorite podcast. A report block becomes less cold when it begins with a cafe playlist and familiar drink. A study session stops feeling like pure obligation when the opening ritual itself becomes desirable. The chapter is showing that desire often gathers around the association before the habit arrives at the reward.

That is where temptation bundling enters. Instead of waiting for discipline to power a useful habit by itself, the person pairs the needed action with something already appealing. The wanted behavior does not replace the needed one. It helps carry it. The walk comes with the podcast. The admin block begins with the music ritual. The reading session starts under the lamp with the tea already prepared. Attraction is being designed, not merely hoped for.

This gives the chapter its practical edge. A good habit often fails not because the cue is hidden, but because the cue is emotionally flat compared with the alternatives. The phone promises stimulation now. The cafeteria detour promises social energy now. The entertainment tab promises relief now. The needed routine may remain visible, but visibility alone does not make it competitive. The chapter is asking how to make the better habit feel more compelling before the first step.

The limit matters here too. Chapter 8 does not say that attraction can solve every habit problem or that people can program desire without friction or context. Bundling helps, but it does not erase structure, fatigue, or competing incentives. The narrower claim is that cues become stronger when they are tied to anticipation and desirable association. That makes the chapter more useful because it avoids magical language while still giving the reader real leverage.

That also explains why the second law opens here. Chapter 7 reduced the number of bad cues that keep demanding resistance. Chapter 8 asks a different question: once the cue remains, what makes it feel magnetic enough to move behavior? The answer is not visibility by itself. It is craving, anticipation, and the attractive meaning attached to the act. From there, the next question widens naturally. If attraction shapes habits this much, what happens when families, friends, and groups start shaping attraction too? That is the opening for Chapter 9.
