A habit can feel easier without yet feeling unavoidable. Chapter 14 pushes the 3rd Law one step further by asking what happens when the room, desk, or daily setup keeps placing the good behavior directly in front of the person. The chapter argues that habits become far more likely when the environment makes the good action hard to ignore and the bad action unusually hard to perform.

That is why setup matters so much here. A book already open on the desk, materials already laid out in the studio room, or the needed tool already waiting at the point of use can make the next move feel almost obvious. The chapter is no longer only about lowering friction on a single step. It is about shaping the whole environment so the desired behavior keeps appearing as the natural next action.

Availability is the key mechanism. People often repeat what is ready, visible, and nearby. If the good habit is already present in the room, it asks for less searching, less negotiation, and less self-persuasion. The chapter stays strongest when it keeps this claim narrow. Environment does not make choice disappear, but it can change the odds dramatically by deciding what is most available at the moment of action.

The same principle works against bad habits. A behavior loses power when the environment blocks it more strongly than before. A distracting device in another room, a locked drawer, a removed shortcut, or a setup that forces several extra steps can make the unwanted action far less likely in the moment. The chapter speaks in near-absolute language because strong barriers can make the bad move feel almost impossible compared with its old easy access.

This is what makes inevitability a stronger claim than ease alone. The good habit is not merely possible. It is already waiting. The bad habit is not merely discouraged. It is obstructed enough that constant self-control is asked to do less work. Chapter 14 therefore teaches a more ambitious form of design: pre-stage the good action until it keeps presenting itself, and block the bad action until it stops presenting itself so cheaply.

The chapter still keeps one limit in view. Environment control is powerful, but it is not perfection. No setup removes all choice, and no barrier guarantees flawless behavior forever. The narrower claim is stronger than that anyway. Better setup shifts the odds so far that the person no longer has to rely on fresh willpower every single time. That is enough to change what becomes normal.

That bridge matters for what comes next. Chapter 13 made the opening move smaller. Chapter 14 makes the setup harder to ignore. Once the behavior is easier to begin and more available in the environment, the next question becomes what makes it feel rewarding enough to repeat again. That is the opening for Chapter 15.
