The right game can still lose its pull if it becomes either dull or crushing. Chapter 19 begins there. The chapter argues that motivation lasts longest when the challenge level stays in a narrow middle zone: difficult enough to require attention, but manageable enough to keep success believable.

That is the Goldilocks Rule. A task that is too easy produces boredom. A task that is too hard produces discouragement. The most engaging work sits near the edge of current ability, where the person has to stretch without feeling hopeless or numb.

This changes how the reader should think about motivation. Motivation is not only a matter of character or inspiration. It is also shaped by task design. A habit can die because it stopped asking enough, or because it started asking far too much. In both cases, the person loses the feeling that the next repetition is worth entering.

That is why calibrated difficulty matters so much. Once a habit becomes automatic, the challenge may need to rise slightly to stay interesting. If repeated failure starts to pile up, the challenge may need to drop so confidence and rhythm can return. The chapter becomes strongest when it treats adjustment as a normal part of maintenance rather than as weakness.

The limit matters because the chapter is not a call for constant novelty or endless escalation. Easy tasks still have a place, and not every day will feel perfectly calibrated. The narrower claim is that long-term engagement improves when the challenge usually stays near the person's current edge instead of drifting into boredom or overload.

That is why Chapter 19 follows Chapter 18 so naturally. Fit gives the reader the right field. The Goldilocks Rule explains how to stay alive and motivated inside that field. The book is now asking not only where effort should go, but how difficulty should be tuned so that effort keeps returning.

That bridge matters for Chapter 20. Once a person finds a suitable challenge zone and repeats it long enough, a new danger appears: the habit can become so automatic that attention fades. That is the next problem the book turns to.
