Chapter Title: How to Find and Fix the Causes of Your Bad Habits
Position In Book: close of the 2nd Law section; turns from attraction and norms toward diagnosing what a bad habit is promising and reframing the cue at the level of cause
Source Sidecar Path: sidecars/source/ch10.source.txt

Core Claim:
Bad habits often persist because they appear to solve an immediate problem or promise a desired feeling. The chapter argues that better change comes from identifying the underlying cause the habit seems to address and then reframing the routine around a healthier response.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
It is the cause-diagnosis chapter. It should feel impossible to confuse with Chapter 9 because the emphasis is no longer on group attraction. The emphasis is on reading the bad habit as a response to a perceived need, then changing the interpretation and the path.

Author Logic Chain:
First the author asks what the bad habit appears to solve.
Then the author reframes cravings as signals that can be interpreted differently.
Then the author shows how awareness and reinterpretation can weaken the attraction of the bad habit while strengthening a better response.

Required Anchors:
- bad habits often look attractive because they seem to address an immediate need or feeling
- the underlying cause matters more than the surface behavior alone
- reframing the cue and the response can reduce the pull of the bad habit

Frameworks or Terms Introduced:
Underlying cause; problem-solution lens; reinterpretation; craving reframing; healthier response path.

Specific Applications:
- see stress-snacking as a response to tension rather than hunger alone
- see scrolling as a response to boredom or avoidance rather than as neutral downtime
- map a substitute routine to the real need being felt

Common Misreadings:
- turning the chapter into therapy claims it does not support
- pretending awareness alone instantly fixes every bad habit
- drifting into later law-3 or law-4 mechanics as the main solution

Counterarguments or Limits:
The chapter should not imply that bad habits disappear once a cause is named. The narrower claim is that identifying the underlying need helps weaken the bad habit's story and opens room for a better response.

Previous Chapter Bridge: Chapter 9 showed how norms can make a bad habit feel normal; Chapter 10 asks what the habit is really doing underneath that social cover.
Next Chapter Bridge: Chapter 11 can shift to the 3rd Law and focus on making good habits easier.

Cross-Chapter Tensions:
- a bad habit can feel socially normal while also serving a private need
- reframing the cause helps attraction change, but it does not replace later ease and friction design

Moral Complexity:
Medium; the chapter can drift into blame or false cure language if it is not kept narrow.

Concept Budget:
- target concept count: 4
- rationale: underlying cause, reinterpretation, perceived solution, and healthier substitute are enough for the chapter's core.

Hard-Depth Minimum:
Preserve the tension that the bad habit may be solving something real, even if badly. Hard depth must keep alive the question of how to respect the real problem without accepting the current solution.

Unsupported Zones:
- no invented psychology studies or numbers
- no therapeutic overclaiming
- no drift into full law-3 friction engineering
- no moral language about weakness or purity
