Core Claim:
What looks like strong self-control is often better exposure design. People with solid habits do not always resist temptation more heroically. They often encounter the tempting cue less often because the environment keeps it out of immediate view.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
It is the self-control-through-exposure chapter. It should feel impossible to confuse with Chapter 6 because the emphasis is no longer on making good cues visible. The emphasis is on keeping bad cues from dominating attention often enough to trigger repeated struggle.

Author Logic Chain:
First the author challenges the myth that successful people simply battle temptation better in every moment.
Then the author shows that repeated exposure to cues keeps bad habits active.
Then the author reframes self-control as a design problem in which fewer tempting cues can beat repeated resistance.

Required Anchors:
- apparent self-control often reflects reduced exposure to temptation
- bad habits stay active when their cues remain visible and frequent
- removing or avoiding cues can outperform repeated in-the-moment resistance

Frameworks or Terms Introduced:
Temptation exposure; cue avoidance; self-control as design; fewer tempting situations; attention protection.

Specific Applications:
- keep distracting apps and tabs out of the first visual field
- remove junk food from visible home surfaces
- avoid routes or contexts that repeatedly cue the bad habit

Common Misreadings:
- treating the chapter as moral praise for avoidance
- turning it into a claim that willpower never matters
- drifting into full friction engineering before the later law-3 chapters

Counterarguments or Limits:
The chapter should not deny that resistance exists. The narrower claim is that reliable self-control often comes from reducing repeated exposure to tempting cues rather than repeatedly winning the same fight at close range.

Previous Chapter Bridge: Chapter 6 made environment design visible; Chapter 7 shows why one major payoff of that design is encountering temptation less often.
Next Chapter Bridge: once the first law is complete, Chapter 8 can widen from cue and exposure design into attraction and craving.

Cross-Chapter Tensions:
- visibility can support good habits while cue removal can weaken bad ones
- self-control can be structural without becoming a denial of human agency

Moral Complexity:
Medium; the chapter can sound virtuous or moralizing if it is not kept structural.

Concept Budget:
- target concept count: 4
- rationale: exposure frequency, tempting cues, cue removal, and self-control as structure are enough for the chapter's core.

Hard-Depth Minimum:
Preserve the tension that self-control still exists, but many repeated wins come from not entering the same tempting setup in the first place. Hard depth must keep alive the question of what it means to be disciplined when the fight is prevented early instead of heroically won late.
