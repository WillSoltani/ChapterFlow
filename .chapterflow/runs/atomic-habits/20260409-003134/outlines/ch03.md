Chapter Promise:
Readers will understand cue, craving, response, and reward as the practical loop behind habits and will see the Four Laws as direct interventions on that loop.

Opening Move:
Open on an everyday action that happens so quickly it feels like no decision happened, then reveal the hidden sequence beneath it.

Anchor Allocation:
- anchor 1 used in paragraph(s): P2-P4 for cue, craving, response, reward
- anchor 2 used in paragraph(s): P4-P6 for the loop as the operating engine of later chapters
- anchor 3 used in paragraph(s): P5-P7 for the Four Laws and their inversion logic

Paragraph Job Map:
- P1: make automatic behavior feel fast enough to hide its own sequence
- P2: define cue and craving cleanly
- P3: define response and reward cleanly
- P4: show why the four-step model is useful as a practical loop
- P5: map the Four Laws onto the loop
- P6: explain why the model helps diagnose a weak or failing habit
- P7: preserve the limit that the model is useful but not magic
- P8 if needed: bridge to cue awareness and Chapter 4

Required Distinction or Mechanism:
The chapter distinguishes the four jobs in the loop and shows that later advice is just targeted intervention on those jobs.

Hard-Depth Tension:
A clean loop is powerful because it simplifies reality, but any real habit still has to be diagnosed concretely rather than treated as if the labels do the work by themselves.

Hard Test:
When a habit fails, can you identify which step in the loop is weak instead of calling the whole problem a lack of discipline?

Takeaway Count Lock:
- Easy: 3 (always)
- Medium: 5
- Hard: 5

Hard Takeaway Topics:
- cue, craving, response, reward as distinct jobs
- the Four Laws mapped to the loop
- later chapters as expansions rather than disconnected tips
- habit diagnosis by weak link
- model usefulness versus overclaiming

Scenario Lesson Map:
- Scenario 1 (decision_point, work): identify the missing cue in a recurring work routine
- Scenario 2 (postmortem, school): exam-prep habit failed because the loop never got installed clearly
- Scenario 3 (dialogue, personal): snack routine diagnosed through the four steps
- Scenario 4 (predict_reveal, work): friction looked like motivation failure but was really a response-step problem
- Scenario 5 (dilemma, school): robotics practice choice between vague intention and visible trigger
- Scenario 6 (before_after, personal): morning stretch works once cue and reward become obvious

Scenario Vividness Targets:
At least four scenarios should include object-level details such as a calendar alert, a notebook margin, a bowl on the counter, a robotics cart, running shoes by the mat, or a browser tab pinned at the same time every day.

Preview Constraint:
The preview should ask what must change first if cues drive the loop.

Genericity Risks:

- later-law advice spilling in too early

Forbidden Drift:
- vague motivational language
- unsupported brain claims
- teaching later chapters instead of installing the engine
- brief-language leakage
