Chapter Promise:
Readers will understand that a bad habit often persists because it appears to solve a problem, and that better change begins by identifying and reframing that underlying cause.

Opening Move:
Open on someone who thinks they simply have a bad habit, then reveal that the habit keeps returning because it seems to answer a real feeling or problem.

Anchor Allocation:
- anchor 1 used in paragraph(s): P1-P3 for bad habits as perceived solutions
- anchor 2 used in paragraph(s): P3-P5 for underlying causes and reinterpretation
- anchor 3 used in paragraph(s): P5-P7 for building a healthier response path

Paragraph Job Map:
- P1: shift from surface habit to underlying cause
- P2: explain why bad habits feel useful in the moment
- P3: show how reinterpretation weakens automatic obedience
- P4: connect cause diagnosis to better substitute responses
- P5: show why naming the real problem changes the bad habit story
- P6: preserve the limit that awareness alone is not total cure
- P7 if needed: bridge into Chapter 11 and the 3rd Law

Required Distinction or Mechanism:
The chapter distinguishes the visible habit from the problem it seems to solve. The person changes more effectively once the cause is named correctly.

Hard-Depth Tension:
The bad habit may really be serving something, even if poorly. The chapter should keep the tension between respecting the underlying need and rejecting the current solution.

Hard Test:
What changes when the bad habit is no longer seen as random weakness but as a flawed answer to a real problem?

Takeaway Count Lock:
- Easy: 3 (always)
- Medium: 5
- Hard: 5

Hard Takeaway Topics:
- bad habits often act like problem-solvers
- the underlying cause matters more than the surface behavior alone
- reinterpretation can weaken a habit's attraction
- healthier substitutes should address the real need
- awareness helps without becoming a total cure

Scenario Lesson Map:
- Scenario 1 (decision_point, work): Nora reframes stress-snacking during a deadline as tension relief, not hunger alone
- Scenario 2 (postmortem, school): Dev sees that repeated scrolling at the bus stop is solving boredom and avoidance
- Scenario 3 (dialogue, personal): Camila names late-night browsing as a response to mental overload and restlessness
- Scenario 4 (predict_reveal, work): Eli learns the real issue behind his constant inbox checking was uncertainty, not diligence
- Scenario 5 (dilemma, school): Piper chooses between obeying a craving and responding to the real need underneath it
- Scenario 6 (before_after, personal): Soren shifts a comfort habit by building a substitute that addresses the original feeling better

Scenario Vividness Targets:
At least four scenarios should include object-level details such as a snack drawer, bus stop bench, open inbox tab, study corner, notebook prompt, or evening browser glow.

Preview Constraint:
The preview should point toward Chapter 11 by asking what happens once the right behavior is chosen but still needs to become easier to do.

Genericity Risks:
- vague self-awareness talk
- implying instant cure through insight alone
- drifting into therapy or neuroscience overreach

Forbidden Drift:
- unsupported source detail
- cure-all language
- drifting into later law-3 mechanics as the main answer
- brief-language leakage
