Big goals make people uneasy long before they make them work. Small goals sound sensible. They seem safer, humbler, and easier to fit into the life a person already has. That is why Chapter 9 matters. Keller treats “big is bad” as a lie because people often distrust scale before they have even examined whether the larger aim is legitimate. The discomfort gets mistaken for wisdom.

That is the chapter's first correction. Size itself is not the problem. The real issue is the reflex that treats a larger target as automatically arrogant, unstable, or unrealistic. People cut the goal down until it stops disturbing their routines, identity, and social plausibility. The result can still sound responsible, but the chapter asks whether the smaller target was chosen because it was wiser or because it was easier to live with.

This is where comfort-sized ambition appears. A person keeps trimming the project, the standard, or the life aim until it no longer forces sharper tradeoffs. The goal becomes easier to explain, easier to imagine completing, and easier to pursue without changing much. Keller does not praise vague grandiosity instead. He attacks the habit of shrinking legitimate scale just to protect comfort.

That is also why the chapter belongs after the balance lie. Some people accept that real priorities create uneven seasons, then quietly choose smaller goals so life will not tilt too much in the first place. Chapter 9 exposes that move. The problem is not always laziness. Sometimes it is a polished preference for aims that leave identity, schedule, and social approval mostly undisturbed.

The chapter becomes practical fast. In work, it asks whether a team keeps reducing the meaningful target so it can stay emotionally safe. In school, it asks whether a student narrows the standard to avoid the strain of a more demanding result. In personal life, it asks whether a person keeps choosing smaller versions of what they want because larger versions would require sharper honesty. The lesson is not to idolize big for its own sake. It is to stop treating scale as suspicious by default.

The limit matters just as much as the permission. Bigger is not automatically better. A large aim can still be vague, performative, or detached from sequence. The chapter stays serious by defending legitimate scale, not fantasy. It wants the reader to keep the goal large when the largeness is real and worth serving. Once that is clear, the next need becomes obvious: if big is not bad, what question keeps a large aim from remaining blurry? Chapter 10 introduces that tool next.
