Chapter 15 picks up after priority and asks what life looks like when the chosen first claim is actually carried out. Keller does not treat productivity as a badge for busy people or as proof that a person can keep many demands moving at once. He treats it as execution shaped by the thing that already earned first claim. The chapter turns ordered focus into motion.

That shift matters because priority can still remain abstract if it never governs the next block of work. A person can say what matters most and still spend the day reacting, tidying, and clearing whatever is loudest. Chapter 15 pushes against that split. Productivity is not the number of moving parts. It is the discipline of keeping action gathered around what priority already chose.

This is why the chapter resists ordinary efficiency language. It does not celebrate a fuller planner, faster email, or the theater of visible output. Productivity here means concentrated execution. The important question is not whether many things moved. The important question is whether the first claim kept receiving the best force, the clearest attention, and the next serious block of effort.

The applications make the contrast concrete. A team may say the right initiative is first, then spend its week in side tasks that never move the main lane. A student may know the most important exam or paper, then fragment a study block until the real work never deepens. A person at home may clear chores all evening and still avoid the one conversation, repair, or planning move that most deserves the hour. In each case, motion exists without concentrated execution.

So Chapter 15 defines productivity by sequence held under pressure. The chosen priority has to keep governing what happens next even when smaller wins and respectable distractions keep offering themselves. Productivity means the day starts to look like the priority. Busyness means the priority keeps getting praised while something else consumes the force.

That is the bridge to the next chapter. If productivity is concentrated execution of the right first claim, then it needs protection. The reader is left with a sharper question than how to do more. The real question is what commitments are required to keep this kind of productivity from dissolving back into a crowded, respectable day.
