Chapter 11 takes the Focusing Question out of the category of clever tools and moves it into the category of repeated behavior. Keller's point is not that one good question changes everything by itself. The point is that leverage gets stronger when a person returns to the same narrowing standard often enough that it starts shaping ordinary choices. The chapter calls that repeated pattern the Success Habit.

That shift matters because a useful question can still stay occasional. Someone may ask it during a kickoff meeting, during a stressful week, or when a project is obviously stuck, then go right back to reacting to whatever feels loudest. Chapter 11 argues that the real gain appears when the question stops being a rescue move and becomes a normal part of selection. Repetition turns leverage-seeking from a tactic into a way of operating.

The success habit is therefore not generic routine worship. Keller is not praising repetition because repetition looks disciplined. He is describing a specific pattern: repeatedly asking what matters most next, repeatedly narrowing toward the move with the strongest effect, and repeatedly refusing to spread equal force across every respectable option. The habit is not the calendar block alone. The habit is the return to leverage.

That distinction protects the chapter from becoming mechanical. A person can repeat the wrong pattern very faithfully. A team can hold the same meeting every week and still avoid the decisive question. A student can sit in the same library carrel every afternoon and still move through familiar tasks without asking which one would actually simplify the rest. Habit is only success habit when repetition keeps serving judgment instead of replacing it.

The applications are easy to see. At work, the question becomes part of the weekly board review instead of a special exercise saved for crisis moments. In school, it shows up in the planner margin before a study block begins, so the hardest leverage point gets named before effort scatters. In personal life, it can become a notebook ritual at home, where one recurring question keeps good intentions from multiplying without sequence. The common feature is not routine for routine's sake. It is repeated narrowing.

That is why Chapter 11 sits exactly where it does. Chapter 10 supplied the leverage question. Chapter 11 shows what happens when that question is practiced enough to shape behavior. It also keeps one warning in view: repetition can deepen judgment, but it can also harden into empty routine if leverage is forgotten. That tension leads directly to the next chapter.
