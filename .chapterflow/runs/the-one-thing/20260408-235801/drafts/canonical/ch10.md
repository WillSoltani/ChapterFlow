After nine chapters of clearing away lies, Chapter 10 finally gives the reader a tool. The problem up to this point has never been the total absence of good intentions. People usually have many worthy tasks, projects, duties, and goals. The real problem is that broad intention can still leave the field crowded and blurry. Keller introduces the Focusing Question as the practical move that narrows the field. Instead of admiring everything that could matter, the reader is pushed to ask which next action has the most leverage.

That is the chapter's core truth. A better question changes the quality of selection. It does not merely organize a list more neatly. It asks which next move would simplify, strengthen, or unlock the rest. The point is not magical certainty. The point is leverage. One well-chosen next action can make other tasks easier, less urgent, or less necessary than they first appeared.

This is why the chapter matters after Big Is Bad. Once a person stops shrinking the goal just because it is large, they still need a way to avoid leaving that large goal vague. The Focusing Question supplies that narrowing move. It turns scale into sequence. It asks for the most consequential next point of effort instead of letting the person hide inside broad ambition or endless worthy options.

That is also what separates the chapter from generic prioritization advice. Keller is not just saying to pick something important. He is trying to train a sharper standard: what next action carries disproportionate consequence? What next move changes the shape of the remaining field? The chapter becomes more disciplined because it treats selection itself as serious work.

The applications spread quickly. In work, the question asks what next move would most strengthen the project instead of what task merely feels busy or visible. In school, it asks what next study action would simplify later effort instead of scattering attention across everything at once. In personal life, it asks what next responsibility would bring the most clarity instead of juggling many vague good intentions. The chapter's promise is not that the answer will always feel obvious. The promise is that the question itself improves the search.

The limit matters too. A focusing question can become another elegant delay tactic if a person keeps polishing the wording instead of acting on the answer. Keller does not present the tool as a shortcut around effort. It is a way to choose better, not a way to avoid doing. Once that distinction is clear, the next step becomes natural. If the right question keeps finding leverage, what happens when a person asks it repeatedly enough for it to shape behavior? Chapter 11 turns that repetition into habit.
