Treating everything equally sounds responsible. It sounds fair, mature, and morally clean. If every task gets some attention, every obligation gets a little care, and every demand gets a polite nod, then no one can accuse you of neglect. Keller attacks that comfort directly. Chapter 4 argues that equal weighting is not wisdom. It is dilution. Different tasks do not produce equal consequence, so pretending they do only spreads force thinly across a field that was never equal to begin with.

That is why the chapter names "everything matters equally" as a lie instead of a harmless habit. The lie is attractive because it hides the pain of ranking. If all claims on your time are treated as peers, then you never have to admit that some deserve more pressure than others. But reality keeps breaking that fiction. One decision can unblock a project while five maintenance tasks leave the project essentially unchanged. One concept can shape half an exam while ten pages of broad review barely move the score. One conversation can stabilize a week at home while a pile of symbolic check-ins leaves the same instability in place. The consequence is unequal even when the tasks all look respectable.

That is the real mechanism under the chapter. Ranking is not justified by preference, mood, or ego. It is justified by unequal leverage. Some actions change the field more than others. Some obligations deserve prime attention because they affect the rest. Keller is not praising obsession for its own sake. He is making a structural claim: if outcomes are unequal, then attention cannot be distributed as if every claim deserves the same weight. Equal treatment of unequal tasks does not create balance. It creates softness where force was needed.

This is also why the chapter is stronger than a flat 80/20 slogan. The issue is not just that a few things are more productive than the many. The issue is that equal weighting often becomes a moral disguise for avoiding hard choices. A crowded task list feels honest because it appears comprehensive. But it can easily become a way of refusing to decide what truly carries consequence. People hide inside broad coverage because coverage looks fairer than ranking. Keller's point is that fairness to the list can become unfairness to the result. When everything gets equal pressure, nothing decisive gets enough.

The chapter becomes practical the moment you apply it. In work, the question becomes which task actually changes the project instead of which one is easiest to show on an update. In school, it becomes which section or concept most affects the outcome instead of what lets you say you reviewed everything. In personal life, it becomes which obligation truly steadies the week instead of which duties merely look virtuous when scattered across a calendar. Forced ranking is uncomfortable because it exposes the gap between visible busyness and real consequence. That discomfort is not a flaw in the method. It is evidence that the method is finally asking the right question.

Still, the chapter needs one ethical limit to stay serious. Ranking can be abused. A person can call something "low leverage" when they simply do not want to carry it. They can protect convenience and rename it priority. Keller's argument only works when consequence, not selfishness, remains the standard. Some maintenance, care, and duty still matter even when they are not glamorous. The chapter is not permission to erase them. It is a demand to stop flattening everything into the same weight. Once you accept that equal weighting is a lie, the next temptation becomes obvious. If ranking is hard and guilt remains high, many people will try to solve the problem by doing several things at once. That is where the next lie enters.
