People like the story of the giant leap because it flatters the imagination. It lets you picture success as one bold move, one burst of courage, one dramatic week when everything changes at once. Keller pushes against that fantasy almost immediately. The image he offers is smaller, but harsher: a line of dominos. Not because progress is cute or tidy, but because it is sequential. One right move matters precisely because it changes what the next move can do.

That shift is easy to miss. Most people hear "one step at a time" and translate it into generic patience. That is weaker than Keller's point. A domino chain is not just a collection of small actions. It is an ordered sequence where one action creates momentum for the next. The first move matters because it is connected. A random small task is still random. A useful first move is the one that starts changing the field. This deepens the first chapter's leverage logic by adding order. It is no longer enough to identify what matters most. Now the question becomes: what move should come first if the rest is supposed to follow?

That is why Keller treats progress as cumulative instead of simultaneous. You do not usually earn extraordinary results by trying to advance every part of your goal evenly. You earn them by finding the first move that unlocks or simplifies what comes after. In work, that might be the project decision that frees two stalled teams instead of another round of status updates. In school, it might be the core concept that the rest of the exam keeps leaning on rather than another broad pass through everything. In personal life, it might be the one conversation, routine, or boundary that makes future consistency less fragile. The chain works because each move changes the conditions of the next move.

This also explains why Keller is not praising tiny steps for their own sake. The size of the move is not the point. The relationship between the moves is the point. A person can spend a day crossing off small tasks and never touch a real domino. Another person can make one difficult decision that rearranges the whole week. The metaphor only works when the first step has direction. Otherwise "start small" becomes another polite form of avoidance. The standard stays strict: choose the next move that produces consequence, not just the next move that reduces your anxiety.

The chapter becomes practical when it changes the emotional burden of ambition. Giant-leap thinking makes people stare at the whole distance until they freeze. Domino thinking breaks that spell. You do not need the whole chain in perfect detail before acting. You need a credible next move that can start the sequence. That is different from pretending the entire path is obvious. It simply means that meaningful progress often begins once you stop asking how to finish everything now and start asking what first action would make later actions stronger, easier, or more likely.

Still, the metaphor has a limit, and the chapter is better when that limit stays visible. Not every form of work unfolds in a neat, visible chain. Some situations are messy, political, or uncertain enough that the next domino is hard to identify. Some sequences have to be revised once motion begins. So the point is not to worship linearity. It is to reject the more damaging illusion that progress comes from equal effort everywhere at once. Sequence gives ambition traction. It does not remove uncertainty. And once you accept that success often leaves a chain of right moves behind it, the next question almost asks itself: if progress does leave a pattern, what can you learn from people who have already found it?
