Multitasking sounds like modern competence. It suggests speed, adaptability, and a kind of professional toughness. If you can answer messages while outlining a proposal, monitor notifications while reviewing a budget, and keep half an ear on a conversation while planning the evening, then it looks as if you are handling more life than other people can handle. Chapter 5 attacks that performance directly. Keller treats multitasking as a lie because simultaneous motion is not the same thing as concentrated progress. Trying to do several meaningful things at once does not multiply force. It divides it.

That is why the chapter matters immediately after the lie of equal weighting. Once people refuse to rank, they still need a way to keep every claim alive. Multitasking becomes the rescue fantasy. Instead of deciding what deserves full pressure now and what must wait, they try to give partial attention to everything. The result looks energetic from the outside. Tabs stay open. Replies keep moving. The day feels full. But fullness is not the same as advancement. A difficult task that keeps getting interrupted does not receive accumulated force. It receives repeated beginnings.

This is the chapter's central mechanism. Multitasking flatters people by hiding switching inside the language of productivity. A person thinks they are doing two or three things at once when they are really bouncing between unfinished lines of thought. Each return costs something. The thread has to be found again. The pressure has to be rebuilt. Small errors slip in because attention is arriving broken instead of whole. Even when no dramatic mistake appears, the work often gets thinner. The sentence is weaker, the judgment is shallower, the conversation is half-heard, and the study session leaves less behind than it should. None of that requires fake science to see. It is already visible in ordinary experience.

That is also why the chapter should not collapse into a generic complaint about devices or modern life. The real target is not technology by itself. It is the pride people attach to divided attention. They want credit for responsiveness, so they keep reopening the same work while pretending it is moving forward. They want to feel efficient, so they confuse activity across several fronts with strength on the one front that actually matters. Keller's point is harsher than simple time management. Split focus does not prove capability. Very often it proves that a person cannot bear the discipline of letting one task matter more than the rest for a while.

The chapter becomes practical the moment it enters real settings. In work, it asks whether the important draft, analysis, or decision is being protected from constant message traffic. In school, it asks whether study time is actually study time or just a sequence of interruptions that creates the feeling of effort without the depth of retention. In personal life, it asks whether a conversation, plan, or obligation is being handled with full presence or with leftover attention squeezed between other tasks. The answer is not to become fragile or unreachable. It is to stop pretending that fractured attention is a performance advantage when it usually leaves every task with less force than it needed.

Still, the chapter needs one limit to stay serious. Some situations really do require monitoring several streams, responding to change, or holding a loose watch over multiple demands. That is different from claiming that two demanding thinking tasks can both receive full cognitive force at the same time. The chapter should keep that distinction clean. It is not condemning all coordination. It is condemning the self-flattering lie that important work improves when attention is split. Once that lie is exposed, another false comfort has to go with it. If multitasking fails, many people assume the answer must be a perfectly disciplined life. That is the next illusion the book is ready to break.
