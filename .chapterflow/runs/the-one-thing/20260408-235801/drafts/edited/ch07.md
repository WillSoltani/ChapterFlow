Willpower becomes dangerous when people treat it like a permanent service. The fantasy is simple: if something matters, then a serious person should be able to summon focused effort whenever needed. Morning, late afternoon, exhausted evening, reactive afternoon after a flood of messages, it should not matter. Discipline should appear on command and rescue the task. Chapter 7 attacks that fantasy directly. Keller says willpower is not always on call. Serious effort is real, but it is not equally available at every moment, and pretending otherwise quietly weakens the work that matters most.

That is why this chapter matters after selective discipline. Chapter 6 teaches the reader to hold the right routine instead of chasing total perfection. Chapter 7 adds a harder correction. Even the right routine can fail if it is placed as though inner strength is always equally available. People often act as if the hard task can be pushed anywhere in the day because discipline will eventually drag it across the line. Keller challenges that assumption. The issue is not whether effort matters. The issue is whether effort is being demanded at the right time.

This is the chapter's central mechanism. Important work should respect timing, freshness, and the availability of mental force. A person may have enough willpower to answer messages, attend meetings, or clear small obligations late in the day, but that does not mean they have the same quality of attention for deep judgment, difficult writing, or demanding study. The chapter's point is that bad timing creates an avoidable tax. When serious work is repeatedly placed after strength has already been spent, the work becomes slower, thinner, and more dependent on rescue attempts that rarely feel as strong as the original opportunity for focused effort.

That is also why the chapter should not collapse into soft excuse-making. Keller is not saying that important things only happen when a person feels inspired. He is not replacing discipline with mood. He is making a sharper demand: if something truly matters, then stop scheduling it as if all moments are equal. Put it where your best force is most likely to exist. Protect the morning block, the fresh study hour, the clear decision window, or whatever period most reliably carries better judgment. The chapter becomes practical because it treats willpower like something to respect, not something to romanticize.

The idea lands quickly in ordinary life. In work, the hardest project decision should not always be deferred until after a day of reactive traffic if there is a fresher block available earlier. In school, the most demanding reading or writing should not always be pushed into a tired late-night slot just because the day was consumed by smaller obligations. In personal life, key conversations and meaningful planning may need to happen before fatigue, noise, and accumulated friction make clarity harder to reach. The lesson is not fragility. It is placement. Important effort deserves the strongest realistic window.

Still, the chapter needs one limit to remain serious. Respecting willpower timing cannot become a polished excuse for endless avoidance. A person can always claim they are not in the right state yet. Keller's point is not to wait for magical readiness. It is to stop pretending that badly placed work can always be saved by heroic self-command later. The wiser move is to match important effort to the best available window and treat that alignment as part of the work itself. Once that is clear, another comforting myth becomes easier to expose. People often respond to uneven energy by chasing a life that feels perfectly balanced at every moment. The next chapter says that fantasy fails too.
