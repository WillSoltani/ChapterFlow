Core Claim:
Chapter 3 argues that success is not random noise. It leaves patterns that can be observed, studied, and adapted. Keller uses this move to justify learning from what works instead of improvising blindly or treating strong outcomes as mysterious accidents.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
This chapter is strongest when it turns success into evidence. It tells the reader to look for repeatable clues in effective action without collapsing into shallow imitation.

Author Logic Chain:
First the author claims that successful outcomes often leave visible traces.
Then the author argues that those traces can be studied for principles rather than admired from a distance.
Then the author pushes the practical shift: adapt what works to your context instead of worshipping originality or copying surfaces.

Frameworks or Terms Introduced:

Specific Applications:
- studying what top performers consistently do before designing a new team workflow
- noticing which study methods repeatedly lead to strong results instead of inventing a new system every week
- observing what routines actually make a relationship calmer or healthier before changing everything at once

Common Misreadings:
- turning the chapter into hero worship
- assuming one successful example proves a universal rule
- confusing copying visible habits with understanding the principle behind them

Counterarguments or Limits:
Patterns can guide judgment, but context still matters. The chapter should not pretend that every successful action transfers cleanly across different constraints, roles, or environments.
