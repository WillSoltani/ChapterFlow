Core Claim:
Chapter 5 argues that multitasking is not a strength but a productivity lie. Trying to advance multiple meaningful tasks at once splits attention, increases switching, and weakens the quality of force applied to the task that matters most.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
This chapter should feel like the operational consequence of Chapter 4. Once the reader admits that tasks are not equal, the next temptation is to keep them all alive at once through multitasking. Chapter 5 should expose that move as flattering chaos.

Author Logic Chain:
First the author names multitasking as a lie rather than as proof of modern competence.
Then the author argues that divided attention and rapid switching are not the same as concentrated progress.
Then the author makes the practical demand: sequence important work, protect the active task, and stop confusing simultaneous motion with real advancement.

Required Anchors:

Frameworks or Terms Introduced:
- multitasking lie: the belief that simultaneous effort creates more output
- attention switching: moving back and forth between demands instead of finishing one active line of thought
- protected sequence: giving one meaningful task uninterrupted force before reopening the rest

Specific Applications:
- trying to answer messages during deep project work and producing weaker thinking on both
- studying while bouncing between notes, notifications, and a browser tab that keeps resetting attention
- handling a personal conversation while half-working through errands and leaving both interactions thin

Common Misreadings:
- reducing the chapter to a complaint about technology rather than about divided attention
- treating harmless batching or waiting-time overlap as the same thing as cognitive multitasking
- pretending that visible responsiveness proves real progress

Counterarguments or Limits:
Some roles involve monitoring several streams or handling rapid interruptions, but that is not the same as pretending two demanding thinking tasks can receive full force at the same moment. The chapter should keep this distinction explicit.
