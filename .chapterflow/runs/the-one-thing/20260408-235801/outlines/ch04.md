Chapter Promise:
Show that equal weighting is a lie, so the reader stops treating every demand as a peer and starts ranking by consequence.

Opening Move:
Open on the comforting idea that treating everything evenly is responsible, then expose it as dilution disguised as fairness.

Anchor Allocation:
- anchor 1 used in paragraph(s): 1-2 -> everything matters equally as a lie
- anchor 2 used in paragraph(s): 3-4 -> unequal leverage makes ranking necessary
- anchor 3 used in paragraph(s): 5-6 -> equal weighting weakens force and blurs contribution

Paragraph Job Map:
- P1: surface the appeal of treating all tasks equally
- P2: name equal weighting as a false and costly belief
- P3: explain why tasks differ in consequence
- P4: show how equal weighting produces dilution
- P5: apply forced ranking to work, school, or personal settings
- P6: preserve the duty limit and bridge to the multitasking lie

Required Distinction or Mechanism:
Unequal consequence, not personal preference, is what justifies ranking.

Hard-Depth Tension:
Ranking clarifies force, but it can be abused when people use leverage language to dismiss necessary maintenance or duty.

Threshold Question:
When does sharp ranking clarify contribution, and when does it become a simplistic excuse for dismissing necessary but lower-leverage work?

Takeaway Count Lock:
- Easy: 3 (always)
- Medium: 5
- Hard: 5

Hard Takeaway Topics:
- Takeaway 1: equal weighting as a lie
- Takeaway 2: unequal leverage
- Takeaway 3: dilution through task democracy
- Takeaway 4: ranking in work / school / personal life
- Takeaway 5: ethical limit on ranking

Scenario Lesson Map:
- Scenario 1 (decision_point, work): choosing the one task that changes the project instead of splitting time evenly
- Scenario 2 (postmortem, school): realizing that equal review produced average results because the hardest concept was underweighted
- Scenario 3 (dialogue, personal): explaining why symbolic attention to everything still leaves the week unstable
- Scenario 4 (predict_reveal, work): predicting what happens when a team ranks one blocker above five respectable side tasks
- Scenario 5 (dilemma, school): choosing between visible small assignments and the essay section that controls the grade
- Scenario 6 (before_after, personal): comparing evenly spread attention with ranked attention

Scenario Vividness Targets:
For at least 4 of 6 scenarios, include details like an overcrowded task board, highlighted syllabus pages, phone reminders stacked in a lock screen, or a grocery list pinned beside a family calendar.

Preview Constraint:
The preview must ask what happens when people try to solve the dilution problem by multitasking.

Genericity Risks:
- flattening the chapter into a vague 80/20 recap
- sounding anti-duty rather than anti-dilution
- confusing urgency, guilt, or visibility with leverage

Forbidden Drift:
- no invented statistics
- no contamination language or source-splice phrasing
- no fake permission to ignore legitimate obligations
- no brief-language leakage into reader-facing prose
