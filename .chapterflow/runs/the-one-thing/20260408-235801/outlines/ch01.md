Chapter Promise:
Show why choosing the highest-leverage priority matters more than spreading effort everywhere, and why this one decision changes the shape of later work.

Opening Move:
Open on Keller's realization that the simple instruction to find one thing that matters cuts through the noise more effectively than trying to manage everything at once.

Anchor Allocation:
- anchor 1 used in paragraph(s): 1-2 -> the chapter-opening realization around one thing
- anchor 2 used in paragraph(s): 3-4 -> Keller's contrast between narrow focus and variable results
- anchor 3 used in paragraph(s): 4-6 -> the leverage question about what makes other work easier or unnecessary

Paragraph Job Map:
- P1: introduce the seductive but difficult simplicity of picking one thing
- P2: explain why most people spread effort instead of choosing
- P3: tie stronger outcomes to narrower concentration
- P4: define leverage rather than generic focus
- P5: show what the question does in work, school, or personal decisions
- P6: preserve the chapter's limit about neglected responsibilities and bridge to sequence

Required Distinction or Mechanism:
The chapter is not about doing less for its own sake. It is about finding the one action whose completion changes the value, difficulty, or necessity of the actions around it.

Hard-Depth Tension:
One-thing thinking clarifies action, but used crudely it can become permission to ignore legitimate obligations that still deserve attention.

Threshold Question:
When does one-thing thinking become clarifying leverage, and when does it become a crude excuse for neglecting responsibilities that still matter?

Takeaway Count Lock:
- Easy: 3 (always)
- Medium: 5
- Hard: 5

Hard Takeaway Topics:
- Takeaway 1: define the one thing as leverage, not preference
- Takeaway 2: narrow concentration versus scattered effort
- Takeaway 3: easier or unnecessary as the governing test
- Takeaway 4: chapter-specific applications
- Takeaway 5: the misuse risk when obligations get flattened

Scenario Lesson Map:
- Scenario 1 (decision_point, work): choosing the highest-leverage task before a deadline crunch
- Scenario 2 (postmortem, school): seeing how broad studying diluted actual progress
- Scenario 3 (dialogue, personal): helping someone distinguish the main priority from emotional noise
- Scenario 4 (predict_reveal, work): forecasting what changes when a team narrows to one decisive priority
- Scenario 5 (dilemma, school): choosing between several worthy commitments when only one unlocks later ease
- Scenario 6 (before_after, personal): comparing a scattered week with one anchored to a single key task

Scenario Vividness Targets:
For at least 4 of 6 scenarios, include details like a half-finished slide deck, highlighted exam notes, a buzzing phone on the kitchen counter, a Kanban board, a robotics parts bin, or a planner crowded with crossed-out tasks.

Preview Constraint:

Genericity Risks:
- turning focus into a vague motivational virtue
- describing productivity in generic terms without leverage
- importing later frameworks too early

Forbidden Drift:

- no contamination language or pseudo-coaching slogans
