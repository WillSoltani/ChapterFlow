# Ulterior Transactions and the Anatomy of a Game

Lena was on her third rejected suggestion when the whole table noticed they were in a different conversation than the one they had thought they were in. She had been describing her morning commute, which was bad, and asking her dinner party guests what she should do about it. The first guest suggested a different route. Lena said, "Yes, but the tolls on that one are brutal." The second guest suggested the train. Lena said, "Yes, but then I have to walk ten blocks in bad shoes." The third guest, a quieter one, suggested working from home two days a week. Lena said, "Yes, but my manager is weird about it." Around the third "yes, but," something shifted at the table without anyone naming it. The conversation was still, on the surface, about Lena's commute. But a second conversation had been running underneath the first one for the last ten minutes, and everyone at the table had started to feel it land.

Here is what Berne added after the three voices and the two arrows. Some transactions run on two levels at once. On the top level, the level that has words in it, Lena was asking for advice. On the level underneath, the level that has no words but has a pattern in it, Lena was doing something else entirely. She was demonstrating, with every rejected suggestion, that no one at the table could help her. The guests were participating in that demonstration by generating fresh suggestions for her to reject. The surface conversation was about the commute. The actual conversation was about Lena's position in the world (unhelpable, interestingly hard) and the guests' position in the world (concerned friends who tried). Berne called the second level ulterior, and he built his whole definition of a game around it.

A game, in his specific technical sense of the word, is not what the phrase sounds like. It is not playful, and it is not the loose thing people mean when they say their friend is "playing games with them." It is an ongoing series of ulterior transactions that move, reliably, toward a predictable payoff, and the shape of the series is repeatable. Victor runs one in his Tuesday retrospective. He opens the meeting, without fail, by asking the room, "Does anyone have anything they want to raise?" On the social level, it is an invitation. On the psychological level, it is a trap. The first few times Teodora raised something, Victor responded in ways that were technically polite and practically punishing: a half-second pause, a reframing that made her concern sound smaller, a follow-up question that implied she had not done her homework. Now, eight months in, Teodora sits in the retrospective with a cup of coffee she is not drinking and waits for the three-second silence that follows the invitation to pass. After the silence passes, Victor says, "Great, no issues this week," and closes the meeting. The payoff is that Victor can report upward that the team has no concerns. Everyone in the room is playing, and nobody in the room is playing on purpose.

The reason to call this a game in the technical sense, and not just a bad meeting, is that it has a shape. Berne proposed that every game has four structural features you can name. Priscilla offers to help her adult son Nils with his kitchen remodel. The thesis of the game is the repeating plot: she offers help, Nils eventually accepts it, she provides it in a way that guarantees something will go wrong (the wrong tile arrives, the subcontractor she recommended is booked, the deadline slips), and she then sighs at him in a way that says, "I don't know what you would do without me." The antithesis is the move that would break the game: Nils declines the offer, or accepts it and insists on using it his own way, or names the pattern out loud. The dynamics are the mechanism that keeps the game running: Priscilla needs to be needed in a specific form, and Nils has been rewarded for being the person who needs her. The payoff is what both of them get from the loss: a confirmation of the parent-child position they have been occupying since Nils was fourteen, which neither of them is ready to give up.

Now go back to Lena. The thesis of her dinner party game is an opening complaint followed by rejected suggestions followed by a small, moral conclusion that no one understands what it is like to be her. The antithesis, the move that would break the game, is not another suggestion. It is the guest who says, in a friendly tone, "What have you already tried?" or "What would you tell a friend who was in the same spot?" Those questions refuse the assigned role of advice-generator and put the problem back in Lena's hands, where the social-level conversation claims it already was. The dynamics are Lena's need to prove that the problem is insoluble, because if the problem were soluble, she would be on the hook for solving it. The payoff is a reliable stream of attention — strokes, to use a word that belongs to the next chapter — plus a confirmation of her position that no one can really help her. The game works. That is why she keeps running it.

Here is the part that the anatomy alone will not tell you, and the part this chapter cannot leave out. The antithesis is expensive. Breaking a game takes away the payoff both players have been using, and if Lena had a better source of those strokes, she would already be getting them elsewhere. If Priscilla had a stable way to feel needed that did not cost her son's autonomy, she would already be using it. If Victor had a way to run a retrospective that made his manager's report look clean without the suppression, he would already be running it. The player is not running the game because they are foolish. They are running it because it is the only reliable source of the thing the payoff is made of, and they do not yet have a better source. Seeing the game is always valuable. Choosing to break it is a separate, costly decision that belongs to the player, not to the observer, and honest readers of this chapter should be careful about using the anatomy to tell other people which games they are playing.

The anatomy says what a game is. It does not yet say why games are so sticky even once you can see them. That answer is in the payoff — in what the payoff is made of and what deeper position it is protecting — and that is where the next chapter has to go.
