# Party and Social Games

The book club had been meeting for three years and the books had, over that time, become secondary. It was thirty-eight minutes into the October meeting before anyone mentioned the novel on the table, and in the thirty-eight minutes before that, the members had covered the state of publishing, the education system, the behavior of a specific cable news channel, and the general decline of people who did not read. Nobody was wrong about any of it. Nobody was particularly moved by any of it either. They had had this conversation, in various forms, every month for three years, and the warmth in the room when the conversation was running was real and the warmth did not require the book. Three of the members had, without ever quite deciding to, stopped reading the monthly selection.

Party games are the cheapest form of what the previous two chapters described. They run in groups, in short bursts, and they bond people together through a shared stance toward something outside the group rather than through any genuine contact between the people inside it. The cost per player is low. The social cost in aggregate is what the chapter is about. Three patterns appear here.

The first is the book club pattern. Berne called it gathering to deplore. A group assembles around a shared complaint about the world, the economy, a situation, a category of other people. The social level is civic engagement. The psychological level is the reliable delivery of warmth through agreement, without the risk of anything personal. The antithesis is the question that points toward action or toward something specific and personal: what would you actually do if you had to do something about it? That question leaves the shared stance and points toward an individual, which is where the game is not going. The payoff is not the depletion of the complaint; it is the strokes that come from mutual agreement and the warmth of being in a room with people who share your sense of the world.

The second pattern runs in offices and study groups and lunch conversations everywhere. Pita has a problem with a client scope, and she describes it to three colleagues at the kitchen table. The first colleague suggests rescheduling the deliverable. Yes, but the client is already frustrated about delays. The second suggests being explicit about the constraints in the next call. Yes, but Pita has tried that and it made things worse. The third suggests escalating. Yes, but that would look like a failure of project management. The colleagues leave the kitchen feeling subtly incompetent, as though the problem is too hard for them. Pita leaves having proved, once again, that the problem is insoluble. That proof is the payoff. The colleagues are not providing solutions; they are providing the raw material for the rejections, and the rejections are what the pattern is running on. The antithesis is the colleague who, instead of offering a suggestion, asks: what would you actually try, if you had to try something? That question refuses the role of solution-generator and hands the problem back. It is slightly rude by the conventions of the advice-giving situation, which is why it almost never gets asked.

Cass brings the same quality of attention to design critiques that a forensic accountant brings to tax returns. Every piece in the review gets an error found first. The finding is real: the errors Cass locates are genuine. The move is in the finding them first, before any strength is noted, and in the specific satisfaction that the finding produces. The rest of the reviewers feel, by the end of the session, that they are in a room with someone who has higher standards than they do. Cass feels the same way. The payoff is social superiority maintained without a direct claim to it: no one can accuse Cass of being wrong about the flaws. The antithesis is the reviewer who, when Cass finds the first flaw, notes it once and immediately names three strengths. That move declines the first-flaw contest without disputing the finding.

Here is the part the chapter cannot leave without saying. Not every gathering-to-deplore is this pattern. Not every rejected suggestion is the insolubility demonstration. Not every first-finding-of-flaws is the superiority game. The difference is in what the dynamics are doing with the activity. In a room where the deploring is moving toward something, where the complaint is heading toward a plan or a decision or even a useful naming of something new, the activity is not the game. The game is in the circling. The book club that has been having the same thirty-eight-minute conversation for three years is circling. The team meeting where the complaints are moving toward an action item is not. The distinction is harder to see from inside the room than from outside it, but it is real and the chapter depends on it.

The party games in this chapter run in the easiest social settings: a kitchen, a book club, a design review. The next chapter looks at games that run inside a professional relationship, where the helper's role itself provides the cover, and the stakes for both players are different.
