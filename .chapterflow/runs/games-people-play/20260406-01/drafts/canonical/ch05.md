# Life Games

Greta is sitting at a coffee table signing the fourth partnership agreement of her career. The new partner is generous, attentive, and excited about what they can build together. Greta has signed this agreement before, with different names at the top and in different cities, and each of the three previous partnerships ended in the same shape: the partner found the ways she had made herself available to be taken from, took, and Greta felt the cold confirmation of something she has known since her twenties. She does not know, sitting at the coffee table, that she is running a game. She knows she is hopeful and she knows she is afraid and she knows that the shape of this moment is familiar in a way she cannot explain.

Most of the patterns in this book play themselves out in a single conversation or a bad afternoon. Life games are different. They run at biographical scale. The same opening move, the same sequence of steps, the same switch at the moment the psychological level takes over, and the same payoff arrived at years later. The thesis is not an exchange; it is a plot that spans a decade. This chapter covers four of them.

Greta's pattern is the one that begins with exploitability. She is genuinely generous, genuinely available, and genuinely not protective of what she offers. The thesis of the game is simple: she makes herself easy to take from, someone takes, and she experiences the taking as proof of something she already knew. The antithesis is the partner who refuses to exploit. When Greta offers more than she should, the antithesis partner says, "That's too much. Take it back." And Greta, who has built her entire stroke supply around being exploited, finds that partner deeply unsatisfying and eventually goes elsewhere for a business relationship. The dynamics are her need for the confirmation. The payoff is the proof: the world takes what it can from me, and this has always been true.

Felix is the department head at a regional hospital and he has, for twenty years, been running a different pattern. Felix is fair. Felix follows procedure. Felix notices, and catalogues, every time the people around him do not. When Rafa, his longest-serving colleague, makes a billing error on a Tuesday, Felix does not address it immediately. He waits. Two weeks later, when the error has compounded and the correct moment arrives, Felix calls Rafa into his office. The anger Felix releases is clean and justified and has been waiting for this specific invoice for two weeks. The payoff is the release without guilt, the moral clarity of a man who caught someone in a real mistake. The dynamics are the standing need for that release, and the need for the position it confirms: I am the one who sees what others miss, and eventually everyone will know it. The antithesis is not argument or appeal. It is Rafa who is so procedurally impeccable that Felix's catalogue stays empty.

Ivar is a project coordinator who has, for most of his working life, found someone to blame for mistakes that were substantially his own. When the Thursday deadline was missed, it was because Zoe had asked him about the client preferences at 2pm on Wednesday and he had lost his train of thought. Zoe did not cause the missed deadline. Zoe was nearby when it happened, and Ivar needed the blame to land somewhere other than himself. The payoff is the erasure of the mistake from Ivar's record, plus the secondary payoff of Zoe's colleagues learning to leave him alone when he is focused. Mona, a freelancer, runs a structurally similar pattern at larger scale: she takes on work she cannot complete for rates she cannot sustain, runs the drama of the near-miss for several months, and then collects the sympathy strokes when it fails. The payoff for Mona is not the completion of the work but the proof that nothing can be done about her situation, which is both a stroke and a confirmation.

Here is the part that does not fit easily on a page but that any honest reading of this chapter has to include. These patterns have been running for years. For Greta, the exploitability game has been running since before her first business partnership. For Felix, the righteous-outrage game has been organizing his professional relationships since his first year of management. For a game at this scale, the antithesis is not a conversation. It is a project. Greta changing her pattern means Greta building a different stroke supply that does not require being exploited, and updating a position she has been holding since her twenties, neither of which happens in a single business meeting. The chapter is honest about this because the other version, the one that says "now that you see it, you can change it," is not honest, and it does not help.

What life games have in common is that they have become, over time, indistinguishable from the player's identity. Greta is not running a game; she is being who Greta is. Felix is not organizing his workplace around stored anger; he is being fair. The position the game confirms has accumulated enough evidence, over enough years, that it no longer feels like a position. It feels like a description of the world. That is the scale the chapter is trying to hand you. The next chapter looks at the same patterns running inside a marriage, where the stakes are different and the distance between the game and the identity is often even smaller.
