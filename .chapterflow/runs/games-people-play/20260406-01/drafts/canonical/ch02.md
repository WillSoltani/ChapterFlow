# What a Transaction Is and Why They Go Wrong

Tomas opened the Tuesday stand-up by asking Aiya whether the API change was going to land Friday. It was a clean question, the kind project managers ask a hundred times a sprint. Aiya did not answer it. She said, in a tone with a quarter-inch of disappointment in it, "Tomas, we have been over this before. The dependency on the auth service has not changed since last week." Tomas felt his face do something he did not authorize. The other four people on the call looked at the screen and waited. Nothing about the API change had been said, and the next ten minutes of the meeting were going to be about something else entirely.

What happened in those two sentences is the only thing this chapter is about. Tomas's question was an arrow that started in his Adult and pointed at Aiya's Adult: a request for present-tense information from a colleague who would have it. Aiya's answer was an arrow that started in her Parent and pointed at Tomas's Child: a corrective from somebody who is tired of explaining. The arrows did not match. The conversation could not continue on the original topic because the original topic was no longer the conversation that was now in the room. Berne's contribution, in the chapter that follows the one about three voices, was to give us a way to draw those two arrows on the same picture and see, in real time, whether they line up or whether they cross.

A transaction, in his vocabulary, is the smallest unit the model handles. One person sends something. Another person sends something back. The thing that matters is which state each arrow comes from and which state each arrow points at. Sometimes the second arrow lands exactly where the first one expected. Coach Reyes meets Diego on the pool deck after a bad race, towel around the boy's shoulders, chlorine still on the air, and says, "It's okay, Diego, you swam your best." Reyes is speaking from Nurturing Parent, aimed at Adapted Child. Diego, thirteen and well-trained in the rituals of this exact moment, says, "Thanks, Coach." That return arrow comes from his Adapted Child and lands exactly where Reyes was waiting for it. Both arrows are parallel. The transaction is what Berne called complementary, and the most important thing about it is not that it is comforting. It is that this exact exchange could repeat itself between Reyes and Diego after every bad race for the next four years and never break, and that the pair of them might never have the conversation Diego actually needs about whether he wants to keep swimming.

The other case, the case Tomas just lived through, gets the name crossed. The response comes from a state the sender did not address, or it points back at a state the sender was not in, or both. Olivia called her older sister Kenji on a Tuesday night because the week had been bad and she wanted somebody to tell her that everything was going to be all right. The phone was propped on the kitchen counter. The arrow Olivia sent was Child to Parent: please soothe me. Kenji, who is a good problem-solver and had had her own long day, answered Olivia's Child from her own Adult: "What specifically happened on Tuesday?" Both sisters are technically right. Olivia's grievance is real and Kenji's question is reasonable. The conversation crossed in the second sentence and ended with a small wound that neither of them will mention next week.

Now go back to Tomas. The picture is the same picture, drawn in a meeting room instead of a kitchen. Tomas's Adult fired an arrow at Aiya's Adult. Aiya's Parent fired one back at Tomas's Child. The arrows crossed in the second exchange, and the meeting changed shape because the meeting changed personnel. Nobody in the room asked Tomas's Child to come to the stand-up. He arrived anyway, and stayed for the rest of the call.

Here is the part the diagram does not tell you. Knowing that the arrows crossed gives Tomas a diagnosis. It does not give him a prescription. He has at least three moves available, and the diagram is silent on which one he should pick. He can recross deliberately, sending another Adult arrow into Aiya's Parent and refusing to play the Child role she just assigned him: "Hold on. The question I'm asking is whether the auth dependency moved this week, not whether you've explained it before." He can accept the cross and name it: "It sounds like there's a different conversation we need to have first. Do you want to have it now or after stand-up?" He can do nothing in the moment and recognize the cross afterward, and use the recognition to walk into next Tuesday's stand-up with a different first sentence. All three moves are sometimes the right answer. The diagram does not pick for him. The diagram only shows him that the bones of the exchange went out of alignment and where the misalignment happened.

Two arrows describe what is visible in the room. The next chapter introduces what is happening underneath, and what changes when the underneath is the message that actually counts.
