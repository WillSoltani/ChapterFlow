# What Comes After Games

Solenne is sitting in a meeting where something has gone wrong and everyone in the room knows it is partly her fault. The words for her explanation are already assembled in her throat: the same framing she has used in some version perhaps forty times over the last decade, technically accurate, calibrated to arrive at the conclusion that what went wrong was understandable. She has said it so many times it takes no effort. This time she notices, for about three seconds, that it takes no effort. She notices the words are already there before she has decided to say them. She watches the sentence from a small distance. Nothing is prompting her. The words are simply visible to her, for the first time, as something she assembled rather than something she is simply saying.

Nine chapters have been about patterns: how they form, how they pay out, what positions they confirm, why they last. This chapter is about the question all nine have been building toward. What remains when the patterns slowly go? Berne named three qualities. He gave each a word that already existed but that he was using with unusual precision. Awareness. Spontaneity. Intimacy. Together, he argued, these constitute autonomy: the capacity to live without games. None of them is a technique. They are what remains when the structure around them is slowly dismantled.

Awareness is the quality Solenne's three-second pause approaches. Berne's definition is narrower than most uses of the term: the capacity to perceive what is actually in front of you, in the present, rather than through what your Parent state has always told you this kind of situation means, or what your Child state has always felt when this particular setup arrives. The small gap between Solenne and the sentence she was about to say is what Berne meant. The failure mode is specific: awareness can collapse back into observation-as-practice, a new routine of noticing that becomes its own script, in which the person is no longer perceiving directly but performing directness on a schedule. When that happens, the game has a new costume.

Casimir runs the tutoring lab at a community college, and when a student arrives frustrated, he explains the concept more carefully. It is his automatic reach. One afternoon a student named Valeria says she has read the explanation four times and understands it fine; what she cannot do is make herself sit down and start. Casimir, instead of reaching for the explanation again, says: "Tell me what starting looks like when it goes wrong." The question surprises him as much as Valeria. It came from somewhere the situation called for rather than from the state his history keeps handing him. That is spontaneity: not impulsivity, not being yourself in the loose sense, but a response from whichever ego state is actually appropriate to the moment. The failure mode is that spontaneity can collapse into compulsive reaction, a pattern of surprising moves that becomes as automatic as the reach it replaced.

Takeshi and Fenna have been friends for eleven years and meet for coffee on the first Saturday of every month. Most Saturdays follow a familiar shape: work, what each is carrying, where things are hard. One Saturday in March the conversation is different, and both of them feel the difference before either can name it. Nothing is solved. Neither is in crisis. Both are present with what the other is actually saying rather than with what they have prepared to say back. The conversation is short. It is also the clearest example of what Berne meant by intimacy: not warmth in the general sense, but game-free contact between two people who are, in that specific hour, running nothing on each other. Berne considered this the rarest of the three qualities, and the chapter has no reason to revise that estimate.

The triad is honest about being rare, and that honesty is part of what it is saying. Awareness is the closest to a practice, which is why it is the most likely to be converted back into one. Spontaneity is available to anyone who has done enough work to know which of their usual moves are habit and which are choice, and knowing that takes time. Intimacy requires two people to be simultaneously free of their patterns rather than one person making a solo move, which is why most conversations that feel intimate are structurally more organized than they appear. All three qualities can occur. None can be installed. Each is what happens when its prerequisites are slowly, imperfectly met, and most of the work in the previous nine chapters was quietly about those prerequisites.

The book has tried to give you vocabulary for what is structurally happening when the same conversations keep arriving, without giving you a plan for making them stop. It has tried to name what pays the patterns, what positions they confirm, and what net effects they produce. It has tried to be honest that leaving a game requires something to replace it, and that the triad at the end is the name for what people sometimes, slowly, build into that replacement. Berne did not promise that destination. He named a direction and left it available to anyone who had read far enough to know what the direction was pointing away from. That is what this book has tried to do.
