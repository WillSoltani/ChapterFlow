# Why Games Feel So Hard to Stop

Maya was reading the angry GitHub issue with the kind of attention she did not give her own birthday. The issue had been opened by a stranger she had never spoken to, and it accused her, in three paragraphs, of not caring about a niche library she had been maintaining for seven years and that almost nobody else in the world used. The issue was unfair. The stranger had not read the contributing guide. Maya hated reading the issue. She also noticed, in a part of herself she did not look at directly, that the months when no angry issue arrived were not the better months. They were the worse ones. And she did not yet have a word for why.

The word, since 1964, has been stroke. Berne used it in a specific technical sense. A stroke is the basic unit of social acknowledgment that human beings need to keep functioning, in roughly the way they need calories to keep walking. Strokes can be positive: a smile across a meeting, a thank-you in a message, a real conversation that lasts twenty minutes and has another person on the other end of it. Strokes can also be negative: a criticism, a fight, an angry message from a stranger about a Python library. The structural fact that the rest of this chapter depends on is the one Berne would not back away from, and that the chapter cannot back away from either: a negative stroke is metabolically better than no stroke at all. Maya's monthly angry issue is doing the work of a positive stroke she does not have a reliable source for. It is the wrong shape and it pays out anyway, and Maya's nervous system files it as evidence that she is still inside the part of the world that pays attention to her.

The same machinery operates at the relationship level, with two people. Inez and her oldest friend, who everyone in the friend group calls Coach Banerjee even though she has never coached anything, have a thing they do at 11pm on weeknights. Inez calls. There is a crisis. Banerjee answers because Banerjee is the kind of friend who answers. The crises are real, and they are also reliably arriving on a schedule that has nothing to do with the underlying frequency of crises in Inez's life. Both of them are getting paid by the call. Inez is getting reliable attention from a person who has known her since college, in a tone she has never figured out how to ask for during the day. Banerjee is getting confirmation that she is the kind of person you can call at 11pm with a crisis, which is one of the only places in her week that anybody needs anything from her in particular. Telling either of them to "just stop calling" or "just stop answering" would be a request that they accept stroke deprivation in the name of a freedom they cannot yet feel.

There is a second leg to the model, and it is the part that explains why people stay in patterns long after the strokes have started to hurt. Behind the strokes is a deeper position the player has been holding since they were small. Berne called it a life position. He proposed four. The first is I'm OK and you're OK, which is the healthy stance and is also, in his framing, the only stance from which game-free contact is possible. The second is I'm OK and you're not OK, which is projective and often persecutory. The third is I'm not OK and you're OK, which is depressive and martyr-like and runs much of the world. The fourth is I'm not OK and you're not OK, which is futile, and which Berne names honestly without dramatizing. Theo, the retired schoolteacher, sits at his daughter Ren's kitchen table on Sundays and ends every visit with a comment about something she is doing wrong with the kids. Ren has told him every Sunday for two years to stop. He says he is sorry. He does it again next Sunday. The comment is the move that pays his strokes for the week. The comment is also the move that confirms a position Theo has been holding since 1962: that you have to point out what is wrong or nothing will get fixed, and that being the person who points it out is the only role that has ever made him feel necessary in a room. Removing the comment would not just take away his strokes. It would take away the position. Both losses would have to be replaced.

Now go back to Maya. The angry issue is paying her two things, not one. The first is a negative stroke, in a month that would otherwise have produced zero of any kind, and her nervous system is metabolically grateful even when her conscious mind is not. The second is a confirmation of a position she has been holding since middle school, when she first noticed that being slightly disliked by an interesting stranger felt different from being ignored by everybody. The position is "I am not OK, but at least somebody is paying attention," and the angry issue is a tiny weekly proof of it. To leave the game, Maya would have to give up the strokes and update the position, and the second is the harder of the two by a factor of years.

Here is the part the chapter cannot leave out, and the part that distinguishes a useful chapter from a self-help one. "Stop playing the game" is not a serious instruction unless the reader has a plan for the strokes the game was paying. People who try to leave a game without a replacement supply do not become free. They become miserable in a different shape, and they return to the game inside of a few weeks because the deprivation is harder to live with than the bad feelings were. The order of operations is the work. Find a different supply of strokes first. Update, slowly, the life position the new strokes are allowed to confirm. Then the game becomes possible to leave. Possible, not easy. The chapter is not contemptuous of the player here, because the player is not running the game out of stupidity or weakness. The player is solving a real problem, the only reliable way they have found to solve it, and the chapter's job is not to take the solution away. The chapter's job is to name what the solution was solving, so the player can find a less expensive one if they want to.

The why of stickiness is in your hands now. The next chapter starts the catalog. Each of the long-running, biography-shaping games in the rest of the book is doing the same thing this chapter just described, in slightly different costumes. The question to ask at every entry is the one Maya would ask if she could: what is the payoff made of, and what position is it confirming?
