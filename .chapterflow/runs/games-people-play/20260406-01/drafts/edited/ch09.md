# Games That Aren't Worth Fighting

Margot is on day two of a three-week vacation she fought her manager to take, and she is sitting in a coffee shop in a different time zone triaging bug reports for an open-source library she does not maintain professionally. She has the laptop open, the issue tracker loaded, and a genuine smile on her face. She is a senior engineer at a company that pays her well to do exactly this kind of work, and she is, on her own time, doing more of it for free because she wants to. Her motives are not entirely pure. She likes being an expert. She likes being thanked. She likes having something to do with her hands and her head while the rest of her rests. And the library is better for her being there.

The eight chapters before this one have been building an instinct in the reader. See the pattern. Name the four parts. Find the antithesis. Interrupt the game. This chapter is the correction to that instinct. Berne was explicit that not all games are destructive, and he catalogued a small number that are, in his term, good. The good games are still games in the technical sense: they are ulterior, they have repeating shapes, they produce strokes and confirm positions that the player has been holding for a long time. What makes them good is that the net effect on the world, on the recipients, and on the player is positive. The chapter's central move is a distinction the previous chapters have not required: between seeing a game and fighting it.

Margot's pattern is Berne's Busman's Holiday. The programmer who goes to a hackathon on her week off. The cook who goes on a food tour. The doctor who volunteers at a clinic abroad. All of them are running the same structural move: they are taking work-shaped activity into the space labeled not-work, and they are getting the strokes of being effective and useful at a time when they are supposedly resting. That is a game. It has all four parts: a thesis (the vacation activity), an antithesis (genuine unstructured rest, which most of these players find unpleasant), dynamics (the player's need for the specific strokes of competent helpfulness), and a payoff (mixed motives, including identity and usefulness). The game is real. And the open-source library is better. And Margot is returning from her vacation less burned out than the colleagues who tried to rest the other way. The chapter's honest recommendation: leave it.

Harriet is seventy-two and retired from a career as a research librarian, and she runs the Saturday morning writing workshop at her local library for adults in their twenties and thirties who are trying to write their first novels. Her advice is good, because she spent forty years reading. Her stories about the writers she worked with are relevant, because they are specific. She does not insist on being heeded. She offers the advice as a gift and does not take offense when the younger writers decide not to use it. The workshop is a game, in Berne's technical sense: Harriet is getting something out of it, specifically the specific stroke of being the one who knows things in a room where knowing things is valued. She calls this legacy. Berne called it the Homely Sage pattern. The younger writers are getting real craft guidance they cannot get anywhere else in their city. The library is getting a well-attended Saturday morning program. Harriet is getting the weekly hour her week is organized around. All four things are true at once.

Yonatan, four houses down from an elderly couple named the Jensens, shovels the Jensens' walk every snowstorm without being asked. He has been doing this for three winters. The Jensens are in their eighties and should not be shoveling a walk at all; the last time Mr. Jensen tried, the slip put him in the hospital for a week. Yonatan likes being the kind of neighbor who shovels walks. He likes the Jensens' gratitude, which is warm and genuine and attached to his name on the block. He likes the story about himself that the shoveling supports. The game is real, in the technical sense. The walk is also shoveled, and Mr. Jensen is not slipping, and the relationship between Yonatan and the Jensens is the kind of relationship most neighborhoods have trouble producing. The chapter's reading of this situation, which is meant to be the simplest example of the principle, is: see the game if you want, and leave it alone.

Here is the limit the chapter has to hold carefully, because it is the one the eight previous chapters have been training the reader not to hold. The instruction is not never interrupt a game. It is distinguish the games whose interruption would be a loss from the games whose interruption would be a gain. The distinction lives in the net effect, and the net effect includes what happens to the player, what happens to the recipients, and what happens to the world around them. When the net is positive, the reader's job is to see the game clearly and then to do nothing about it, which is a harder discipline than it sounds after eight chapters of being taught to find the antithesis. Berne named these games partly so that his readers would not try to interrupt every pattern they noticed. He thought the antithesis instinct, applied without the distinction, was its own failure mode.

The chapters so far have been about patterns. The last chapter is about what sits underneath patterns, and what remains when the good ones are protected and the destructive ones are slowly, honestly left.
