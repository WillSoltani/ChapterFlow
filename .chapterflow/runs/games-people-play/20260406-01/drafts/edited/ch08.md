# Professional and Therapy-Room Games

Ife is in the small room at the back of the community mental health center where she has been supervising Rémy every Thursday at 2pm for three years. Rémy has just presented a case: a client with a substance-use history and a complicated relationship with her adult daughter, exactly the kind of case Rémy presents most weeks. Ife offered a perspective she has offered before in slightly different words, about holding the client's agency and the daughter's separateness in the same frame. Rémy nodded and took a note. The supervision hour ended. Ife walked back to her office and noticed, with more clarity than she had allowed herself in a while, that Rémy had been an effective clinician for all three of those years, and that nothing about the Thursday hour had been changing anything about how he worked.

The games in this chapter have a feature the previous catalog chapters did not. The helper is not an innocent bystander who accidentally got caught in someone else's pattern. The helper is a professional whose job requires them to offer help, and the game is using that job. The supervisor, the coach, the teacher, the therapist, the manager who is also a mentor: each of them has a role that makes the game's moves look like the role's normal moves, and the role is the thing that makes the game invisible from inside it. Three patterns are covered here.

The first is the one Berne called I'm Only Trying To Help You. Rémy brings a case. Ife offers perspective. Rémy takes a note. Nothing changes in Rémy's practice. Ife goes home with the feeling of a productive supervision session. Rémy goes home with the feeling of having done his professional development for the week. The game runs because both seats are being played well. The antithesis is the question Ife has not asked in three years: 'What has actually changed in how you work since we started meeting?' That question requires Ife to give up the strokes she has been getting from being the helpful supervisor, and it requires Rémy to give up the structured weekly hour that has been one of the few reliable sources of professional attention in his week. Neither of them has wanted to ask it, and until this Thursday, Ife did not let herself notice that she did not want to.

Caleb has been in Bo's office at the community college every Monday for two years citing a real and well-documented learning disability as the reason he has not made progress on his degree. The disability is real. Bo has been accepting that fact as the explanation because the alternative feels like denying what the medical documentation says. What Bo has not been doing is asking Caleb whether he has tried the accommodations the college offers for this specific disability, which he has not, or asking what Caleb is afraid of finding out about his own capacity if he tried them. The Wooden Leg game, as Berne named it, runs on exactly that structural honesty: the limitation is real, and the game is in the deployment. The antithesis is not to deny the reality of the limitation. It is to hold the reality of the limitation and the reality of Caleb's agency in the same sentence: 'The disability is real. The accommodation is real. Which one do you want to try first, and what would a successful first week look like?' That sentence is uncomfortable because it refuses to let the limitation stand as the whole explanation.

Tamsin has been working with Zara for two years. Zara has read three books on attachment theory and can describe her own relational patterns with real insight. Every session produces a new self-observation: Zara notices a reaction she had, traces it to an earlier pattern, articulates it with accurate vocabulary. Tamsin has been rewarded for the sophistication of these conversations and has been, without quite meaning to, letting them substitute for what the sessions were supposed to produce. Nothing in Zara's life has changed in two years. Her insights have gotten more precise. The Greenhouse game, in Berne's catalog, runs on exactly this: the theory talk is the thing being delivered, not the thing being prepared for. The antithesis is Tamsin noticing, out loud: 'I have heard three insights today and they were accurate and interesting, and I want to ask about the conversation you said you were going to have with your sister last week. Did it happen?' That question declines the reward of the sophisticated conversation and asks about experience instead.

Here is the part the chapter cannot leave without saying, and the part that distinguishes this catalog entry from every other one in the book. The helper is part of the game. The games do not run despite the professional role; they run inside it, and the role is what makes them invisible. Ife cannot diagnose ITHY by looking only at Rémy. Bo cannot diagnose Wooden Leg by looking only at Caleb. Tamsin cannot diagnose Greenhouse by looking only at Zara. Each of these diagnoses requires the helper to ask what they have been getting from the arrangement, which is a question that professional training often does not teach helpers to ask. The helpers in these examples are not failing at their jobs. They are succeeding at them inside a structure that has gradually become the game, and the structure is harder to see than the behavior.

Not all games are destructive. Berne himself acknowledged that some games produce net-positive effects on the world even when the structure underneath is still ulterior. The next chapter covers those.
