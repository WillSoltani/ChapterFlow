# Marital Games

Parveen asked Dev on Sunday afternoon whether he wanted to go to his sister's house for Eid or stay home and have people over. Dev said he was happy either way and asked what she preferred. Parveen said she didn't mind and that he should decide. Dev said, "Let's go to your sister's." Parveen said, "You only said that because you know I'd rather stay home." Dev suggested staying home. Parveen said, "You just don't want to make the effort." This conversation has been happening, with different surfaces, for four years.

The games in this chapter are different from the life games in the previous one in a specific way. They are not run by strangers. They are run inside the structure of the closest relationship a person has, and they share a common psychological function: they provide the form of closeness without the risk of the real thing. What Parveen and Dev are doing with the Sunday planning conversation is having a version of the conversation that protects both of them from the one underneath. In the one underneath, Parveen would say what she actually wants. In the one they are having, she gets to confirm that Dev is the problem. This distinction, the game as substitute for the thing the players are avoiding, is what the chapter is about.

Parveen's game has a name in Berne's catalog. The thesis is: engineer a situation where any choice the other person makes proves the accusation against them. Dev cannot choose correctly because the game is not about the choice; it is about the proof. The payoff is a confirmation that Dev is impossible to please, which functions as the reason Parveen does not have to say what she actually wants. The antithesis is the partner who notices the trap and names it: "I notice both options are wrong. What is it you actually want us to do?" That question refuses the role of person-being-proved-impossible and offers the conversation underneath. It is uncomfortable in the exact proportion that Parveen's actual want is vulnerable.

Luca has been telling the story about architecture for seventeen years. If it weren't for Nadia needing the practical salary, he says, he would have gone to architecture school. The story has several versions: sometimes Nadia is the direct cause, sometimes it is the mortgage, sometimes it is the timing. Berne's structural insight about this pattern is the hardest thing in this chapter and the thing that most resists being softened: Luca chose Nadia, partly, because Nadia provided a reason not to try. The restriction is not imposed from the outside. It was invited in. The game requires a figure to blame for the thing the player is afraid of failing at, and the partner was chosen, in part, for their suitability for that role. The payoff is protection from the fear of an attempt that might produce real evidence. The antithesis, which Nadia has never made, is: "Go. I will support us. Try." And the reason Nadia has never made it is that Luca has built his half of their life around the game, and the antithesis would remove the game and leave Luca in a room with the attempt.

Vera is telling a story about Bastian at a dinner party. The story is warm and the laugh it produces is genuine and the last clause of the story contains a thing about Bastian that, heard at three in the morning, would hurt. Bastian is at the same table, and he knows this, and he cannot object to it without looking like the kind of person who takes offense at an affectionate anecdote. The game is exactly that tight. Vera gets the laugh, the warmth, the social proof, and the small controlled release of a grievance she has been holding since Tuesday. Bastian gets the Tuesday grievance confirmed in front of eight people without any language to object in. The antithesis is Bastian who, privately and after the party, names what just happened: "The last part of that story was the real thing you wanted to say." That move names the psychological level of the transaction without producing a scene, and it costs Bastian something real: the willingness to name a hurt that he has been not-naming for a long time.

Here is what all three patterns have in common, and it is the thing the chapter cannot leave out. The antithesis for every marital game requires the stopping player to do the exact thing the game was protecting them from. Parveen, to break the Corner game, has to say what she actually wants. Luca, to break the IWFY game, has to attempt the thing he is afraid of failing at. Bastian, to break the Sweetheart game, has to name that he is hurt. In every case, the antithesis is not a clever move. It is a real thing. That is why marital games are so persistent and so hard to leave from inside: the game was running precisely because both players were not ready to be that real with each other, and the antithesis requires them to be.

The pattern that runs inside a partnership tends to run most visibly there because the stakes of real vulnerability are highest there. The next chapter looks at the same avoidance strategy in its shortest, cheapest form: the party games that let people spend an evening together without once being real.
