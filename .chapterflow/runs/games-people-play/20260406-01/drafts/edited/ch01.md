# The Three Voices Inside You

Priya was halfway through a code review when the voice she was using stopped belonging to her. She had been leaving neutral comments on Marcus's pull request, noting that a function mutated state it shouldn't, pointing to the line, suggesting an alternative. It was the kind of feedback she gave a dozen times a week. Then, in the last clause of the comment, a second voice arrived uninvited: "I don't know why I keep having to explain this." Marcus read the line on his screen and his shoulders answered before his fingers did. He hunched a little, braced a little, and typed a careful reply that sounded like a tenth-grader writing to a disappointed teacher. Nothing about the code had changed. Something about Priya had.

What changed was not Priya's opinion or her information. The technical content of the comment was identical. What shifted was the register she was speaking in, and with it the kind of reply she was unconsciously demanding. She had started as a senior engineer noticing a bug and ended as someone's irritated mother. Marcus had started as a junior engineer defending a design decision and ended as a child caught at something. This happens all day, everywhere, and most of the time nobody in the room notices that the conversation has quietly changed bodies.

The graduate seminar three floors down was doing the same thing in a different key. Elena had raised her hand and asked what she thought was a factual question about a reading, something about how Professor Halden was defining a term. Halden answered as if the question had been a challenge to his authority. His voice dropped a register. The room went quiet. Elena's highlighter stopped moving halfway across her page. She had asked the question from one place and he had answered from another, and there was now, in the silence, a small crater where the discussion used to be. Sixty years ago, a Canadian-born psychiatrist named Eric Berne gave us a plain-language vocabulary for what just happened. He proposed that at any given moment, an adult is speaking from one of three observable states: the Parent, the Adult, or the Child. Parent is the voice that speaks in rules and shoulds, picked up early from the authority figures in one's life. Adult is the voice that looks at what is actually in front of it, handles present-tense information, and can update. Child is the voice that still carries the original feelings from one's own childhood: the wanting, the hurt, the curiosity, the rebellion.

Noor and Chidi had been roommates for eight months when the dishes conversation happened. Noor started with a reasonable proposal. They could alternate the sink on a schedule, she didn't mind taking odd days, it would take thirty seconds to set up. By the third sentence she was no longer talking about the schedule. She was giving a speech about responsibility, about how certain people needed things spelled out, about how she had grown up in a house where you cleaned up after yourself. Chidi was standing at the counter with his hands open, trying to figure out when the kitchen had turned into a courtroom. Noor, later that night, would not be able to say exactly when she had left the scheduling question behind. She had not decided to give a speech. She had slipped out of one voice and into another without asking her own permission.

Here is the part that is easy to miss, and the part on which everything else depends: the shift is almost never chosen. Noor did not decide to become her mother in the middle of a conversation about a sink. Priya did not decide to scold. Halden did not decide to take an Adult question as a challenge from a Child. The states do things to people; people don't put them on like jackets. Which means that the tool Berne built is not a switch. You cannot will yourself into the Adult. If you try, what you usually find is that the voice coming out of your mouth already belongs to somebody else, and noticing it does not, on its own, make it stop. A reader who expects the model to be a remote control will feel betrayed by it the first time they catch themselves sliding back into Child while thinking "I am being Adult now."

So what is noticing for, then, if it cannot change a state? It is for locating one. Once Noor could see, the next morning, that the speech about responsibility had not been the scheduling conversation she had meant to have, a different question opened up. Not how do I win the argument, but what was actually happening in the room. When Priya reread her code-review comment the next day, she did not flinch at the technical content. She flinched at the last clause and at the voice she heard in it. The next comment she wrote used the same information and left the clause out. She did not become more Adult. She noticed, once, where her Parent had been standing, and she did not put her weight back on that foot the next time. That is a smaller claim than self-help usually wants to make, and it is the one this model will actually hold.

The three voices are the nouns of Berne's system. The next question is what happens when one voice answers another: when Priya's Parent speaks to Marcus's Child, when Elena's Adult asks Halden's Parent a question. That is where conversations stabilize, or stop working, or turn into something neither person signed up for.
