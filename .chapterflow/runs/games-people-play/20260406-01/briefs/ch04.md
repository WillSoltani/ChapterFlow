Author: Eric Berne
Publication Year: 1964
Edition / Translation: First edition (Grove Press, 1964)

Chapter Title: Why Games Feel So Hard to Stop
Position In Book: Fourth theoretical chapter; the synthesis chapter that pays the debt Ch3 left open. Ch3 said games are sticky even once you can see them; Ch4 explains the stickiness in terms of strokes (the unit the payoff is made of) and life positions (the deeper position the payoff is protecting).
Source Sidecar Path: .chapterflow/runs/games-people-play/20260406-01/sidecars/source/ch04.source.txt

Core Claim:
Games are sticky for two reasons that compound. The first is that the payoff is made of something every adult actually needs and rarely has a clean source for: strokes, the basic units of social acknowledgment that human beings require to stay psychologically functional. Even a negative stroke is metabolically better than no stroke at all, which is why people can run painful games for decades. The second reason is that games confirm the player's existing life position about themselves and the world (I'm OK / I'm not OK, You're OK / You're not OK), and life positions are sticky because they were learned early and have organized everything since. A game is not just a habit. It is a reliable stroke-delivery system whose payoff also happens to confirm a position the player has been holding since they were very small.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
This is the only chapter that names what the payoff is made of. Ch3 used the word "payoff" structurally. Ch4 opens it up: the payoff is strokes, plus a confirmation of a life position. Without this chapter, the rest of the book is a catalog of patterns the reader cannot explain the persistence of. With it, every later chapter (life games, marital games, party games, therapy games, even the closing chapter on autonomy) is reading the same two underlying things.

Author Logic Chain:
First the author establishes that human beings need a steady supply of social acknowledgment to stay functional, and that this need is not optional or weak. Then he names the unit: a stroke. Then he shows that strokes can be positive or negative, and that, when no positive ones are available, people will reliably accept negative ones rather than starve. Then he introduces the second concept: every person carries a life position about themselves and others (I'm OK / not OK; you're OK / not OK), formed early and confirmed every day by the kinds of transactions they get involved in. Then he closes the loop: a game is sticky because it pays out strokes on a reliable schedule AND because the strokes it pays out confirm the player's existing life position. To leave the game requires both a new source of strokes AND a willingness to update the life position the game has been confirming. Both of those are hard.

Required Anchors:
- A solo open-source maintainer named Maya who maintains a niche library that almost nobody uses. Once a month, somebody files an angry GitHub issue accusing her of not caring. Maya hates these issues. Maya also knows, in some part of herself she does not look at directly, that she would feel worse if a month went by with no issue at all. The angry issue is a negative stroke. The negative stroke is doing the work of the positive stroke she does not have a reliable source for, and it is also confirming the life position she has been holding since middle school: "I'm not OK, but at least somebody is paying attention."
- A retired schoolteacher named Theo who spends every Sunday at his daughter Ren's house and ends every visit by criticizing the way Ren is raising her kids. Ren tells him every week to stop. He says he is sorry. He does it again next Sunday. The criticism is the only reliable way Theo has of being needed in the family, and it confirms his lifelong position that you have to point out what is wrong or nothing will get fixed. The criticism is an acidic stroke and Theo is starving without it.
- A close friendship between two adults, Inez and Coach Banerjee, in which Inez routinely calls late at night with crises. Banerjee has tried for two years to be helpful, and the calls keep escalating. The calls are paying Inez a steady stream of attention she has not found a way to get during the day, and they are confirming her position that the world is dangerous and she needs a rescuer. Banerjee is the rescuer, and the rescuer role is itself a stroke-delivery system she did not realize she had become dependent on.

Frameworks or Terms Introduced:
- Stroke: Berne's name for the basic unit of social acknowledgment. Positive strokes feel good (a smile, a thanks, a real conversation). Negative strokes feel bad (a criticism, an angry message, a fight) but still register as acknowledgment. Strokes are not optional; humans deprived of strokes deteriorate. The chapter must say this clearly enough that the reader understands why a negative stroke is metabolically better than zero strokes, without being lurid about it.
- Stroke deprivation / stroke economy: the broader framing that every person is operating inside a stroke economy whose supply is limited and unevenly distributed. This is mentioned but not unpacked at length; the reader-facing version uses it as context.
- Six payoff advantages (Berne's original list): mentioned as a footnote-level detail at hard depth only, and in compressed form. Reader prose should not enumerate all six. Structural categories at reader level: strokes, position confirmation, structure (the game gives you something to do), and avoidance (the game keeps you out of a feared situation). Three or four functional categories carry the work without the lurid taxonomy.
- Life position: the basic stance a person takes toward themselves and others, formed early. Berne names four: I'm OK / You're OK (the healthy, game-free stance), I'm OK / You're not OK (projective and often persecutory), I'm not OK / You're OK (depressive and often martyr-like), and I'm not OK / You're not OK (futile, sometimes self-destructive). The chapter should name all four briefly and use the first three in the anchors. The fourth is named honestly but not dramatized.
- The stickiness equation: a game is sticky to the degree that it is the only reliable source of strokes a player has AND it confirms their existing life position. Lower the first or update the second and the game becomes possible to leave.

Specific Applications:
- Reading why a relationship that is "obviously bad" for both people is so hard for either of them to leave: the strokes are reliable, the life positions are confirmed, and the alternative is uncertainty about both.
- Recognizing that "just stop playing the game" is not a serious instruction unless the player has a plan for the strokes the game was paying.
- Understanding why early childhood matters: the life position the game is confirming was usually formed before the player had words.

Common Misreadings:
- Treating "stroke" as a metaphor or as an abstraction. It is not. Berne meant something specific: a unit of social acknowledgment that humans need to stay functional. Treat it as a real thing or the chapter does not work.
- Treating life positions as personality types. They are stances, formed early, that can be updated, although the updating is slow and rarely happens by accident.
- Treating "I'm not OK / You're OK" as just self-criticism. It is the deeper structural stance behind self-criticism, which is much harder to dislodge than the visible behavior.

Counterarguments or Limits:
The stroke model is a useful simplification of human social need; it is not a complete theory of motivation. Some games are sustained by power, by economic dependence, by trauma, or by structural conditions the model does not name. The chapter must not let the reader believe that every persistent painful pattern is a stroke economy problem.

A harder limit, which has to survive into hard depth: even when the reader correctly diagnoses a game as a stroke-delivery system, the cost of leaving is real. People who lose a reliable source of negative strokes without a positive replacement do not become free; they become miserable in a different shape. The chapter has to be honest about the order of operations: replacement first, then exit.

Cross-Chapter Tensions:
- Ch4 (the why of stickiness) vs Ch3 (the anatomy). Ch3 was diagnostic; Ch4 explains the energetics. Both are necessary; neither is sufficient.
- Ch4 (life positions are sticky) vs Ch10 (autonomy is possible). The two have to be held together. Autonomy is not the easy refusal of life positions; it is the harder, slower work of building a different stroke supply and updating the position the new strokes are allowed to confirm.

Moral Complexity:
Medium-high. The chapter is talking about why people stay in painful patterns, and the easy reader response is contempt or pity. Both are wrong. Berne's framing is that the game-player is solving a real problem (stroke deprivation) with the only tool they have, and that the chapter has to honor that. Modern framing must be careful not to pathologize negative stroke seeking; it is not a character flaw, it is metabolism.

Concept Budget:
- target concept count: 4
- rationale: stroke (the unit), positive vs negative strokes (the mechanism), life position (the second leg), the stickiness equation (synthesis). The six-payoff taxonomy is named only as a footnote at hard depth.

Hard-Depth Minimum:
Hard must preserve this tension: order of operations matters. Telling someone to "just leave the game" without first understanding what the game is paying them is not a neutral instruction; it is a request that they accept stroke deprivation in exchange for freedom they cannot yet feel. The threshold question for hard depth: if the only honest exit from a painful game requires building a new stroke supply first, what does the chapter actually advise the reader to do today?

Unsupported Zones:
- Neurochemistry of attachment, oxytocin, vagal tone, etc. These are not in Berne's source and would be inventing authority.
- Claims about stroke deprivation rates, statistics, or therapy outcomes.
- Specific catalog game names from later chapters (Why Don't You Yes But, NIGYSOB, Alcoholic — those belong to Chs 5–8 even if the structural patterns are similar).
- Any unpacking of the awareness/spontaneity/intimacy triad (Ch10).
- Any claim that life positions are "fixed" or "innate." They are formed early and they are sticky, but they are formed by experience and can be updated.
