Book: Games People Play / Eric Berne / 1964 / games-people-play

Chapter Number: 9
Chapter Title: Games That Aren't Worth Fighting
Position: Fifth and final catalog chapter. Covers Berne's 'good games' category — games whose structure is still ulterior but whose net effect on the world is positive.

Core Claim:
Berne named a small number of games whose structure is still the structure of a game (ulterior, with a payoff, with players who are getting something they have not named) but whose effect on the world is good enough that the chapter's honest recommendation is to leave them alone. Busman's Holiday, Cavalier, Happy To Help, Homely Sage. Each is a game in the technical sense. Each produces something real and useful that neither the players nor the people around them would be better off without. The chapter's central move is the distinction between seeing a game and fighting it. Not every game needs an antithesis. Some games are the best available version of a trade between what a player can give and what they need.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
This is the only chapter that resists the antithesis instinct the first eight chapters have been building. Every prior catalog chapter gave the reader a set of moves to interrupt a pattern. This one says: some patterns should not be interrupted. The distinctive claim is that the reader has to learn not to swing the tool.

Note on chapter scope: Ch9 is shorter than the other catalog chapters per the skeleton's thin-chapter mitigation strategy. The source material for good games is thinner than for other categories. The chapter compensates with example richness and a single sustained argument rather than multiple distinct analytical moves.

Required Anchors:
- Busman's Holiday: a senior software engineer named Margot who spends her three-week vacation volunteering for an open-source project she does not maintain professionally. The volunteering is real work. Her motives are mixed (she likes being an expert, she enjoys being thanked, she wants the structure). The project benefits. Margot benefits. The game structure is still there. The chapter's position: leave it alone.
- Homely Sage: a retired librarian named Harriet who runs the Saturday morning writing workshop at her local library and offers hard-won advice to writers in their twenties and thirties without insisting on being heeded. Knowledge is offered as a gift, not a test. The motive includes legacy. The workshop is real. The advice is often good.
- Happy To Help: a neighbor named Yonatan who shovels the walk for the elderly couple next door every snowstorm without being asked. He likes being the kind of neighbor who shovels walks. The walk gets shoveled. The elderly couple do not get injured.

Moral complexity: Low. The chapter's risk is not in being harsh; the risk is in being cynical about generosity. The chapter must not undercut the genuine good these patterns produce while still honestly naming the ulterior structure.

Concept budget: 2 (the good-games category, and the see-it-but-don't-fight-it instruction).

Hard-depth minimum: The instruction not to interrupt some games is harder than it sounds because the previous chapters have been training the reader to interrupt. The threshold question: if a game is producing good outcomes, does noticing its ulterior structure create any obligation to change anything? The chapter answers no, and holds that answer.

Forbidden: moralizing generosity; implying the players are secretly selfish; catalog names from Ch5-8; Ch10 vocabulary (autonomy, awareness, spontaneity, intimacy); Ch1-8 character names.

New Ch9 cast: Margot, Harriet, Yonatan, the Jensens (elderly neighbors), Rania, Piotr, Ms. Okafor, Quentin, Vasilisa, Augustus.
