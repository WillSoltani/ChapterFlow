Chapter Title: Party and Social Games
Position: Third catalog chapter; short-duration group patterns.
Source Sidecar: .chapterflow/runs/games-people-play/20260406-01/sidecars/source/ch07.source.txt

Core Claim:
Party games are the cheapest form of the same avoidance the previous two chapters described. They run in groups, in short bursts, and they bond people together through shared grievance, shared deflection, or shared entertainment without requiring anyone to be genuinely present with anyone else. Three patterns: Ain't It Awful (bonding through shared deploring), Why Don't You Yes But (advice-rejection as stroke supply), and Blemish (finding fault as social superiority management). Each serves a social function that people actually need — belonging, acknowledgment, comparison — at a low intimacy cost.

Core distinction: party games are not as individually costly as life or marital games, but they are socially expensive in aggregate. A room full of people running Ain't It Awful is a room full of people who have not connected with each other. The chapter's limit: not all shared complaining is a game. Some is appropriate solidarity. The difference is in the dynamics — whether the deploring is moving toward something or circling in place.

Required Anchors:
- Ain't It Awful: a book club where the first forty minutes are spent deploying the state of publishing, the education system, and the economy, and then the book is discussed for fifteen minutes. Nobody is wrong about the things they are deploring. The deploring is also not the reason the people came. It is the warmup, and for three members it has become the reason they come.
- Why Don't You Yes But: a coworker named Pita who describes a problem to three colleagues, receives five suggestions, rejects all five with perfectly reasonable objections, and leaves the conversation having proved that no one understands the problem. The colleagues feel subtly inadequate. Pita feels confirmed.
- Blemish: a design critique session where one reviewer, Cass, finds a flaw in every piece presented and raises each flaw first, before any strength is noted. The session ends and every designer feels slightly smaller. Cass feels rigorous.

Moral complexity: LOW. These patterns are the least individually harmful of the catalog. The chapter does not moralize them. It describes them structurally and names the social cost.

Hard-depth minimum: Why Don't You Yes But is the hardest pattern to interrupt because the social contract of the advice-giving situation makes refusing to advise seem rude. The hard-depth antithesis for WDYYB is not refusing to engage but asking the question that hands the problem back: 'What do you think would actually help?' The threshold: when does asking that question become its own game?
