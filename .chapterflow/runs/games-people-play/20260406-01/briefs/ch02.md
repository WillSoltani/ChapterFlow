Core Claim:
A transaction is the smallest unit of social exchange: one person says or does something, the other responds. Berne's contribution was to classify the response by which state it came from and which state it was aimed at. When the response comes from the state that was addressed, and is aimed back at the state that sent the original message, the transaction is complementary and the conversation can continue indefinitely. When the response comes from an unexpected state, or is aimed somewhere else, the transaction is crossed, and the conversation breaks. Most communication failures that feel personal are not personal. They are crossed transactions, and they can be diagrammed.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
This is the only chapter in the book that teaches the reader to read a single exchange as a vector — sender state, receiver state, return vector — and to predict whether the conversation will stabilize or fall apart. Ch1 gave the reader three nouns. Ch2 gives them a way to draw arrows between the nouns. Ch3 will add the second layer (ulterior) on top of the diagram. Without the diagram, the rest of the book has nothing to plot itself on.

Author Logic Chain:
First the author establishes that any single exchange can be drawn as an arrow from one ego state in person A to one ego state in person B, plus a return arrow from B to A. Then he shows that when the return arrow lands where person A expected it (the same state pair, reversed), the conversation is stable: it can run for hours on the same vector. Then he shows that when the return arrow lands somewhere unexpected, the conversation goes sideways instantly, and the people in it usually feel the break before they can name it. Then he concludes that this is a tool, not a metaphor: most stalled conversations are crossed transactions, and crossed transactions can be diagnosed and answered.

Required Anchors:
- A workplace request between Tomas (a project manager) and his colleague Aiya. Tomas asks an Adult-to-Adult question about a deadline. Aiya answers from Parent, with a faintly disappointed tone about how "we have been over this before." The question was a vector from Adult to Adult; the answer came back from Parent aimed at Child. The meeting goes nowhere for the next ten minutes, and neither of them can quite say why.
- A swim coach (Coach Reyes) and a thirteen-year-old swimmer (Diego) at the pool deck after a bad race. Reyes addresses Diego from Nurturing Parent: "It's okay, you swam your best." Diego responds from Adapted Child: "Thanks, coach." This is complementary. The conversation could repeat itself for years and never break. It is also not the conversation Diego actually needs, but that is a different problem.
- A late-night phone call between Olivia and her older sister Kenji. Olivia, Child to Parent, asks for comfort about a bad week. Kenji, instead of answering from Parent, answers from Adult: "What specifically happened on Tuesday?" The conversation crosses immediately. Olivia hears the question as cold. Kenji thinks she is being helpful. Both are technically right.

Frameworks or Terms Introduced:
- Transaction: one unit of exchange (a stimulus + a response).
- Complementary transaction: the response comes from the state that was addressed, and is aimed back at the state that sent the stimulus. The diagram has two parallel arrows. Conversation is stable.
- Crossed transaction: the response comes from a different state than the one that was addressed, OR is aimed at a different state than the one that sent the stimulus. The diagram has crossing arrows. Conversation breaks.
- The third type, ulterior, is named here only as a forward-pointer to Ch3. It is not unpacked.

Specific Applications:
- Diagnosing why a stalled work conversation will not move forward by drawing the most recent two arrows and looking for the cross.
- Recognizing that a complementary exchange can run forever on the wrong vector, and noticing when "easy conversation" is also "going nowhere."
- Choosing whether to respond complementarily to keep a conversation going, or deliberately cross it to interrupt a stuck loop.

Common Misreadings:
- Treating "complementary" as "good" and "crossed" as "bad." Complementary transactions are stable, but stability is not value. A long-running Parent-Child loop between coworkers is complementary and useless. Some crossed transactions are repairs.
- Treating the diagram as a literal claim about what is happening inside the brain. The arrows are a notation for what is happening in the room, not a model of cognition.
- Assuming the speaker chose which state to send from. Ch1 already settled this, but the reader will be tempted to forget it the moment they have a diagram in front of them.

Counterarguments or Limits:
The diagram is coarse. Real conversations involve many states cycling through fast, group dynamics, history, and power asymmetries that the two-arrow picture cannot represent. The diagram is useful for the same reason a chord chart is useful: it shows you the bones of the exchange so you can see whether the bones are aligned. It does not show you the music.

A harder limit, which has to survive into hard depth: noticing a crossed transaction does not always tell you what to do about it. Sometimes the right answer is to recross deliberately (sending an Adult response to a Parent attack to refuse the assigned Child role). Sometimes the right answer is to accept the cross, name it, and ask the other person which conversation they want to have. Sometimes there is no good answer in the moment and the only intervention is later. The chapter must say this.

Cross-Chapter Tensions:
- Ch2 (single exchanges) vs Ch3 (sustained games): a single crossed transaction is usually accidental; a sustained pattern of crossed transactions in the same relationship is closer to a game.
- Ch2 (the diagram is observational) vs Ch4 (sustained patterns are sticky): the chapter must not let the reader believe that drawing the diagram fixes the relationship.

Moral Complexity:
Low. The chapter is mechanical. Watch only that "crossed" is not framed as moral failure on either party's part.

Concept Budget:
- target concept count: 3
- rationale: transaction (the unit), complementary (the stable case), crossed (the broken case). Ulterior is named only as a forward-pointer, not unpacked.

Hard-Depth Minimum:
Hard must preserve this tension: noticing a crossed transaction does not by itself tell you what to do about it. The diagram is diagnostic, and the next move is a separate decision that depends on what you actually want from the conversation. The threshold question for hard depth: when you see a cross, how do you know whether to cross back, accept it, or step out?
