Core Claim:
At any moment, an adult is speaking and behaving from one of three live states that the person around them can actually observe. Berne names them Parent (rules and judgments learned from early authority figures), Adult (present-tense data-handling), and Child (the original emotional and reactive patterns from one's own childhood, still operating). The states are not metaphors. They show up as changes in voice, posture, vocabulary, and the kind of response the speaker expects back. Learning to notice which state is speaking, in yourself and in someone else, is the first lever for changing a conversation that keeps going wrong.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
No other chapter in the book is about three-state observation. Later chapters assume the reader can already tell when a person has shifted from Adult into Parent mid-sentence; Chapter 1 is the only place that skill is taught. The chapter's distinct craft is showing a shift in real time inside a concrete scene, not defining the three states abstractly. If the reader can recognize a state-shift in someone they know by the last page, the chapter did its job.

Author Logic Chain:
First the author establishes that adult behavior is not one thing: the same person can switch, inside a single conversation, into a voice that sounds like their mother and then back into a neutral, problem-solving register. Then he shows that these switches are observable from the outside — voice, posture, and word choice all change. Then he concludes that this gives people a practical vocabulary: instead of saying "he's being difficult," you can say "his Parent is talking to my Child," and once you can name the pattern you can interrupt it.

Required Anchors:
- A software code review where a senior engineer's feedback shifts mid-sentence from neutral technical comment ("this function mutates state") into a clipped, fault-finding tone that sounds like a teacher scolding ("I don't know why I keep having to explain this"). The listener's shoulders tighten. Nothing about the code changed; the state of the speaker did, and the listener's state answered.
- A graduate seminar where a student asks what looks like a factual question about a reading, and the professor answers as though the student has challenged their authority. The question was Adult-to-Adult. The answer came from Parent aimed at Child. The room goes quiet for reasons nobody names.
- A roommate conflict over dishes where one roommate starts with a reasonable scheduling proposal and, by the third sentence, is making a moral speech about responsibility. The scheduling problem was solvable; the Parent voice made it unsolvable.

Frameworks or Terms Introduced:
- Ego state: a coherent set of feelings and behaviors a person is in at a given moment. Paraphrase as "behavioral state" or "voice" on first encounter.
- Parent: the state that speaks in rules, judgments, and shoulds, picked up early from authority figures. Has a Critical face (restrictive, fault-finding) and a Nurturing face (supportive, caretaking).
- Adult: the state that observes and evaluates the situation in front of it, in the present, using current information. Neutral in tone. The only state that can update.
- Child: the state that still carries the original feelings from one's own childhood. Comes in several flavors: Natural (spontaneous, playful), Adapted (compliant or rebellious), Little Professor (intuitive, clever in a childlike way).

Specific Applications:
- Catching the moment a work conversation switches out of Adult without either person noticing, and gently moving it back.
- Noticing when your own response is coming from Child (hurt, defensive, wanting approval) rather than Adult, and buying yourself five seconds before you speak.
- Recognizing that "that's just how it's done" is almost always the Parent state talking, and deciding whether to obey, question, or update it.

Common Misreadings:
- Treating Parent / Adult / Child as personality types. They are states a single person moves through, not three kinds of people.
- Treating Adult as "good" and Child as "bad." The Child carries creativity and joy; a person with no access to their Child is not healthier, they are flatter.
- Treating the model as diagnosis. It is an observational tool. The point is to notice, not to label.

Counterarguments or Limits:
The model is deliberately coarse. Three categories cannot capture the full inner life of a person. It leaves out culture, power, neurodivergence, and context. The reason to use it is speed — it is something you can do in real time in a room — not completeness. The chapter must say this, so the reader does not mistake a crude observational tool for a theory of mind.

A harder limit: state-shifting is not voluntary in the way willpower is voluntary. A person does not decide "I will now be Adult" and become Adult. Noticing that you are in Child mode can sometimes loosen it; it rarely switches it off. The chapter's hard-depth must preserve this: the tool is observational, not executive.

Moral Complexity:
Berne's original case studies use examples drawn from mid-century American psychiatric practice, often gendered and frequently condescending. This chapter should not endorse those framings. Examples are to be drawn from neutral contemporary settings. The language "nurturing Parent" is fine (it is structural), but specific 1964 anecdotes about mothers, housewives, and patients are not to be imported.

Concept Budget:
- target concept count: 3
- rationale: Parent, Adult, Child. The chapter must not sprawl into discussing Critical vs Nurturing Parent, or all five sub-states of Child, at the level of the reader-facing prose. Those distinctions can appear briefly in Hard depth as the structural refinement that makes the model less crude, but the chapter is about three states, not eleven.

Hard-Depth Minimum:
Hard must preserve this tension: noticing a state shift is observational, not executive. You cannot will yourself into Adult; you can only notice which state is actually speaking right now. A person who thinks the model is a switch will feel betrayed by it the first time they try to "be Adult" and catch themselves sliding back into Child. The threshold question for hard depth: if the model cannot make me choose a state, what is it actually for?

Unsupported Zones:
- Neurochemistry of any kind (no cortisol, no mirror neurons, no vagal tone).
- Evolutionary psychology framings.
- Claims about effectiveness rates of transactional analysis as a therapy.
- Any reference to Freud by name beyond "not those three."
- Any claim that naming a state makes it change.
