Author: Eric Berne

Chapter Number: 5
Chapter Title: Life Games
Position In Book: First catalog chapter. Opens the thesaurus with the category of games that run for years or decades and tend to shape the player's whole biography. Assumes all four prior chapters.
Source Sidecar Path: .chapterflow/runs/games-people-play/20260406-01/sidecars/source/ch05.source.txt

Core Claim:
Life games are the long-running ones. They last years, sometimes whole decades, and the script of each is recognizable across very different players: the same opening move, the same predictable responses, the same switch, the same payoff. This chapter covers four patterns from Berne's catalog (Kick Me, NIGYSOB, See What You Made Me Do, and one composite pattern structurally similar to Debtor) and shows the anatomy from Chapter 3 working on real material. The chapter's thesis is not that these are bad people or weak people. It is that these are people who have found a reliable stroke-delivery system whose cost happens to be organized around years.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
This is the first catalog chapter, and it sets the tone for all the others. It must demonstrate the anatomy on four distinct patterns without collapsing them into one lesson. Each game must be unmistakably itself — the Kick Me player is not the NIGYSOB player — while also being legible through the same framework. The catalog form is the new form here: four worked examples instead of one governing argument.

Author Logic Chain:
The chapter opens with the feature that distinguishes life games from shorter games: time. Then it walks the reader through four patterns, applying the four-part anatomy to each. It closes by naming what all four have in common: a life position that the game has been confirming long enough to feel like identity.

Required Anchors:
- Greta, who manages a small graphic design studio and reliably puts herself in situations where her business partners will eventually take advantage of her. The thesis is: she makes herself exploitable and then is hurt when she is exploited. The antithesis is: the partner who declines to exploit her, treats her generously, and watches Greta find another partner who will. The payoff is: confirmation of the position "I'm not OK / you're OK, and specifically you will take what you can."
- Felix, the department head at a regional hospital, who collects offenses. Every slight, every breach of protocol, every procedural shortfall is catalogued, and periodically Felix finds a moment when the offender has made an unambiguous mistake and releases the stored-up anger as a justified response. Berne called this pattern NIGYSOB (Now I've Got You, You S.O.B.). The payoff is moral superiority and guilt-free rage. The antithesis is: the staff member who is impeccable in their procedure and gives Felix no opening.
- Ivar, a project coordinator, whose standard explanation for anything that goes wrong in a project is the person who interrupted or distracted him at the critical moment. Berne called this SWYMD (See What You Made Me Do). The payoff is avoidance of responsibility. The antithesis is the colleague who refuses to accept the blame assignment.
- Mona, a freelancer who has been cycling through a pattern for ten years: takes on work she cannot realistically complete for a rate she cannot realistically sustain, then spends the next several months proving that nothing can be done about this and everyone should feel sorry for her. Structurally similar to Debtor in the source.

Allowed Quotes or Near-Quotes: paraphrase_only.

Frameworks or Terms Introduced:
- Life games (as a category): games that run over years or decades and tend to shape the player's biography, not just their afternoons.
- The four specific game patterns above, described structurally but NOT by Berne's catalog names in reader prose (names are factual history and appear in hard depth at most; the reader-facing prose describes the structure).

Specific Applications:
- Recognizing the long-running pattern in a relationship before the next instance confirms it.
- Reading the payoff of a life game in terms of both strokes and the life position being confirmed (applying Ch4).
- Choosing the antithesis knowing it will cost something (applying Ch3's limit).

Common Misreadings:
- Treating catalog entries as personality types. Greta is not "an exploited person." She is a person running a specific pattern for a specific payoff.
- Assuming the antithesis is always available. For some long-running life games, the antithesis requires years of structural change, not a single conversation.

Counterarguments or Limits:
Life games are an abstraction over a real distribution. Many people who repeat painful patterns are doing so under structural constraint (economic dependence, power asymmetry, genuine victimization) that the game vocabulary does not and should not flatten into a behavioral pattern. The chapter must say this without undermining the model.

The harder limit: for a life game that has been running thirty years, the antithesis is not a piece of advice. It is a multi-year project. The chapter is honest about this scale.

Previous Chapter Bridge: Ch4 explained why games are sticky. Ch5 shows what that stickiness looks like at the scale of a life.

Next Chapter Bridge: Marital games (Ch6) are life games with an intimate partner. The patterns are similar but the stakes are different.

Moral Complexity:
High. Some of the patterns described (especially the Kick Me variant and the Debtor-adjacent pattern) are often associated with experiences of trauma and genuine harm. The chapter must hold that the model is diagnostic and not contemptuous. Real victimization exists; the game framework is about the self-perpetuating pattern layered on top of it, when that layer exists.

Concept Budget: 4 (life games as category, the four game patterns as worked examples of Ch3 anatomy plus Ch4 energetics, the antithesis-at-life-scale limit, the no-judgment framing).

Hard-Depth Minimum:
Hard must address this: the antithesis for a life game that has been running for decades is not a one-sentence move. It is a project. A reader who leaves this chapter thinking they can break Greta's pattern with one honest conversation about her generosity is mistaken. The threshold question: if the antithesis requires years, is seeing the game still worth the cost of seeing it?

Unsupported Zones:
- Diagnosis of any real DSM condition from game patterns.
- Neurochemistry, trauma theory, or attachment science (not in source).
- Claiming that all repeated victimization is a game. Some is; not all is.
- Berne's original catalog names (Alcoholic, Kick Me, NIGYSOB, SWYMD, Debtor) should be handled carefully — they are historical names, acceptable in hard depth briefly, but not as the primary framing in reader prose.
- Ch10 autonomy vocabulary.
