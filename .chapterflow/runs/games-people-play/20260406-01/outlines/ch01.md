Chapter Promise:
By the end, the reader can watch a conversation and see, in real time, when a person's state shifts — and can catch themselves doing the same thing.

Opening Move:
Start inside the code review between Priya and Marcus. No announcement that the chapter is about ego states. The shift happens in front of the reader before the reader knows what they are watching.

Anchor Allocation:
- anchor 1 (Priya/Marcus code review): P1, P2, returned to briefly in P6.
- anchor 2 (Prof. Halden / Elena seminar): P3.
- anchor 3 (Noor/Chidi dishes): P4, returned to in P6.

Paragraph Job Map:
- P1: the scene opens inside Priya's comment on a code review. Adult-to-Adult technical note. Final clause of the paragraph, the voice shifts. Reader feels the shift before it is named.
- P2: name what just happened. Introduce the idea that a person has more than one state available at once, and that Priya just traded one for another. Do not give the three names yet. Describe the difference by what changed: her voice, the kind of reply she now expects, the fact that Marcus's shoulders answered before his mouth did.
- P3: move to Prof. Halden and Elena. Here the shift is the other direction — a question addressed to an Adult gets answered by a Parent who takes the question as a challenge. Use this paragraph to give the model its three names: Parent, Adult, Child. One sentence each on what each carries. Do not turn it into a taxonomy.
- P4: Noor and Chidi. The personal example. Show a reasonable scheduling proposal sliding into a moral speech. Show that the speaker (Noor) did not plan the shift; it happened to her. This paragraph carries the first real insight: the states are not chosen, they are observed.
- P5: the limit. You cannot will yourself into Adult. Noticing is an observational move, not an executive one. This is the paragraph that prevents the chapter from becoming a self-help promise. It has to land honestly.
- P6: the payoff of the tool anyway. Return to Priya and to Noor. Show what changes when you can name which state is speaking: not the state itself, but the question you ask next. You stop trying to win the argument and start trying to locate where the conversation actually is.
- P7: bridge to Ch2 without using the word "transaction." The model gave us three voices. The next question is what happens when one voice answers another.

Required Distinction or Mechanism:
The chapter must draw a clean line between observation (what this tool is good for) and control (what this tool is not good for). That distinction is what separates Ch1 from every later chapter in the book.

Hard-Depth Tension:
You cannot will your way into Adult. Noticing a state rarely switches it off; it only loosens its grip, and sometimes not even that. The tool is diagnostic, not executive.

Threshold Question:
If naming the state cannot make me choose it, what is noticing actually for?

Takeaway Count Lock:
- Easy: 3 (always)
- Medium: 5
- Hard: 6

Rationale for hard = 6: the framework is three concepts, but hard must also carry the observation-vs-control distinction, the Critical/Nurturing refinement, and the threshold question. Six is the right number without sprawling.

Hard Takeaway Topics:
- Takeaway 1: three states are observable, not inferred. The tool works because the signals are on the outside of the person.
- Takeaway 2: each state carries a characteristic vocabulary and cadence; the Parent speaks in rules, the Adult in observations, the Child in wants.
- Takeaway 3: a person slips between states without deciding to. Most shifts are unchosen and unnoticed.
- Takeaway 4: the Parent has two faces — Critical and Nurturing — and both are Parent. A warm voice telling you what you should feel is still Parent.
- Takeaway 5: noticing a state does not change it. Noticing only gives you a place to stand.
- Takeaway 6: the point of the model is not to diagnose the other person. It is to stop reading their behavior as "they're being difficult" and start reading it as "which voice is in the room right now."

Scenario Lesson Map:
- Scenario 1 (short_dialogue, work, Priya/Marcus): teaches what a mid-sentence shift out of Adult sounds like.
- Scenario 2 (first_person, work, Sam's 1:1 as new manager): teaches how to notice Parent rising in your own throat before it arrives in the room.
- Scenario 3 (third_person_scene, school, Halden/Elena): teaches the cost of answering an Adult question from Parent.
- Scenario 4 (coaching_script, school, Ms. Alvarez): teaches how to name the state for a pair of kids without jargon, so they can see it.
- Scenario 5 (narrative_flashback, personal, Noor/Chidi): teaches retrospective recognition — catching the shift hours later.
- Scenario 6 (inner_monologue, personal, Jamal's mother Danielle at the parent-teacher conference): teaches Child reactions in oneself during a formal setting.

Scenario Vividness Targets:
At least 4 of 6 scenarios include one sensory or situational detail:
- Priya/Marcus: the tightening of Marcus's shoulders; the line of code on screen.
- Halden/Elena: the quiet that arrives after the answer, the highlighter still in Elena's hand.
- Noor/Chidi: the kitchen counter, the half-full sink.
- Danielle/Ms. Alvarez: the low chair, the strip of tape on the classroom wall.

Preview Constraint:
The preview of Ch2 must hold a question, not summarize. The question is: now that we have three voices, what happens when one voice answers another?

Genericity Risks:
- risk 1: describing the three states as "inner critic / inner adult / inner child" in a way that collapses into pop-psych territory.
- risk 2: using the word "triggered" or any contemporary self-help vocabulary that would drift the chapter toward another book.
- risk 3: turning the code review into a generic "feedback" example instead of a specific state-shift.

Forbidden Drift:
- any implication that noticing equals fixing.
- any reference to brain regions or hormones.
- any extension into games or transactions (those are later chapters).
- any leak of the phrase "ego state" from the brief into the reader prose unless it is paraphrased first.
