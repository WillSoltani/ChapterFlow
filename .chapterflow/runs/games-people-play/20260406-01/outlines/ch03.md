Chapter Promise:
By the end, the reader can watch a two-layer conversation, name the layer that is doing the real work, and see the repeating shape of the game the conversation is running inside.

Opening Move:
Open inside the dinner party with Lena. The third rejected suggestion is the moment the reader feels the second layer before it is named.

Anchor Allocation:
- anchor 1 (Lena's dinner party): P1, P2, returned in P5.
- anchor 2 (Victor's retrospective): P3.
- anchor 3 (Priscilla and Nils): P4, returned to briefly in P6.

Paragraph Job Map:
- P1: Lena at the dinner party. Third suggestion, third "yes, but." The reader feels the social level (request for help) and feels the other thing underneath (demonstration of helplessness). Do not name the layers yet.
- P2: name what just happened. Every transaction in the conversation has had two levels running at once. The words were one kind of exchange (asking and offering solutions). Underneath, a different exchange was running (Lena demonstrating that the problem is insoluble, the guests providing attention she could call advice-giving). Introduce "ulterior" as the word for the second layer.
- P3: Victor's retrospective. Switch the setting, keep the structure. Show that when a pattern like this runs across many conversations between the same people, it starts to have a shape: opening move, predictable counter, switch, payoff. Introduce "game" in Berne's technical sense as the word for the repeating shape. Distinguish from the loose sense of "playing games with someone."
- P4: Priscilla and Nils. Show the four-part anatomy explicitly, using the Priscilla-offering-help example. Thesis: she offers, he accepts, she provides it in a way that guarantees failure. Antithesis: he declines the help, or accepts it and insists on using it his own way. Dynamics: her need to keep him in the position of someone who needs her. Payoff: a confirmation of the parent-child role on both sides. Name all four.
- P5: return to Lena. Use the anatomy on her. Thesis = problem presented + every suggestion rejected. Antithesis = the guest who refuses to offer more advice and asks instead what Lena has already tried. Dynamics = her need to prove the problem is insoluble. Payoff = strokes from repeated attention plus confirmation that no one can help her. Preview "stroke" once as a forward-pointer to Ch4; do not unpack it.
- P6: the limit. The antithesis is expensive. Breaking a game takes away the payoff both players have been using to keep something else at bay. If Lena had a better source of those strokes, she would already be getting them elsewhere. If Priscilla had a stable way to feel needed that was not at her son's expense, she would already be using it. The antithesis is sometimes the right move and sometimes the move that costs more than the game. Seeing the game is always valuable; choosing to break it is a separate, costly decision the reader makes for themselves.
- P7: bridge to Ch4. The anatomy explains what games are. It does not yet explain why they are so hard to stop even once you can see them. That answer is in the payoff, specifically in what the payoff is made of and what deeper position it is protecting.

Required Distinction or Mechanism:
The chapter must distinguish (a) an ulterior transaction (a single two-layer exchange) from (b) a game (a sustained pattern of ulterior transactions with a repeating shape and a payoff). Many chapters in later books conflate these; Berne does not. The chapter must not.

Hard-Depth Tension:
The antithesis is expensive. Breaking the game takes away the payoff the player has been using. Seeing the game and choosing not to break it is a legitimate decision if the player does not yet have a replacement source of what the payoff was providing.

Threshold Question:
If the antithesis costs the player something they actually needed, is seeing the game still a net good?

Takeaway Count Lock:
- Easy: 3
- Medium: 6
- Hard: 7

Rationale for hard = 7: there are four structural features of a game (thesis, antithesis, dynamics, payoff), plus the two-layer framing, plus the anatomy-is-not-a-hammer limit, plus the antithesis-is-expensive limit. Seven is justified and necessary.

Hard Takeaway Topics:
- Takeaway 1: some transactions run on two levels; the psychological level is usually the one that actually matters.
- Takeaway 2: a game in Berne's sense is an ongoing series of ulterior transactions with a predictable payoff; not a metaphor, not the loose sense of "playing games."
- Takeaway 3: every game has a thesis — a repeating plot with an opening move, a predictable counter, a switch, and a payoff moment.
- Takeaway 4: every game has an antithesis — the move that would break it by refusing the role the game has assigned.
- Takeaway 5: the dynamics are the psychological mechanism that keeps the game running; the payoff is what both players get out of losing.
- Takeaway 6: the antithesis is expensive. Breaking a game takes away the payoff the player has been using, and if they do not have a better source of it, naming the game can hurt before it helps.
- Takeaway 7: seeing the game is always valuable; choosing to break it is a separate, costly decision that belongs to the player, not to the observer.

Preview Constraint:

Genericity Risks:

- risk 2: treating the anatomy as a universal pattern detector that applies to every hard conversation.
- risk 3: framing game-players as manipulative instead of adaptive.
