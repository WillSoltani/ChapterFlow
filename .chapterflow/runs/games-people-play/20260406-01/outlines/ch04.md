Chapter Promise:
By the end, the reader can answer the question the previous chapter left open: why painful games keep running even when both players can see them. The answer is in two parts (strokes and life positions), and the reader can spot both inside a pattern in their own life by the last paragraph.

Opening Move:
Open inside Maya's monthly angry GitHub issue. The reader feels the stroke economy before the word "stroke" arrives.

Paragraph Job Map:
- P1: open inside Maya's GitHub issue. She is reading an angry comment from a stranger and feeling the bad feelings she always feels. Then, in the last sentence of the paragraph, the harder thing arrives: she catches herself thinking that a month with no angry issue would feel even worse, and she does not yet have a word for why.
- P2: name the unit. Berne called it a stroke. A stroke is the basic unit of social acknowledgment that humans need to stay functional. Strokes can be positive (a smile, a thanks, a real conversation) or negative (a criticism, an angry message, a fight), and the structural fact is that a negative stroke is metabolically better than no stroke at all. Maya's monthly angry issue is a stroke. It is the wrong shape and it pays out anyway.
- P3: cut to Inez and Banerjee. Show that the same machinery operates at the relationship level. Inez calls Banerjee at 11pm with the third crisis of the week. The calls are paying both women: Inez gets reliable attention, Banerjee gets to be the kind of person who is needed in a crisis. Both are getting strokes from the loop. Neither is getting them in any other reliable way.
- P4: introduce the second concept. Behind the strokes is a deeper position the player has been holding since they were small. Berne called it a life position, and he proposed four. I'm OK / You're OK is the healthy, game-free stance. I'm OK / You're not OK is projective and often persecutory. I'm not OK / You're OK is depressive and often martyr-like. I'm not OK / You're not OK is the futile one. Theo on Sunday at his daughter Ren's house is running the second one. The criticism is the move that confirms his lifelong stance that you have to point out what is wrong or nothing will get fixed. Removing the criticism would not just take away the strokes; it would take away the position. Both losses would have to be replaced.
- P5: return to Maya. Apply both legs of the model. The angry GitHub issue pays out a negative stroke and confirms a position Maya has been holding since middle school: "I am not OK, but at least somebody is paying attention." Removing the angry issue without putting something else in its place would not be freedom. It would be silence.
- P6: the limit. The chapter cannot leave the reader with "stop playing the game." The honest order of operations is replacement first, then exit. A reader who tries to leave a game without first finding a different stroke supply will discover, fast, that the game was load-bearing in their life, and will return to it within weeks. The chapter says this clearly because it is the most useful piece of advice it has.
- P7: bridge to Ch5. With strokes and life positions in hand, the catalog is finally readable. The next chapters look at the recurring games that organize whole lives, and at every one of them, the question to ask is the one this chapter has just taught: what is the payoff made of, and what position is it confirming?

Required Distinction or Mechanism:
The chapter must distinguish "the game gives me bad feelings" from "the game pays me strokes I cannot get any other way, and the strokes happen to come in the form of bad feelings." The first framing is moralistic. The second framing is structural. The book depends on the second.

Hard-Depth Tension:
Order of operations. A reader who tries to leave a game without first replacing what the game was paying them will not become free; they will become deprived. The chapter's hardest claim is that "stop playing the game" is not a serious instruction without a replacement plan, and that the replacement plan is the actual work.

Threshold Question:
If the only honest exit from a painful game requires building a new stroke supply first, what does the chapter actually advise the reader to do today?

Hard Takeaway Topics:
- Takeaway 1: humans need a steady supply of social acknowledgment (strokes) to stay functional, and this need is metabolic, not optional.
- Takeaway 2: positive and negative strokes are not equivalents, but a negative stroke is metabolically better than no stroke at all.
- Takeaway 3: every player carries a life position formed early — I'm OK / not OK, you OK / not OK — and the game is confirming it on a schedule.
- Takeaway 4: a game is sticky to the degree it is the only reliable source of strokes a player has AND it confirms their existing life position.
- Takeaway 5: leaving a game requires a replacement stroke supply AND a willingness to update the life position the game has been confirming.
- Takeaway 6: "stop playing the game" is not a serious instruction without a replacement plan; the replacement plan is the work.
- Takeaway 7: the chapter is not contemptuous of game-players. They are solving a real problem (stroke deprivation) with the only reliable tool they have.
