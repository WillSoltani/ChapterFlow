--- Slice A: Part I, transaction type C (ulterior), lines 94-100 of source document ---
Ulterior transaction — the transaction has two levels: a social level (what is visibly said) and a psychological level (what is really going on). The words are one thing, but the actual message and response happen underneath. Berne argues that games are always built on ulterior transactions.

--- Slice B: Part I, Chapter 5 of source document (formal game definition), lines 146-186 ---
A game, in Berne's technical usage, is an ongoing series of complementary ulterior transactions progressing toward a well-defined, predictable outcome. Games are always basically dishonest — the ulterior level contradicts the social level — and the outcome (the payoff) is usually some form of bad feeling that both players are, underneath, looking for.

Every game has these structural features:
  - THESIS: the basic plot of the game, including roles, sequence of moves, and the eventual switch (the moment the social-level story collapses into the psychological-level payoff).
  - ANTITHESIS: what the other person would have to do to refuse to play. The antithesis is usually uncomfortable because it requires ignoring the social-level rules and responding to the ulterior level directly.
  - DYNAMICS: the underlying psychological mechanism that keeps the game running.
  - ADVANTAGES / PAYOFFS: what the player gets out of running the game. Berne lists six types (internal psychological, external psychological, internal social, external social, biological, existential). The chapter does not need to unpack all six; the reader-facing version can use "payoff" without the six-part taxonomy. Stroke theory and life positions belong to Ch4.

Berne also notes that games are a creative adaptation to the problem of needing strokes in a world that does not always offer them freely. Game-players are not contemptible; they are people who have found a reliable source of strokes through a costly pattern, and who do not yet have a better source.

--- Slice C: Ch3 boundary and vocabulary ---
Ch3 covers ONLY ulterior transactions, the formal definition of a game, and the four-part anatomy (thesis/antithesis/dynamics/payoff). It does NOT unpack stroke theory, life positions, or any of the catalog-level game names. The word "stroke" may appear exactly once as a forward-pointer to Ch4, not as a concept introduction.

Forbidden to this chapter:

- Introduction of life positions.

--- Slice E: Hard-depth tension ---
The antithesis is expensive. Breaking a game takes away the payoff both players have been using, and if the player does not yet have a better source of what the payoff was providing, seeing the game is not the same as being able to leave it. The chapter must hold this distinction so the reader is not handed a tool that makes them feel worse when they use it on a close relationship.
