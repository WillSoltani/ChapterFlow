Core Claim:
Chapter 6 argues that negotiation outcomes depend heavily on perceived fairness, loss, and reference points, so the negotiator must understand and shape how the other side experiences what is reasonable.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
This is the perception chapter. It must focus on fairness framing, loss aversion, and anchoring without drifting into abstract psychology detached from negotiation use.

Author Logic Chain:
First the chapter shows that people do not evaluate offers in a vacuum.
Then it argues that fairness and loss pressure shape what feels acceptable.
Then it explores how reference points bend perceived reality.
Then it warns that these frames can harden resistance if used crudely.

Required Anchors:
- source-heading support that this chapter centers on fairness, loss aversion, anchoring, and perception
- continuity from Chapter 5: deep understanding is required before shaping reference points responsibly
- continuity from prior chapters: safety and trust still matter even when the negotiator is working with perception

Counterarguments or Limits:
Perception-shaping is not permission to manipulate recklessly. Fairness framing or loss pressure can backfire if the other side feels tricked, cornered, or ethically dismissed.

Hard-Depth Minimum:
When does bending reality clarify what is already true about risk and fairness, and when does it become crude pressure that collapses trust?
