There is a difference between hearing agreement and hearing understanding. Someone can say yes because they want the conversation to keep moving, because they are tired of resisting, or because they want to be polite. That word may calm the negotiator, but it does not necessarily mean the other side feels seen. Chapter 5 shifts attention to a different signal: "that's right." Voss treats those two words as more valuable because they suggest the other person has finally heard their own view reflected back accurately enough to stop correcting you.

That distinction matters because movement in negotiation often stalls not from lack of proposals, but from lack of felt understanding. The other side may still believe you are working with a distorted map of what matters to them. Until that changes, persuasion remains premature. The chapter argues that accurate summary is what creates the change. When you can state the other side's perspective, constraints, and concerns in a way they recognize as their own, resistance often softens. Not because they lost, but because they no longer need to keep defending the fact that their reality exists.

This is why the chapter follows labeling and no. Chapter 3 established that recognition lowers defensiveness. Chapter 4 established that autonomy lowers it too. Chapter 5 combines those ideas into a deeper standard: before you push for movement, can the other side hear themselves in your summary? A real "that's right" moment means they can. The summary is not a performance of listening. It is a demonstration that you understand the shape of their situation closely enough to describe it from their side.

That does not mean repeating their words mechanically. A useful summary gathers the pressures, priorities, and emotional logic underneath the conversation. It shows the other side that you understand not only what they said, but why it made sense for them to say it. When that lands, the room changes. The other side may become more open to problem-solving because they no longer feel misread. The summary creates permission to move because it creates alignment before persuasion.

The chapter also keeps a necessary warning in place. "That's right" is not magic by itself. Someone can approve a summary politely without feeling fully understood, especially if the summary is shallow, flattering, or steering too obviously toward your agenda. Forced alignment fails for the same reason fake empathy fails. If the other side hears your script instead of their own perspective, the phrase means little. The value depends on genuine accuracy, not on collecting the words.

That is the chapter's real challenge. When does "that's right" signal that the other side feels accurately represented, and when is it only verbal approval of a recap that still missed the live concern? The answer depends on whether the summary captured the person's actual map or merely organized the conversation in a way convenient to you. Chapter 5 therefore raises the bar on listening again: do not settle for agreement that flatters you. Work until the other side recognizes themselves in what you say back.
