Some negotiations feel finished because every visible argument has been exhausted. The positions are clear, the range looks fixed, and the room starts to believe the outcome is already determined. Chapter 10 attacks that feeling by arguing that deadlock may say less about final reality than about incomplete information. What looks settled can change fast when one previously hidden fact suddenly shifts what the whole negotiation means.

That is why the chapter centers on black swans. A black swan is not just an interesting detail. It is hidden information that changes leverage, resets pressure, or creates a breakthrough that did not seem possible before. Chapter 10 treats those discoveries as decisive because negotiation is not only a contest of visible positions. It is also a search through what the room does not yet know.

This deepens the book's larger logic that negotiation is discovery rather than pure combat. Earlier chapters taught the negotiator to listen, label, mirror, and bargain with more discipline. Chapter 10 adds that even strong bargaining logic may still be incomplete if crucial information remains buried. The room can feel hard because the negotiator is reading only what is already visible.

That is why hidden leverage matters. Information asymmetry means one side may know something the other does not: a pressure point, a constraint, a preference, a fear, an outside timeline, or a private incentive. Once that hidden fact surfaces, the negotiation can reorganize itself quickly. A stalled exchange may begin moving not because anyone argued better, but because the room finally understood something real that had been missing.

The chapter also keeps an important boundary in place. Not every odd detail is a black swan. Not every surprise deserves leverage status. The chapter does not authorize speculative storytelling or mystical intuition. It asks the negotiator to keep searching for evidence-based discoveries that materially change the structure of the negotiation rather than over-reading noise.

That is the chapter's hard question. When does hidden information genuinely change leverage, and when is the negotiator only projecting importance onto weak signals? Chapter 10 closes the book by insisting that breakthroughs often belong to the side that keeps discovering longer than everyone else. The negotiation is not finished just because the visible conversation is tired. It may only be unfinished at a deeper level.
