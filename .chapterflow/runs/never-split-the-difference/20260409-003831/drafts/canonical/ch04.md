Hearing yes too early can feel like progress when it is really a sedative. The room relaxes, the negotiator feels validated, and everyone gets to pretend something moved. Then the follow-through never comes. Chapter 4 starts by attacking that false relief. A quick yes may only mean politeness, avoidance, fatigue, or a desire to escape pressure. The word sounds cooperative, but it often hides the fact that the other side does not yet feel safe enough to tell the truth.

That is why the chapter reframes no. In most conversations, people treat no as the enemy. Voss treats it as useful information and often as a safer place to begin. A no lets the other person protect their autonomy. They do not feel cornered into compliance. They feel that they still control their side of the exchange. That sense of control matters because people are more honest when they do not think agreement is being forced out of them.

The mechanism is less sentimental than it first sounds. No is not magical because rejection is noble. It is useful because it lowers threat. When someone can say no, they stop spending as much energy defending themselves against being pushed. That makes room for cleaner disclosure. The negotiator who stops worshipping yes can learn much faster whether the other side actually agrees, what they resist, and what condition would make the conversation real.

This is where no-oriented questions enter. Instead of asking for affirmative commitment too early, the negotiator can frame questions that let the other side say no while still staying engaged. A question such as "Is now a bad time to talk?" or "Would it be ridiculous to explore this?" preserves agency. The other side can answer without feeling trapped. If they say no, the conversation may actually open rather than close because the response came from autonomy rather than pressure.

The chapter keeps one necessary limit in view. No only helps when the conversation remains safe enough to continue. A no can open honesty, but it can also be the endpoint of a relationship or framing that never earned trust. If the room is brittle, disrespectful, or badly timed, no may simply mean stop. The skill is not about forcing every refusal into hidden cooperation. It is about recognizing when a safe no gives you real information and when the conversation needs more groundwork before anything useful can happen.

That limit keeps the chapter from becoming a gimmick. The goal is not to collect no for style points. The goal is to create conditions where truth is cheaper than fake agreement. Chapter 3 showed that recognition lowers defensiveness. Chapter 4 adds that preserved autonomy lowers it too. Together they form a sharper negotiating logic: stop chasing the word that flatters you, and start building the kind of safety that makes the other side willing to answer honestly.
