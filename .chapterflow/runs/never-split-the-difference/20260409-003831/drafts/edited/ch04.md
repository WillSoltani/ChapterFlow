An early yes can feel like momentum when it is really only relief. The negotiator hears agreement, relaxes, and assumes the conversation moved forward. Then the follow-through vanishes. Chapter 4 begins by attacking that false comfort. A quick yes may only mean politeness, fatigue, avoidance, or a wish to get out of the pressure of the moment. The word sounds cooperative, but it often hides the absence of real commitment.

That is why the chapter reframes no. Most people treat no as a setback or a sign that the conversation failed. Voss treats it as information and often as a safer starting point. When someone can say no, they keep their autonomy. They do not feel cornered into compliance. That sense of control matters because people are usually more honest when they do not think agreement is being extracted from them.

No helps because it lowers threat. The move is not valuable because rejection is somehow virtuous. It is valuable because preserved agency reduces defensive behavior. Once the other side feels less pressure to submit, they can reveal what they actually think, what condition they need, or what part of the proposal does not work for them. The negotiator who stops worshipping yes gets a cleaner read on the real state of the conversation.

This is where no-oriented questions become practical. Instead of asking for affirmative commitment too early, the negotiator can ask questions that allow the other side to answer no while still staying engaged. A question like "Is now a bad time to talk?" or "Would it be unreasonable to explore this?" gives the other person room to retain control. If they answer no, the conversation may open rather than close because the answer came from safety instead of pressure.

The chapter also keeps a hard boundary in place. No only helps when the relationship and framing are strong enough for the conversation to continue after it. Sometimes no is just the honest endpoint of a badly timed or weakly prepared exchange. The skill is not to romanticize refusal. It is to understand when a safe no is producing truth and when it is telling you that trust, timing, or framing is not yet strong enough.

That limit keeps the chapter grounded. The goal is not to collect more no responses. The goal is to make honesty cheaper than fake agreement. Chapter 3 lowered defensiveness through recognition. Chapter 4 adds autonomy. Together they show that the negotiator should stop chasing the word that flatters the ego and start building the conditions that make the truth easier to say.
