There is a difference between hearing agreement and hearing understanding. Someone can say yes because they want the conversation to move on, because they are tired of resisting, or because politeness feels easier than correction. That word may calm the negotiator, but it does not prove the other side feels seen. Chapter 5 shifts attention to a different signal: "that's right." Voss treats those two words as more valuable because they suggest the other person has finally heard their own view reflected back accurately enough to stop fixing your map.

That distinction matters because negotiation often stalls when the other side believes you still do not really get what matters to them. More proposals do not fix that. More pressure does not fix it either. The chapter argues that accurate summary is what changes the room. When you can restate the other side's perspective, constraints, and concerns in a way they recognize as their own, resistance often softens. They no longer need to keep fighting for the fact that their reality exists inside the conversation.

This is why the chapter belongs after labeling and no. Chapter 3 established that recognition lowers defensiveness. Chapter 4 established that autonomy lowers it too. Chapter 5 combines both into a sharper standard: before you try to persuade, can the other side hear themselves in your summary? A real "that's right" moment means they can. The summary is not a performance of listening. It is proof that you understand the shape of their situation closely enough to describe it from their side.

A good summary therefore does more than repeat words. It gathers pressures, priorities, and emotional logic into a clear picture. It shows the other side that you understand not only what they said, but why their position makes sense from where they stand. When that lands, the conversation can move. The other side becomes more open because they feel represented rather than managed. In that sense, accurate summary creates permission to persuade only after alignment is real.

The chapter keeps one hard warning in place. "That's right" is not magic by itself. Someone can approve a summary politely even when it is shallow or steering too obviously toward your agenda. Forced alignment fails for the same reason fake empathy fails. If the other side hears your script instead of their own perspective, the phrase means almost nothing.

That is the chapter's main test. When does "that's right" signal real alignment, and when is it only verbal approval of a recap that still missed the live concern? The answer depends on whether the summary captured the person's actual map or merely organized the conversation in a way that served you. Chapter 5 raises the bar on listening again: do not settle for agreement that flatters you. Work until the other side recognizes themselves in what you say back.
