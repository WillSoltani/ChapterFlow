Taleb's scandal is not only that forecasts fail. It is that forecasts are delivered with much more confidence than their signal deserves. The offense is overprecision. Uncertainty gets translated into authority theater.

This persists because confidence is socially rewarded. A hesitant forecast sounds weak. A crisp forecast sounds professional. Institutions therefore keep selecting for people who can compress ambiguity into decisive language. Fluency outranks calibration.

That is why the chapter is systemic, not merely personal. Forecasts travel through media, boardrooms, and management culture because they create the impression that reality is being domesticated. The person who says, I do not know, rarely gets the same prestige as the person who gives a number and a date.

Taleb is not claiming that all foresight is useless. He is claiming that prediction culture routinely exaggerates what is knowable. Precision often rides on prestige, tidy models, and the audience's appetite for certainty more than on real signal.

Chapter 11 follows naturally. Once prediction is stripped of false grandeur, the stronger question becomes how to search and act without pretending the map is already clear.
