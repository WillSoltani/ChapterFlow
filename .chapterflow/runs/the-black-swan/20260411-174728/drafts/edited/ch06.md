Taleb's narrative fallacy begins with a simple appetite: people prefer a story to a pile of fragments. That preference becomes dangerous when the story starts claiming more explanation than the evidence earned. A narrative selects, arranges, and assigns causes while quietly dropping the noise around the event.

What disappears in that compression is often the uncertainty that mattered most. Randomness gets pushed into the background. Omitted variables stop existing once the story sounds complete. The result feels cleaner than reality was, and that cleanliness is exactly why it persuades.

This is why polished explanations spread so easily after shocks, careers, and market moves. Once the outcome is visible, a writer can guide the reader through motives and turning points that make the result feel almost destined. The story gives order, but it also hides how much opacity and chance were present before the result arrived.

Taleb is not banning stories. He is warning that stories can smuggle certainty past scrutiny. A narrative can feel like understanding while mainly reflecting omission, editing, and hindsight.

The cost is larger than memory style. If people keep absorbing neat after-stories, they become easier to fool about prediction, causality, and control. When a story feels perfectly shaped, the chapter wants one question kept alive: what had to be cut away to make it look that clean?
