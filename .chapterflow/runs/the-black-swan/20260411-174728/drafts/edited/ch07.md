Taleb's phrase is precise. The antechamber of hope is not success itself. It is the waiting room just outside imagined arrival. The chapter examines what happens when people live psychologically inside that room, sustained by the vivid possibility of a rare payoff that almost never arrives.

Hope is not the enemy by itself. The distortion begins when a visible jackpot or breakout becomes so emotionally vivid that it bends judgment about probability. The mind keeps rehearsing the upside while the full field of failures fades into abstraction.

That is why the chapter matters. Improbable reward often feels personal before it feels statistical. Someone imagines being the founder who breaks through, the performer who gets discovered, or the trader who catches the move. Once that branch becomes vivid, denominator reality weakens.

Taleb's correction is to separate emotional availability from actual likelihood. A possibility can be real and still be badly overinhabited by the imagination. That is what makes the antechamber dangerous. It can feel like movement while mostly functioning as attachment to a fantasy branch.

Chapter 8 follows naturally. Once the visible winner dominates attention, the invisible losers around that winner begin disappearing from judgment altogether.
