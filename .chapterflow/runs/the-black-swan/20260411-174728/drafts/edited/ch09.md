Taleb's target here is not games themselves but misplaced transfer. A game has bounded rules, visible moves, known constraints, and a relatively stable environment. Real life often does not. Yet people keep borrowing the clarity of games and importing it into domains where hidden variables, shifting incentives, and unknown tails matter more.

That is the ludic fallacy. Reality gets treated as if it were a chessboard, casino table, or classroom puzzle. Uncertainty starts looking tamer because the frame carries an atmosphere of containment. The rules feel knowable. The possibilities feel countable.

Taleb is not banning models. He is warning that clean models become dangerous when they are mistaken for faithful copies of messy domains. A structure that behaves well inside a bounded toy system may fail badly outside it because the world never promised the same limits.

This is why the chapter matters for expertise. Analysts and managers often feel safer once a problem is translated into a formal frame. But the frame may be shrinking the problem into something more elegant than reality. Precision can distort as easily as it can help.

The bridge to Chapter 10 is direct. Once game-shaped understanding is mistaken for world-shaped understanding, prediction becomes much easier to oversell.
