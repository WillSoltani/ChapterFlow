Taleb's title is mocking a habit, not naming a method. People do not simply receive evidence and judge it neutrally. They go looking for support that lets a prior belief stay comfortable. Confirmation feels like rigor because the mind experiences agreement as proof.

The chapter asks a harder question: what happened to the evidence that would have injured the belief? A person can gather supportive facts for a long time and still know very little if the search itself was biased. The issue is not empty evidence. It is filtered evidence.

That is why the mistake scales beyond private opinion. Analysts favor reports that support the trade they already want. Managers notice the dashboard that flatters the plan they already approved. Students collect sources that defend the thesis they already prefer. In each case, support feels scientific while disconfirmation gets ignored or delayed.

Taleb's correction is severe. Stop scoring a claim by how much friendly support it has accumulated. Ask what would count against it and whether that pressure was genuinely invited. Otherwise conviction grows faster than calibration.

The bridge to the next chapter is clear. Once evidence is selectively curated, it becomes easy to package the curated sample into a tidy story that feels much cleaner than reality was.
