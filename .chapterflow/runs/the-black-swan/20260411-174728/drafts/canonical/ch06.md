Human beings do not like loose fragments. They prefer a story. That preference looks harmless until the story begins pretending it has explained more than it really has. Taleb calls this the narrative fallacy: the mind's habit of compressing uncertainty into a sequence that feels cleaner, more causal, and more necessary than reality was.

A story performs several tricks at once. It selects a few details, arranges them into order, assigns causes, and silently throws away the noise around them. What disappears in the process is often the very uncertainty that mattered. Randomness becomes background clutter instead of a live part of the outcome. Omitted variables stop existing the moment the story starts sounding complete.

That is why narratives are so persuasive after shocks, careers, and market moves. Once the event has happened, a reader can be led through a chain of motives and turning points that make the result feel almost destined. The story does emotional work. It removes mess. It gives sequence to events that may have involved more opacity and chance than the story will admit.

Taleb is not arguing that stories are useless. He is arguing that they are dangerous when they smuggle certainty past scrutiny. A polished explanation can feel like understanding even when it mainly reflects omission, editing, and hindsight.

The chapter matters because stories do not merely summarize the past. They train the mind for the future. If a person keeps absorbing neat after-stories, that person becomes more willing to believe that causes are clearer and prediction is stronger than the world deserves. Narrative satisfaction quietly weakens calibration.

So the correction is not to ban explanation. It is to distrust explanations that arrive too smooth, too total, and too eager to erase the random or the unknown. When a story feels perfectly shaped, Taleb wants the reader to ask what had to be cut away to make it look that way.
