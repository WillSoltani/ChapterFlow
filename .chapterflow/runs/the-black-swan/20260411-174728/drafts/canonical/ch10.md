Taleb's scandal is not just that people predict badly. It is that they predict badly while speaking as if the world had granted them a level of visibility it has not granted. The offense is inflated confidence. Error matters, but overprecision matters more because it turns uncertainty into authority theater.

This happens because prediction is socially rewarded when it sounds crisp. A hesitant forecast feels weak. A confident forecast feels professional. Institutions, media, and organizations therefore keep selecting for people who can compress ambiguity into sentences that sound decisive. Calibration loses status to fluency.

The chapter matters because this is not only an individual vice. It is a system of incentives. Forecasts travel upward through memos, interviews, boardrooms, and television because they create the impression that reality is being domesticated. The person who says "I do not know" rarely gets the same stage time as the person who gives a number, a date, and a confident tone.

Taleb is not arguing that all foresight is worthless. He is arguing that the social packaging of prediction routinely exaggerates what is knowable. Experts borrow prestige from formality, from past calm, from tidy models, and from the audience's hunger for certainty. The forecast may therefore be respected for reasons that have little to do with genuine signal.

The correction is not permanent silence. It is harsher discipline about what counts as knowledge, what deserves precision, and when ignorance is the stronger answer. Prediction should shrink when the domain is opaque, tail-driven, or model-fragile. Instead it often expands. That is the scandal.

The next chapter follows naturally because once prediction has been exposed as overconfident theater, the better question becomes: how do you search, notice, and act without pretending you already know the map?
