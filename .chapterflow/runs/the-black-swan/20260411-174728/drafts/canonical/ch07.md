Taleb's phrase is architectural on purpose. An antechamber is not the throne room. It is the waiting space just outside imagined arrival. The chapter is about what happens when people live psychologically inside that waiting space, sustained by the vivid possibility of an extraordinary payoff that almost never lands.

Hope itself is not the target. Taleb is after a more precise distortion. A visible jackpot, celebrated winner, or legendary breakout becomes so emotionally available that it starts bending judgment about probability. The mind keeps rehearsing the upside while failing to keep the full field of failures equally present.

That imbalance matters because improbable reward often looks personal before it looks statistical. A person imagines being the singer discovered, the trader who catches the move, the founder whose company detonates upward. Once that image becomes vivid, denominator reality fades. The crowd of people who waited in the same room and got nothing becomes abstract.

The chapter therefore sits close to Chapter 3 but shifts emphasis. There the issue was payoff shape. Here the issue is psychological seduction. People do not just misread asymmetry. They also emotionally overinhabit the lucky branch of the tree. Hope fills the room more loudly than probability.

Taleb is not asking the reader to abolish ambition. He is asking the reader to see how visible possibility can create an inflated sense of personal nearness to a rare event. That is what makes the antechamber dangerous. It can feel like progress even when it is mostly exposure to fantasy.

The bridge to Chapter 8 is obvious once the chapter is read this way. If the visible winner occupies the imagination, then the invisible losers around that winner must be doing hidden explanatory work. Silent evidence is already standing just outside the door.
