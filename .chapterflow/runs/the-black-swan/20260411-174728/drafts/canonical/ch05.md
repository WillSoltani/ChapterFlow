People do not merely misread evidence after it arrives. They often go looking for the kind of evidence that will let them keep a prior belief intact. That is the irritation inside Taleb's title. Confirmation is not treated as a noble scientific step. It is treated as a comfortable human habit.

A belief feels stronger when new facts seem to support it, but the chapter presses a sharper question: what happened to the facts that would have injured the belief? A person can collect supportive fragments for a long time and still know very little if the search itself was biased. The problem is not that evidence was absent. The problem is that the evidence stream was filtered in advance.

This matters because confirmation bias does not stay private. Analysts search for reports that fit the trade they already want. Managers notice the dashboard that flatters the plan they already approved. Students read the sources that defend the thesis they already drafted. In each case, the mind experiences support as rigor even though it may be avoiding the only evidence that could really test the claim.

Taleb's point is harsher than a plea for fairness. Selective confirmation creates overconfidence. A belief that keeps surviving only because hostile evidence was screened out begins to feel proven. That sensation is dangerous because it resembles knowledge while being built on self-protection.

The chapter therefore changes the burden of proof. Instead of asking how much support a claim can gather, ask what would count against it and whether that evidence was given a serious chance to appear. Without that pressure, conviction grows faster than calibration.

That move also prepares the narrative turn that follows. Once a mind is already selecting the evidence it prefers, it becomes much easier to wrap the selected pieces into a story that feels smooth, complete, and deserved.
