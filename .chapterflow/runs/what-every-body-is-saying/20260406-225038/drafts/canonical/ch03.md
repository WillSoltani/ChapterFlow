Evan's body reacted before his explanation did. When the budget-cut announcement landed, one hand closed on the table, his shoulders rose, and his torso leaned back before his answer became clean enough to sound composed. That order matters because it helps explain one of the book's central claims: the body often gets there first.

A useful way to frame that speed is to remember that people carry older protective responses built for safety, discomfort, surprise, and relief. When pressure enters a room, the body can freeze, narrow, distance itself, shield vulnerable areas, or start soothing itself before speech finishes organizing the experience. You do not need exact neuroscience to see the pattern. You only need to notice how often protection moves faster than explanation.

That is why comfort and discomfort remain such strong categories. A student called on without warning may go still before speaking. A parent hearing hard news may fold inward before answering. A glass breaking at dinner can make someone step back or lift the shoulders before they know what they think about the sound. The body's first job is often to manage the hit, not to narrate it.

Seen that way, many nonverbal cues become easier to understand. Rubbing the hands, tightening the torso, pausing in place, leaning away, or touching the neck can all be part of a fast effort to steady the system. Relief can be visible too. Once the pressure drops, the body may loosen, the shoulders may fall, and breathing may return before anyone says, I am fine now.

The chapter gets dangerous only when a reader treats that speed like certainty. Fast protective response is not the same thing as confession. A freeze can mean fear, overload, embarrassment, surprise, or social exposure. A shielding motion can mean discomfort without telling you exactly what kind. The gain is timing and category, not total explanation.

That smaller gain is still powerful. If you understand that the body often protects first, you stop treating sudden reaction as random and you stop demanding instant clarity from a person who is still regulating. You slow the pace, lower pressure, and ask better questions. The body is not betraying the person. It is often trying to help them endure the moment.

Once that timing makes sense, the next question becomes physical rather than philosophical: where in the body do those fast movement plans show up most clearly? The next chapter moves downward to feet and legs, where direction and withdrawal often become visible early.
