Rina kept saying the presentation was under control, but her hands had already tightened around the marker. Her fingers interlaced, then separated, then rubbed together before her words admitted any pressure. That is why hands and fingers matter so much in body-language reading.

Hands are highly expressive, and they also help people regulate stress. When someone feels easier, the hands may rest openly, gesture more freely, or show the palms without much caution. When pressure rises, the hands may hide, grip, rub, interlace, pick, or touch the body in ways that steady the system. Hands often show what the body is doing with tension.

That does not mean one hand cue solves the moment. Open palms are not proof of honesty. Hidden hands are not proof of deceit. What matters is how the hands behave with timing and context. Finger interlacing can show tension or control. Rubbing can soothe. Tightly clasped hands can contain pressure. Open, fluid hand use can show ease or willingness to engage.

The school setting makes this easier to see. During a student council pitch, a speaker who feels unsure may keep rubbing the fingers against the note cards, then let the hands open once the first good response comes back. In a seminar, another student may hide the hands under the desk until the discussion turns toward a topic they know well. The hands can reveal confidence or strain before the verbal tone fully changes.

Personal settings show the same pattern. On a couch, someone may keep the voice steady while the fingers keep picking at a sleeve during a difficult conversation. In a kitchen, another person may open both hands and relax the grip on a mug once a misunderstanding clears. Hand behavior often helps the observer see whether the moment is becoming safer, tighter, or more controlled.

The gain is practical. If the hands tighten or start soothing early, the observer can lower pressure, simplify the question, or change pace and see whether the tension in the hands eases. If the hands open after reassurance, that transition teaches more than the first tense grip did. Hand reading works best when it tracks regulation over time, not when it worships one gesture.

Still, context rules the chapter. Tables, props, habit, temperature, culture, and social style can all shape what the hands are doing. Hidden hands may be blocked by furniture, not secrecy. Open hands may be personal style, not deep sincerity. The chapter's lesson is disciplined observation: watch the hands and fingers for expression, soothing, concealment, and tension, then confirm the read with timing, arms, torso, and the rest of the body. From there the book can move to the face, where the signals are tempting but the risk of overreading grows even higher.
