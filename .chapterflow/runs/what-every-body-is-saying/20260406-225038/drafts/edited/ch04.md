Nina's smile stayed in place through the meeting, but her feet had already turned toward the door. She kept answering politely, yet the lower half of her body was voting for departure before her words admitted any strain. That is why feet and legs matter so much in body-language reading.

They are built for movement. Feet and legs help us approach, stop, brace, turn, freeze, and leave. Because they are tied so closely to direction and readiness, they often reveal where the person wants to go, whether they are settling in, and how much pressure is entering the moment. People usually manage the face first. The lower body often gets less theatrical supervision.

That does not mean every foot angle is a confession. It means feet and legs can provide early evidence of movement intention. A planted stance can show commitment or resistance. Rapid bouncing can show impatience, stress, or extra energy. Ankles locking back under a chair can signal restraint. Legs pulling away can reveal discomfort before the person says they want distance.

The school setting makes this easy to see. On a campus tour, one student keeps drifting a foot toward the art room while pretending to stay neutral. In a principal's office doorway, another student's legs freeze before any explanation arrives. The body can point, stall, or prepare to leave before the sentence catches up.

Personal settings show the same pattern. At dinner, someone may keep the torso angled politely while the feet stay aimed toward the exit. On a couch, a leg may start bouncing when a topic turns tense, then settle once the topic changes. Lower-body behavior often teaches the observer when pressure rose and when it eased.

The gain is practical, not magical. If feet and legs show direction or withdrawal early, the observer can change pacing, shorten a demand, or test whether the lower body settles when the pressure changes. That is better than staring at the face and pretending one smile solves the room.

Still, context rules the chapter. Injury, furniture, habit, footwear, room layout, and temperature all matter. A foot aimed at the door may reflect the easiest path out, not secret dislike. A bouncing leg may reflect caffeine, not contempt. The chapter's lesson is disciplined observation: use feet and legs as early directional clues, then confirm the read with timing, context, and the rest of the body. From there the book can move upward to the torso, where openness and protection become even more visible.
