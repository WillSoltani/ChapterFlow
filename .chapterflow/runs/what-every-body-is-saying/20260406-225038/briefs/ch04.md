Core Claim:
Feet and legs often reveal movement intention and comfort level earlier than the face because they are tied to going, staying, freezing, and leaving. The chapter should teach direction and readiness, not pretend that one foot angle solves motive by itself.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
Chapter 3 explained why the body reacts early. Chapter 4 identifies a practical place to watch first. Its job is anatomical and tactical: feet and legs often show approach, withdrawal, impatience, settling, and restraint before a person manages the rest of the body.

Author Logic Chain:
First show a mismatch between polite upper-body behavior and honest lower-body direction. Then explain why feet and legs matter: they are built for movement and often receive less conscious management. Then map common patterns such as pointing, freezing, bouncing, leg withdrawal, and blocking. Finish with contextual limits and a bridge into the torso.

Required Anchors:
- A work scene where feet angle toward the exit or settle back in during a tense conversation.
- A school scene where feet or legs reveal readiness, reluctance, or directional preference before speech does.
- A personal scene where under-table feet or legs show interest, impatience, or withdrawal that the face tries to hide.

Common Misreadings:
- treating pointed feet as a perfect confession of desire or dislike
- ignoring injury, habit, temperature, footwear, furniture, or room geometry
- reading one leg cue without timing, context, or supporting behavior

Counterarguments or Limits:
Feet and legs are useful because they are tied to movement, but they are also shaped by habit, space, fatigue, pain, social rules, and furniture. The chapter must keep the gain practical: better early directional reading, not certainty.
