Core Claim:
The torso is a strong comfort-and-protection surface because people reveal openness, withdrawal, shielding, and territorial confidence through the chest, shoulders, hips, and lean. The chapter should teach front-of-body access and protection, not act as if one posture explains motive by itself.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
Chapter 4 tracked direction through feet and legs. Chapter 5 moves upward to show whether the person is opening, protecting, bracing, or claiming space through the torso. Its job is to teach access to the front of the body and torso-related territorial tension.

Author Logic Chain:
First show a torso mismatch between polite words and protected posture. Then explain why the front of the body matters: people open it when comfortable and protect it when strained. Then map common patterns such as leaning, torso turn, shoulder rise, chest expansion, narrowing, and covering. Finish with contextual limits and a bridge into the arms chapter.

Required Anchors:
- A work scene where torso lean, chest posture, or shoulder rise reveals comfort or strain before verbal clarity.
- A school scene where torso openness or protective turning reveals readiness, discomfort, or territory.
- A personal scene where chest, hips, or shoulders show distance, shielding, or warmth before speech does.

Common Misreadings:
- treating crossed or covered torso behavior as one fixed motive
- ignoring cold, chair design, habit, pain, formal posture norms, or social setting
- reading one torso cue without timing, transition, or lower-body support

Counterarguments or Limits:
Torso behavior is powerful but still shaped by furniture, habit, temperature, pain, culture, and role. The chapter must keep the gain practical: better access/protection reading, not certainty about what someone privately thinks.
