Core Claim:
Arms often show how a person is managing distance, access, and self-protection. The chapter should keep the gain practical and narrow: folded arms, tucked arms, reaching, and arm barriers can reveal comfort, discomfort, invitation, or restraint, but no single arm cue solves motive.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
Chapter 5 tracked access through the torso. Chapter 6 moves outward to the arms, which often build the barriers, invitations, and holding patterns that manage distance in a visible way. Its job is to teach arm-based distance management without padding the chapter into a giant posture taxonomy.

Author Logic Chain:
First show an arm barrier appearing before words admit strain. Then explain arms as tools for blocking, reaching, containing, or inviting. Then map common patterns such as folded arms, tucked elbows, self-holding, open reach, and arm placement across desks or spaces. Finish with contextual limits and a bridge into hands and fingers.

Required Anchors:
- A work scene where folded arms, tucked elbows, or arm reach reveals comfort or strain before verbal clarity.
- A school scene where arms show barrier, invitation, or restraint in a tutoring or sideline setting.
- A personal scene where arms reveal distance, self-comfort, or welcome before speech does.

Common Misreadings:
- treating folded arms as automatic hostility or refusal
- ignoring cold, habit, chairs, desk layout, or self-soothing comfort
- reading one arm cue without timing, transition, or torso support

Counterarguments or Limits:
Arms are useful because they are visible distance-management tools, but they are also shaped by habit, temperature, furniture, comfort, and social role. The chapter must keep the gain narrow: better barrier/invitation reading, not certainty.
