Core Claim:
The first chapter argues that greatness is often blocked not by obvious failure but by comfortable competence. Collins sets up the book by showing that many organizations never become great because being good removes the pressure to change. The chapter earns that claim by describing a disciplined research process that identified a small group of companies that made a sustained leap. Its main job is to convince the reader that the later concepts come from comparative evidence rather than motivational opinion.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
It is the only chapter built around the study frame itself: the leap, the comparison set, the long time horizon, and the idea that greatness emerged from a sequence rather than one dramatic event.

Author Logic Chain:
First the author frames "good" as the hidden obstacle to greatness.
Then the author describes the disciplined study that searched for companies that made a durable leap.
Then the author concludes that greatness leaves a pattern that can be studied, which opens the door to the concepts that follow.

Required Anchors:
- the idea that "good" can become the ceiling that blocks greatness
- the study design: a large field narrowed to 11 companies and matched comparison companies
- the sustained-outperformance standard rather than a short burst of success

Frameworks or Terms Introduced:

Specific Applications:
- distinguish a real performance leap from a temporary hot streak
- diagnose when comfort with "good enough" is suppressing harder strategic questions
- frame later concepts as part of a studied sequence instead of disconnected tips

Common Misreadings:
- treating the chapter as a generic ambition speech
- assuming the book claims one heroic move creates greatness

Counterarguments or Limits:
The chapter's evidence frame is company-performance focused, so later applications need judgment when readers map it onto teams, schools, or personal projects. The argument also risks sounding abstract unless the research and momentum logic stay concrete.
