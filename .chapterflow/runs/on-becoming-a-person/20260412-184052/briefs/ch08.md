Core Claim:
This chapter should frame personal goals not as conformity to an external ideal, but as movement toward more real and congruent selfhood. It should preserve the paradox that people move toward change through greater acceptance and more truthful contact with what they are, rather than through harder self-manufacture. The chapter should feel exacting, not sentimental.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
This is the personal-goals chapter. It should feel impossible to confuse with Chapter 7 because the focus shifts from what psychotherapy is to what sort of personal becoming it serves.

Author Logic Chain:
First Rogers asks what goals psychotherapy can rightly serve.
Then he frames those goals in terms of becoming more real rather than more compliant.
Then he clarifies that acceptance and truthful contact are not passivity, but the condition of less false change.

Specific Applications:

Common Misreadings:
- treating self-acceptance as passive resignation
- treating real selfhood as impulse or self-indulgence
- collapsing personal goals into private preference with no discipline

Counterarguments or Limits:
The chapter should not promise that being oneself means following every impulse or ignoring moral demands. Its point is narrower: personal goals become truer when they move toward congruence and less falseness rather than toward external compliance or image maintenance. Keep the acceptance-change tension visible.

Previous Chapter Bridge: Chapter 7 conceived psychotherapy as process; this chapter asks what personal goals that process should serve.
Next Chapter Bridge: once personal goals are framed this way, Rogers can describe the good life and the fully functioning person more explicitly.
