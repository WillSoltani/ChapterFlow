Core Claim:
Rogers now asks what can actually be known about psychotherapy from two different angles: what can be observed or measured, and what is lived from within by therapist and client. The chapter's core move is to resist flattening psychotherapy into only one of those registers. Objective findings matter, but so does the inward, subjective movement that cannot be fully replaced by external description. The chapter should make the two modes feel distinct but not hostile. It should end by showing that any serious account of psychotherapy must hold both evidence and lived process together.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
This is the knowledge-modes chapter. It should feel impossible to confuse with Chapter 3 because it shifts from relationship description to the epistemic question of what psychotherapy knowledge looks like.

Author Logic Chain:
First Rogers asks what psychotherapy can be known objectively.
Then he insists that subjective experience is not noise but part of the phenomenon itself.
Then he argues that a serious understanding of psychotherapy requires both angles.

Common Misreadings:
- treating subjectivity as anti-scientific

- making the chapter into a generic science-versus-feelings argument

Counterarguments or Limits:
The chapter should not turn into a dismissal of evidence. Its point is narrower: psychotherapy includes lived inner process that objective description alone cannot exhaust. Keep visible that subjective testimony can be distorted too, so both modes need discipline.

Previous Chapter Bridge: Chapter 3 described the helping relationship; this chapter asks what can be known about psychotherapy from outside and from within that relationship.
Next Chapter Bridge: once these two knowledge modes are in view, the next step is to follow the actual directions evident in therapy.
