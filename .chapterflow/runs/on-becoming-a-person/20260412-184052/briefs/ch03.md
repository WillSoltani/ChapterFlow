Core Claim:
After naming the conditions that facilitate growth, Rogers turns to the practical characteristics of a relationship that can genuinely help. The core move is to show that help depends not on expert pressure or superior moral standing, but on a relationship in which the other person feels safely received, accurately understood, and less forced into defense. The chapter should feel like a refinement of Chapter 2, not a repetition. Its pressure lies in showing that what sounds simple in theory is demanding in lived contact. It should end with the sense that a helping relationship is recognizable by what it makes possible in the other person.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
This is the relationship-shape chapter. It should feel impossible to confuse with Chapter 2 because it moves from named conditions to the characteristics of the helping relationship itself.

Author Logic Chain:
First Rogers asks what marks a relationship as genuinely helpful.
Then he describes the qualities of contact that make trust, openness, and movement more possible.
Then he clarifies how those characteristics differ from control, judgment, and subtle domination.

Common Misreadings:
- treating a helping relationship as mere warmth
- assuming a relationship is helpful because it feels emotionally comfortable
- flattening it into generic listening advice

Counterarguments or Limits:
The chapter should not imply that one relational moment fixes everything. The claim is narrower: some relationship qualities make growth more possible because they change what can be faced and said. Keep visible that a pleasant but controlling relationship can still fail the test.

Previous Chapter Bridge: Chapter 2 named the facilitative conditions; this chapter asks how a helping relationship actually takes shape when those conditions are real.
