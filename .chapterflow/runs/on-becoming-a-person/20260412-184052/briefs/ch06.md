Core Claim:
After tracing the directions evident in therapy, Rogers asks what that movement amounts to in human terms. The core move is to define becoming a person not as achieving a fixed ideal identity, but as shedding defensive falseness and moving toward more open, owned, lived selfhood. The chapter should feel like an interpretation of movement, not a slogan about self-expression. It should end with the sense that becoming is a living process rather than a finished state one can finally possess.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
This is the becoming chapter. It should feel impossible to confuse with Chapter 5 because it asks what therapeutic movement means existentially, not just what direction it takes.

Author Logic Chain:
First Rogers asks what therapeutic movement means for the person's way of being.
Then he describes becoming as movement away from defensive falseness toward more open contact with lived experience.
Then he clarifies that becoming a person is process, not a final perfected identity.

Common Misreadings:
- treating becoming as self-indulgent self-expression
- treating personhood as a finished ideal one can arrive at once and keep
- flattening openness into saying whatever you feel without discipline

Counterarguments or Limits:
The chapter should not promise a final authentic state that stays permanently secure. Its point is narrower: becoming a person is an ongoing process of living less defensively and more openly from one's experience. Keep visible that openness still requires discipline and does not eliminate conflict or uncertainty.

Previous Chapter Bridge: Chapter 5 traced the directions of movement in therapy; this chapter asks what that movement means as a way of becoming more fully a person.
Next Chapter Bridge: once becoming is framed as process, the next step is to conceive psychotherapy itself more explicitly as process.

Medium. The chapter values more open living, but it should not romanticize instability or confuse process with looseness.
