If the person in therapy is a living process, then psychotherapy cannot be understood as a fixed technique applied to a static object. That is the pressure Rogers now faces. After describing becoming as process, he turns back to therapy itself and asks what sort of activity it must be. His answer is not that method disappears, but that therapy is truer when conceived as an unfolding process rather than as an external procedure used on someone from the outside.

A fixed-technique picture is too crude because it tempts therapist and client to treat movement as something mechanically produced. It favors sequence, control, and visible management. Yet the living material of therapy is not a machine part. It is the person's experiencing, awareness, and relation to self as these begin to shift with less distortion. A process conception keeps the therapist closer to that reality.

In this view, psychotherapy happens through unfolding experiencing. More of what is actually lived becomes available to awareness and less trapped behind defensive arrangement. The movement cannot be reduced to steps on a chart because it is not only a matter of procedure. It is a changing relation to experience, to self, and to what can be felt or admitted.

That does not make therapy vague. Rogers is not replacing method with drift. A process can be disciplined without being mechanical. It can have form, attention, and standards without pretending that one intervention sequence captures every living person. The chapter asks for a harder rigor: attention to what is unfolding rather than loyalty to a script for its own sake.

This matters because formulas can distort what they mean to help. Once therapist and client start performing a method, the process can freeze into something managed from outside. Neat procedure may survive, but the living movement can thin out. Rogers wants psychotherapy understood in a way that protects the person's reality from being overruled by the tidiness of technique.

So the chapter settles in a narrow but important middle. Psychotherapy is not shapeless, and it is not mechanical. It is a disciplined process in which experience, awareness, and self-relation can unfold with less distortion. That conception matters because help becomes more truthful when it meets a person as process rather than as an object to be handled by formula. Once that is clear, the next question follows: what personal goals should such a process actually serve?
