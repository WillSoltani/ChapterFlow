Most people want growth to end in a finished self. Rogers resists that wish. After tracing the directions visible in therapy, he now asks what those directions amount to in human terms. His answer is not that a person uncovers a polished identity and then keeps it secure. The movement means becoming more of a person by living less from defense and more from directly encountered experience.

Becoming, then, is not possession. It is process. A person does not arrive at a final authentic version and retire from struggle. Rogers is describing a life that grows more open, more owned, and less organized around falseness. The self becomes less like an image to maintain and more like a way of meeting experience as it actually happens.

Part of that process is movement away from defensive falseness. A person can spend years speaking from role, explanation, and practiced surface. Therapy loosens that arrangement. What had to stay hidden, edited, or translated into safer language can be approached more honestly. The point is not confession for its own sake. The point is that more of lived experience can be admitted without the same automatic need for protection.

Another part is greater openness to experience. Rogers does not mean that every feeling should be blurted out or obeyed. He means that inward life is met more directly and with less distortion. Experience no longer has to pass entirely through image management or borrowed definitions. It is felt, tested, and owned with more immediacy.

That is why becoming a person cannot mean constructing a cleaner identity project. The moment personhood becomes a polished ideal to secure, the process hardens again. The person starts performing the very selfhood that should remain alive. Rogers wants a different honesty: not looseness, not impulse, but disciplined openness that stays answerable to what is actually being lived.

Conflict and uncertainty therefore do not disappear. In some ways they become easier to notice because less energy goes into pretending they are absent. Becoming a person does not promise calm completion. It means increasing ability to live in closer contact with reality without fleeing so quickly into defensive simplification.

So the chapter ends with a demanding claim. To become a person is not to possess a final self. It is to keep becoming through less falseness, more openness, and greater ownership of experience. Once that much is clear, the next question follows naturally: if personhood is this kind of movement, how should psychotherapy itself be understood as process?
