Core Claim:
This chapter argues that startup work should be run as a series of experiments testing business hypotheses. Ries focuses on value and growth hypotheses because they determine whether the product matters and whether the business can spread. Real-world tests produce stronger learning than market research alone because they force users and conditions to answer back. The chapter therefore turns learning into a sharper operating discipline.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
Chapter 3 defines valid learning. Chapter 4 shows how to produce it. Its distinctive mechanism is the shift from abstract learning language to hypothesis-driven experiments.

Author Logic Chain:
First the author says new ventures are experiments.
Then the author identifies value and growth hypotheses as core tests.
Then the author argues that live tests beat safer indirect research when uncertainty is high.

Required Anchors:
- startup as experiment, with the consequence that work becomes hypothesis-driven
- value and growth hypotheses, with the friction that teams often test easier questions instead
- real-world actionable feedback, with the consequence that safer research can mislead when it avoids exposure
