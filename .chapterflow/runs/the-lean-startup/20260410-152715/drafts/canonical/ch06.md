Teams often talk about minimum viable products as if the phrase simply meant "ship something cheap." Eric Ries uses Chapter 6 to reject that shallow reading. The point of an MVP is not to celebrate roughness. Its purpose is to complete one build-measure-learn cycle with as little waste as possible.

That definition changes what counts as minimal. Minimal does not mean the fewest pixels, the fewest features, or the lowest production quality in some abstract sense. It means the smallest product or test that can teach the team what it actually needs to know about an important assumption. If a stripped-down version fails to test the right belief, then it is not minimal in the way this chapter cares about. It is simply too small to do its job.

This is why Ries widens the category beyond ordinary software releases. A low-fidelity prototype can be a valid MVP if it reveals useful behavior. A concierge version can also qualify if the team manually delivers the experience and learns whether users truly want it. A video, a mockup, or a manual service can all count when they expose the assumption under pressure. The standard is not polish. The standard is learning.

That logic also explains the connection to waste. If the business needs an answer about user value or behavior, then building a feature-complete product before asking the question may be the expensive path, not the responsible one. Waste is not only bad code or excess design work. Waste is any effort spent before the team has learned whether the assumption deserves that effort at all.

But the chapter is not giving permission for lazy underbuilding. An MVP can become too thin. If the test is so small or artificial that it cannot produce meaningful evidence about the real assumption, then the team has saved effort at the cost of insight. Cheapness by itself is not the target. Useful learning with minimum waste is the target.

That makes Chapter 6 more demanding than startup slogans usually suggest. It asks teams to build just enough to learn, but not so little that the test stops being honest. The right MVP is therefore sized by the question, not by a generic rule about always doing less.

The pressure this creates is productive. A team has to ask what assumption it is truly trying to test, what user behavior would count as evidence, and what rough form could reveal that answer fastest. Once those questions are active, the next chapter can move toward measuring what these tests actually prove and how a startup should account for the learning it claims.
