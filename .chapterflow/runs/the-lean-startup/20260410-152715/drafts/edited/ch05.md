The moment a startup starts talking about all the things it needs to learn, it risks hiding the one thing that could actually break the business. A team can make a long list of unknowns, fill a wall with sticky notes, and still avoid the assumption whose failure would matter most. Eric Ries uses this chapter to stop that blur. Startup work begins with leap-of-faith assumptions, but not every leap deserves equal attention.

That is the chapter's first hard move. A leap-of-faith assumption is not just any uncertain belief. It is a belief the business already depends on before reality has approved it. The product might be assuming that users care enough to change behavior. The company might be assuming that, once users care, growth can follow through some real path. The business can sound confident long before any of that confidence has been earned.

Ries does not treat that situation as shameful. New ventures have to begin before everything is proven. The problem is not that assumptions exist. The problem is that teams often rank them badly. They test what is easiest to test, what is closest to the product they already want to build, or what is least likely to deliver a painful answer. That creates motion without much steering.

This is why the chapter keeps returning to the riskiest assumptions first. Risk here is not broad uncertainty. It is business consequence. Which belief, if false, would most damage the venture? Which belief is carrying the most weight while receiving the least proof? Once the question is framed that way, startup learning stops looking like broad curiosity and starts looking like triage.

Ries centers that triage on two leap categories: value and growth. The value hypothesis asks whether the product matters enough that users will care in a serious way. The growth hypothesis asks whether that value can spread through a path strong enough to support a business. Those are dangerous assumptions because a team can keep building for months while quietly presuming both are true.

The temptation is to work around those assumptions instead of through them. Teams collect reactions, refine designs, and run low-stakes checks that feel intelligent because they produce discussion. But an elegant discussion about safe uncertainty can still leave the business-critical leap untouched. In that situation, the team is not really steering. It is decorating confidence.

The deeper tension is emotional. The riskiest assumption is often tied to the story the founders most want to keep. If the answer turns negative, a roadmap may collapse, an identity may crack, or a favored explanation of the market may no longer survive. That is one reason teams postpone the most important test. They call it sequencing, but sometimes it is protection.

Chapter 5 does not simply tell startups to test assumptions. It tells them to rank assumptions by consequence and go first toward the ones that can do the most damage. Once that principle lands, the next question becomes operational: what is the smallest product or test that can expose the highest-risk leap without wasting months of work?
