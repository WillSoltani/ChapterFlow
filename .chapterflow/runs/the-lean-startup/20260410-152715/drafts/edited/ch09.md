Teams often feel proud of large releases because those releases look substantial. A big batch seems like serious work. But Eric Ries uses Chapter 9 to show how that feeling can be deceptive. The larger the batch, the longer the startup may have to wait before reality answers back, and the more expensive the correction becomes once problems finally appear.

That is why the chapter treats batch size as a learning problem instead of only an efficiency problem. Small batches shorten the distance between action and feedback. They let the team see sooner whether something works, whether something breaks, or whether an assumption was weaker than it seemed. The value is not smallness for its own sake. The value is faster contact with truth.

Large batches create the opposite condition. Work accumulates before the business gets a clear answer. Errors stay buried longer. Defects, misunderstandings, and weak assumptions are carried forward inside a larger release until they become harder to isolate and more painful to repair. Ries describes that accumulation as a kind of waste because the business is carrying unproven work for too long.

This is also why the chapter fits naturally with smaller releases and continuous deployment. Those practices matter here not because they are fashionable engineering moves, but because they reduce the waiting time between building something and learning what reality says about it. Shorter gaps make correction cheaper.

The trap is that large batches can feel efficient at first. They promise a bigger reveal, a cleaner launch, or the satisfaction of moving many things at once. But a startup can mistake that feeling for progress while feedback is arriving too late to help. In that case the team is optimizing for the emotional comfort of a big delivery rather than for the practical value of early correction.

Chapter 9 is about operational humility. Smaller batches admit that the startup does not benefit from waiting too long to discover error. They keep the system close to evidence and make it easier to change direction before the costs spread. The chapter is not celebrating fragmentation. It is defending faster learning.

That matters because the book is now moving toward acceleration. Once the startup can learn and correct faster, it can ask a new question: what kind of growth mechanism can actually sustain expansion? Chapter 10 takes up that question by turning from batch size to engines of growth.
