By Chapter 4, the hidden construction work is supposed to be done. The groups exist. The top line is earned. The structure can carry weight. But a reader still has to enter that structure somewhere. If the opening starts too far back, it drags. If it starts too abruptly, the answer can feel dropped from nowhere. An introduction is not ornamental warm-up. It is the controlled entry point into a finished pyramid.

The frozen heading map makes that practical aim clear. The section list asks about introductory flow, where to start the situation, what counts as a complication, why the order matters, how long the opening should be, and what to do about the key line. Those topics all point to one discipline: prepare the frame before asking the reader to absorb the answer. A strong opening activates the right question so the answer lands as a response instead of as an unexplained assertion.

That is where situation, complication, and question become useful. The situation tells the reader where things stand now. The complication introduces the pressure, change, tension, or obstacle that makes the situation unstable. The question is often implied even when it is not written formally. It is the problem now alive in the reader's mind. Once that question is active, the answer can arrive with force because it is answering something the reader already feels needs answering.

The order matters because readers process pressure before they process prescription. If a memo begins with the answer before the reader understands the condition that made the answer necessary, the top line can sound unsupported or premature. If the opening wanders through background without ever tightening into a complication, the answer arrives late and the reader has to guess what the real issue was. Sequence turns background into relevance instead of leaving it as drift.

That does not mean every introduction has to become long. The frozen subsection list asks directly how long it should be because length is a judgment, not a fixed formula. The opening should be only as long as needed to establish the frame cleanly. If the audience already shares the situation, the writer can move quickly. If the audience lacks the context needed to understand the complication, the opening has to do more work. Extra setup is not a virtue by itself.

Where the situation begins matters for the same reason. Start too early and the reader gets history that does not yet help. Start too late and the complication feels unearned. The opening should begin at the point where the reader can understand the current condition and then feel the pressure that turns that condition into a live problem. The writer is choosing the start of the frame, not recounting everything that happened beforehand.

The key line belongs after the frame has prepared the reader for it. In a recommendation, the answer should appear once the pressure is clear enough that the recommendation feels necessary rather than random. In a proposal, the opening has to make the need legible before it announces the response. In a progress review, the introduction has to clarify the changed condition before it delivers the update that matters. Different document types vary in tone, but they still rely on the same entry logic.

The common patterns in the frozen heading map show how flexible that logic can be. A request for funds needs an opening that clarifies the need, the pressure, and the question of response. A how-to document still benefits when the reader understands the condition that makes the process relevant now. A proposal letter has to establish the circumstance that makes the proposal necessary. A progress review has to show what changed before the summary line can matter.

Introductions also connect groups inside a document, not just whole documents. A transition between major sections often repeats the same work in smaller form. It reminds the reader where they are, signals what new pressure or distinction matters now, and then leads into the next answer or sub-answer. The same logic that governs the opening of a memo can govern the opening of a new group inside the memo.

The beginner error is to treat introductions as places to sound polished instead of places to create orientation. Some openings spread broad scene-setting that never tightens into a real complication. Others fire the key line too early, before the reader has any active question in mind. Both weaken the answer. The opening has failed because it did not prepare the reader's attention for what comes next.

The gain is practical rather than theatrical. A good introduction spares the reader from asking silent questions about why this topic matters, why this answer is arriving now, and what problem it is meant to solve. It lets the answer enter a frame already prepared to receive it. Once that opening discipline is clear, the next problem is inside the reasoning itself: whether the writer is moving deductively or inductively and why that difference matters.
