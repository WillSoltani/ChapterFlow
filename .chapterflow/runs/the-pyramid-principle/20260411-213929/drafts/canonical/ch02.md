A slide can look orderly and still be structurally false. Three bullets line up beneath a heading, each starting with a clean verb, each carrying roughly the same length, and the page gives off the reassuring feeling that the thinking has already been done. Then you read more closely and notice the bullets are not siblings at all. One names a cause. One names an action. One names a hoped-for result. The formatting says "group." The logic says "pile."

That is the problem Chapter 2 moves into. Once a writer accepts that ideas need a pyramid, the next question is what makes the inside of that pyramid hold. Barbara Minto's frozen rule set gives the answer in a stripped-down form. The point above must summarize the ideas below it. The ideas below must be the same kind of thing. And they must appear in a logical order. Those are not small style notes. They are the internal load-bearing rules.

The horizontal relationship is where most quiet failures begin. Sibling points under one higher claim need to belong to the same class. They should all be reasons, or all steps, or all categories, or all consequences. Once they start mixing types, the group stops thinking in one direction. The reader may still be able to follow each sentence individually, but the set no longer supports one clean summary. It becomes harder to tell what the points add up to because they are no longer doing the same job.

The vertical relationship tests something different. Even if the lower points belong together, the higher line above them has to state what they actually mean together. It cannot simply sound polished. It has to summarize the lower set honestly. A weak summary line often overreaches. It says more than the points beneath it can really support, or it uses broad language to hide that the lower group has not yet settled into one idea. The top line then becomes decorative confidence instead of earned compression.

Order matters for the same reason. The lower points should not only belong together. They should arrive in an order that helps the reader see the relationship among them. Sometimes that order is temporal. Sometimes it is structural. Sometimes it moves from problem to cause to remedy, or from general category to specific instance. The frozen support does not force one universal sequence, but it does make one thing clear: arbitrary order weakens meaning. A reader feels when a set has been stacked rather than arranged.

You can see the gain when a mixed list gets regrouped. A manager's slide that once blended causes, decisions, and results may suddenly become stable once the causes are grouped together, the decisions are grouped together elsewhere, and the higher line is rewritten to summarize only one of those sets at a time. A study outline works the same way. Students often think they need better wording when what they need is cleaner sibling logic. Once the points beneath a heading become parallel, the higher summary usually becomes easier to write because the structure has finally stopped lying.

That creates the chapter's real warning. A pyramid can fail from the inside while still looking clean from the outside. Neat bullets, tidy indentation, and a confident summary line can hide mixed categories, overlap, or a sequence with no governing reason. The writer may feel clear because the page looks calm. The reader, however, keeps feeling drag because the substructure is unstable. Clean formatting can disguise incoherence for a while. It cannot make incoherence disappear.

The useful discipline is to test any support group twice. First ask whether the sibling points are really the same kind of thing. Then ask whether the higher point truly summarizes what those siblings say together in the order they appear. If either answer is weak, the structure is not finished. That is why the next move has to be construction. Once you know what internal relationships a real pyramid needs, the question becomes how to build one deliberately instead of discovering halfway through the draft that the inside does not hold.
