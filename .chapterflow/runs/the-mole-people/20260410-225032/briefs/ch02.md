Core Claim:
This chapter explains why the tunnels can begin to look like shelter rather than madness when the available life above ground is shaped by assault risk, theft, humiliation, shelter instability, addiction pressure, and constant exposure. The point is not that the tunnels are good. The point is that under severe topside pressure, a place can qualify as home because it is less unbearable than the alternatives. Seville's chapter-local support gives that logic a human center. His line about the decade of crack, homelessness, and the tunnels turns underground settlement into a historical pressure pattern rather than an individual eccentricity.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
This is the first chapter that answers the introduction's open question. It shifts from hidden-world framing to the narrower logic of entry: what makes going below feel livable enough to choose.

Author Logic Chain:
First the chapter rejects the outsider assumption that entering the tunnels is irrational on its face.
Then it shows how topside life can become so violent, unstable, and humiliating that underground danger starts to look more governable.
Then it uses Seville's historical frame to connect personal shelter-seeking to the larger decade of crack, homelessness, and urban abandonment.
