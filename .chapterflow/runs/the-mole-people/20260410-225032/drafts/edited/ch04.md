Mac's chapter refuses two bad readings at once. It does not ask the reader to gawk at him, and it does not ask the reader to admire him cheaply.

The lawful support gives a narrow but firm portrait. Mac is older, methodical, hungry, and practiced. He hunts rats, calls them track rabbits, cooks them, and keeps moving through a life where scarcity is a climate. Those details show survival becoming organized.

That code is partly verbal. Calling rats track rabbits does not clean up what he must eat. It gives him a usable name for it. The rename creates distance from disgust and humiliation. The phrase works as survival language, not cute eccentricity.

Mac also does not appear as a rugged legend. He appears as someone forced into competence by conditions that keep pressing. The competence carries dignity because it means he is not passive before deprivation. But competence is not freedom. A person can become highly capable inside a bad world without the world becoming less bad.

The Thoreau detail sharpens that point. Mac recites Thoreau, and the value of that fact is contrast, not uplift. Rough subsistence and reflective language can live in the same person. Mac is not only hunger. He is also memory, quotation, and self-conception. But Thoreau does not rescue the rats. It only complicates the man.

That is why the title works. Mac's war is the siege logic of his life. The routines protect dignity because they let him answer pressure with action. They also threaten to harden into identity armor. The strongest reading keeps dignity, scarcity, language, and cost in one frame, then widens carefully toward the larger underground population.
