The hardest word in this chapter is home. If you hear it too softly, the whole argument gives way.

From the outside, going underground can look like the final proof that life has fallen apart. A tunnel sounds like the opposite of a home: dark, unstable, dangerous, and cut off from ordinary public life. If that is all you see, then anyone who moves below starts to look irrational or defeated. "Finding a Home" breaks that shallow reading. The real question is not whether the tunnels are good. It is what the surface has become when a place like that starts to look usable.

That change begins with pressure above ground. If the street means robbery while asleep, nights in the rain, humiliation, unstable shelter, or exposure to violence, then the decision map changes. Danger does not disappear below ground. It changes form. A tunnel can still injure, isolate, expose, or trap. But it may also offer a patch of governable space, a place where threats are more familiar, where some faces are known, and where the next hour is less chaotic than the one waiting topside. The chapter asks the reader to think comparatively, not theatrically.

That is where Seville matters. The lawful Chapter 2 support keeps him narrow but vivid: wounded, sharp, and darkly funny. He is not a mascot for underground life. He is a pressure witness. When he says, "It's the decade of crack and homelessness. It's the decade of the tunnels," he turns tunnel entry into a social pattern rather than a private eccentricity. The line does not praise the tunnels. It names the environment that made them thinkable.

That matters because the chapter is not only about one person. Crack, homelessness, and tunnel settlement are presented as linked pressures. Addiction fractures street life and family life. Housing instability removes the small buffers that keep danger from becoming constant. Public systems that should reduce harm can become one more source of uncertainty or humiliation. Under those conditions, home stops meaning a good place. It starts meaning the place where exposure is least unbearable. That is a damaged definition, but it is still one people may have to live inside.

The chapter works because it blocks two bad readings at once. One is moral superiority: no sane person would choose this. That assumes the alternatives were workable. The other is sentimental correction: the tunnels became a hidden refuge. That also fails, because danger stays alive below ground. The point is not safety. It is manageability. A place can be bad and still count as home if every other place is worse in the ways that matter tonight.

So the title is not comforting. It is accusatory. Here, home no longer means warmth or dignity. It means routine, known risks, and slightly reduced exposure. If a tunnel can meet that lowered standard, the first indictment falls on the city above that shrank the meaning of home so far.

By the end, the chapter changes the question. The issue is no longer why anyone would do something so extreme. It is why the ordinary world above became punishing enough that a dangerous underground space could feel more livable than the official alternatives. The next chapter has to stay with Seville long enough for that pressure logic to become a fuller human portrait.
