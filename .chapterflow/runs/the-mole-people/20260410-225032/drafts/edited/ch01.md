Most cities hide their failures in plain sight. New York hid some of its own underground.

Jennifer Toth's introduction begins from that disorienting fact: people were not only sleeping on sidewalks or moving through shelters and stations, they were living below the city in tunnels most of the public never had to imagine for more than a second. That fact lands like rumor at first. It sounds like the kind of story people tell to make urban life feel darker and stranger than it already is. The introduction's first job is to refuse that comfort. The tunnels are not fantasy space. They are part of the same city that sends people to work above them every morning.

The frame matters because hidden suffering gets misread in two opposite ways. Some people dismiss it because they cannot see it, so it stays at the level of gossip or exaggeration. Others rush the other direction and turn it into underground mythology, a secret kingdom below the streets with its own eerie glamour. The introduction resists both habits. Toth goes below as a reporter because distance is part of the problem. If the city hears about the people there only through rumor, then everything underground becomes easier to simplify.

What she finds is not a clean moral tableau. Later reviews and reporting describe the tunnel world as dangerous, dirty, improvised, and unstable. They also describe it as social. People live alone, but they also live in camps and communities. There are loners, addicts, predators, frightened children, makeshift leaders, outreach workers, graffiti artists, and people trying to keep one another alive long enough to reach another day. That complexity is the chapter's real opening argument. The tunnels do not reveal a separate species of person. They reveal ordinary human dependence under brutal conditions.

That is why the introduction should not be read as a tour of a strange place. It works more like an accusation. The underground world is not detached from the city above. It is produced by it. The same failures already visible topside become harder, colder, and more concentrated below ground. Poverty does not become poetic because it disappears from public view. Neglect does not become mysterious because it happens in darkness.

The hiddenness still matters because invisibility changes how the public behaves. Once people are out of sight, they can be treated as rumor, nuisance, pathology, or folklore. Even sympathy can become evasive. It is easy to feel a quick flash of horror about people living under the city and then move on, satisfied that shock itself counts as attention. The introduction pushes against that lazy reaction. Looking is not enough. The harder task is to understand that a hidden world like this grows from public arrangements that remain visible all the time.

The chapter also keeps one uncomfortable limit alive. Any account of a hidden world risks turning the people inside it into spectacle. Later reporting on the book makes that tension impossible to ignore. Some of Toth's broader claims and some of the wilder stories attached to tunnel life were challenged later or were hard to verify. That does not erase the underground world she documented, but it does sharpen the reader's responsibility. The point is not to become intoxicated by darkness. The point is to see how quickly hidden suffering attracts exaggeration, curiosity, and myth when direct public knowledge is weak.

That makes the introduction more disciplined than its premise might suggest. It does not ask the reader to admire the tunnels, fear them from a distance, or treat the people below as symbols of urban authenticity. It asks for something stricter: attention without fantasy. The danger is real. The community is real. The improvisation is real. So is the social failure that helped force people there in the first place. The chapter's opening success is that it keeps all of those truths in the same frame.

By the end, the question is no longer whether such a world exists. The question is what pressure could make it livable enough, or unavoidable enough, for someone to choose it over the options above ground. Once the hidden world is real, the reader has to ask what kind of city produces it and what kind of life drives a person into it.
