The hardest part of this chapter is the word home. If you hear it too softly, the whole argument collapses.

From the outside, going underground can look like the final proof that life has come apart. A tunnel sounds like the opposite of a home. It is dark, unstable, dangerous, and physically cut off from ordinary public life. If that is all you see, then anyone who moves below starts to look irrational, defeated, or somehow drawn to extremity for its own sake. "Finding a Home" exists to break that shallow reading. The real question is not whether the tunnels are good. The real question is what the surface has become when a place like that starts to look usable.

That change begins with pressure above ground. If the street means being robbed while asleep, soaked through the night, chased away, humiliated in public, or trapped in shelters that feel violent and unpredictable, then the decision map changes. Danger does not disappear below ground. It changes form. A tunnel can still maim, isolate, expose, or trap. But it may also offer a patch of governable space, a place where other people know the rules, where some threats are visible earlier, where theft is not disguised as official help, and where the next hour is less chaotic than the one waiting topside. The chapter asks the reader to think comparatively, not theatrically.

That is where Seville matters. The lawful Chapter 2 support keeps him narrow but vivid: a wounded, sharp, darkly funny tunnel dweller whose voice turns the chapter away from outsider shock and toward historical pressure. He does not function as a mascot for underground life. He functions as someone who knows what the decade felt like from inside it. His framing is blunt enough to do more than summarize his own condition. When he says, "It's the decade of crack and homelessness. It's the decade of the tunnels," he turns underground shelter into a social pattern. The line does not romanticize the tunnels. It names the environment that made them thinkable.

That matters because the chapter is not only about one person's decision. Crack, homelessness, and tunnel settlement are presented as linked pressures, not isolated curiosities. Addiction tears apart families and street networks. Housing insecurity strips away the small buffers that keep danger from becoming constant. Public systems that are already overloaded or careless become one more source of humiliation and instability. Under those conditions, home stops meaning a good place. It starts meaning the place where your exposure is least unbearable. That is a damaged definition, but it is still a definition people can live inside.

The chapter's force comes from refusing two easy mistakes at once. One mistake is moral superiority: no decent person would choose this. That reaction treats the available alternatives as if they were normal and workable when the chapter is saying the opposite. The second mistake is sentimental correction: the tunnels became a hidden refuge. That reading fails too, because the chapter never releases danger below ground. The point is not safety. The point is manageability. A place can be bad and still become home if every other place is worse in the ways that matter most tonight.

That is why the word home sounds damaged here. It no longer means warmth, dignity, or privacy in any full sense. It means some version of location, routine, familiarity, and slightly reduced exposure. It means the place where a person can lower vigilance by a few degrees, store something without losing it instantly, or rely on known faces instead of random threat. Read that way, the title stops sounding sentimental. It becomes accusatory. If a tunnel counts as home, the accusation is aimed first at the city above that narrowed the meaning of the word so far.

The chapter does not let that accusation become abstract policy language either. Seville's presence keeps the pressure embodied. The point is not that history moved people like pieces on a board. The point is that a particular decade hardened certain streets, certain habits, certain dangers, and certain forms of abandonment until the tunnels entered the range of practical choices. Once that is true, outsider disbelief becomes one more luxury of topside life. The person judging the choice gets to ignore the menu that was actually available.

By the end, the chapter has changed the question. The question is no longer why anyone would do something so extreme. It is why the ordinary world above became so punishing that a dangerous underground space could feel more livable than the official alternatives. That shift prepares the next step. If this chapter explains the pressure logic of going below, the next one has to keep close to Seville long enough for him to become more than a witness to collapse. He has to register as a person living inside that narrowed idea of home.
