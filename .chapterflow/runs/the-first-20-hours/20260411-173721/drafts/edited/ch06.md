Touch typing creates a special embarrassment because the learner is not starting from zero. The keyboard is already familiar. Words still appear on the screen. The old habit is fast enough to feel competent even when it is clumsy, limiting, or full of hidden strain. Chapter 6 opens inside that humiliation: the moment when improvement first looks like getting worse on purpose.

That is why habit repair, not beginner learning, is the real subject. The learner is trying to overwrite a pattern that already feels natural. Slowing down matters because the old pattern usually wins whenever speed stays in charge. The chapter does not treat the slowdown as weakness. It treats it as the price of giving the corrected pattern enough space to exist.

The hardest part is frustration tolerance. A learner can know the new fingering is cleaner and still panic when the typing drill screen fills with pauses, stumbles, and mistyped bursts. The old habit offers immediate relief because it still feels smoother. Chapter 6 keeps the pressure visible: temporary regression is not proof that the new pattern is wrong. It is often proof that the old pattern is finally being challenged.

Corrected repetition is what makes that frustration productive. Repetition alone is not enough. If the learner keeps repeating the old motion faster, the old motion becomes harder to replace. The mechanism here is stricter: slow down, use the corrected pattern, notice the mistake, try the cleaner movement again. A keyboard can hide bad habits for a long time because output still appears. What matters is not merely producing letters, but producing them through the new pattern often enough that the body starts treating it as the one to keep.

That is where consolidation matters. The learner cannot force the new habit into place through one long angry session. The chapter stays narrower than pop neuroscience, but its practical lesson is firm: corrected practice needs return, spacing, and time to stabilize. Sleep-linked consolidation matters only because something corrected was practiced first. Rest does not rescue sloppy reps. It helps preserve the pattern that was actually reinforced.

The limit keeps the chapter honest. "Slow down" can become an excuse if the learner moves carefully without installing anything new. Passive waiting is not habit repair. Neither is vague patience. The slowdown helps only while it is attached to a real corrected pattern that the learner is trying again and again. Otherwise the learner is just moving slowly while the old habit remains in charge.

That is what gives the touch typing chapter its force. Relearning a familiar skill demands temporary humility, not just more effort. The learner has to accept the awkward dip, repeat the corrected pattern, and trust consolidation only after correction has actually happened. Programming asked how to reduce conceptual sprawl into tighter evidence loops. Touch typing asks a harsher question: what happens when the obstacle is not confusion about the skill, but loyalty to the wrong habit already living in your hands?
