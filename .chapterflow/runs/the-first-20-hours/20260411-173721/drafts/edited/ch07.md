Go creates a special kind of intimidation because the board seems to expand faster than the learner's attention can follow it. One stone lands, and every nearby point suddenly appears connected to too many futures. The learner does not merely feel inexperienced. The learner feels outnumbered by consequences. Chapter 7 opens there, in the impression that a strategic skill this large cannot honestly be entered until the whole field makes sense.

That is why pattern recognition matters so much in this chapter. The learner does not need the entire board at once. The learner needs recurring shapes, local pressures, and strategic situations that can be recognized before they can be mastered. A pattern does not solve the whole game. It shrinks the field enough for the next decision to become readable. The chapter's first claim is not that Go is simple. It is that strategic vastness becomes more manageable when the learner starts seeing repeated structures instead of one endless blur.

Attack and defense give those structures pressure. A position is not just a diagram to admire. It is a live question about where to press, where to protect, and what can be lost if the reading is careless. That matters because pattern recognition without conflict can turn into empty labeling. Chapter 7 keeps the learner close to local fights so the game remains strategic instead of decorative.

This is where the chapter stops pretending total mastery is the price of entry. A learner can wait forever for the whole board to become transparent. Go does not offer that kind of comfort early. The chapter answers by defending selective competence. Learn enough patterns to recognize danger and opportunity in a smaller area. Read enough to survive local attack/defense pressure. Make useful decisions before the whole game feels owned. That is not fake mastery. It is an honest way into a skill that would otherwise stay impossibly large.

The chapter still keeps a limit visible. Not needing total mastery is not permission to become lazy about the position. A learner can recognize one shape, feel a burst of confidence, and stop reading too early. That confidence is one of the chapter's quiet traps. Patterns are entry points, not substitutes for attention. The board still has to be respected as a real strategic environment where local certainty can collapse under better reading.

That is what gives the Go chapter its force. It does not solve strategic complexity by denying it. It solves it by refusing to make total understanding the starting requirement. Pattern recognition narrows the field. Attack and defense keep the field alive. Selective competence gives the learner permission to play meaningfully before the whole board feels mastered. The skill becomes reachable not because the game shrinks in truth, but because the learner stops demanding complete control before taking the next real look at the position.

Touch typing asked what it takes to betray a familiar mistake. Go asks a different question: what happens when the next skill cannot be repaired through repetition alone, but must be entered through partial reading, local pressure, and decisions made before the whole system is understood?
