Chapter 9 begins where rapid skill acquisition becomes harder to fake. Windsurfing does not let the learner stay inside a tidy practice room. Board, sail, wind, water, balance, and recovery all arrive together, and that bundle can make the skill feel larger than any sensible beginner can enter. If the whole live environment is treated as the first assignment, the learner is likely to confuse legitimate difficulty with an impossible starting condition.

That is why the chapter leans on environment-shaped practice. The method still applies, but it cannot pretend the environment is background scenery. The learner has to notice that some parts of the difficulty come from conditions rather than from the skill in the abstract. Wind changes. Water resists. Equipment behaves differently once it is under load. The chapter therefore treats conditions as part of the learning problem, not as noise that will disappear if the learner just tries harder.

The next move is staged isolation of subskills. A learner does not need the whole live experience immediately in order to begin learning honestly. Balance, sail handling, launch sequence, stance, turning, and recovery can be separated enough to become trainable. That does not make windsurfing artificial. It gives practice somewhere precise to land. One calmer session handling the sail, one shorter window working on balance, or one deliberate repetition of setup and recovery can teach more than a chaotic broad attempt that never stabilizes any part of the skill.

This is where the chapter stays disciplined about risk. Staging is not a softer substitute for the real thing. It is a way to make the real thing enterable without pretending that consequences are irrelevant. The learner still has to reconnect the isolated parts to live conditions. But the sequence matters. Practice goes better when the learner meets wind, water, and equipment through manageable demands instead of through one giant test of courage.

Condition-aware progression is what makes the chapter credible. A calmer launch window, a more controlled shoreline, or a shorter live attempt can be the right choice not because the learner is avoiding the skill, but because the learner is matching the session to current control. The chapter refuses the romance of reckless exposure. Broad contact with the environment does not automatically create better learning. If the learner is too overloaded to notice what failed, the session may feel intense without becoming instructive.

The limit is important. A learner can isolate subskills so aggressively that the live skill never really arrives. Safe fragments can become their own comfort loop. Handling equipment on shore forever is not the same as learning to handle it when wind and water start pushing back. Chapter 9 does not permit that drift. Isolation is useful only while it is building toward honest contact with the environment the skill actually lives in.

That makes windsurfing a sharp late-book test of the method. Earlier chapters proved that a learner can narrow a field, repeat crucial moves, and begin without total mastery. Windsurfing adds a harder condition: the world gets a vote. The method survives that condition by getting more disciplined, not less. Break the skill into parts. Choose conditions that fit current control. Keep risk visible. Then reconnect the parts to the live environment before careful staging turns into permanent shelter.
