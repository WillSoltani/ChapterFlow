Touch typing creates a strange kind of embarrassment because the learner is not starting from zero. The keyboard is already familiar. Words still appear on the screen. The old habit is fast enough to feel competent, even when it is clumsy, limited, or full of hidden strain. Chapter 6 begins inside that humiliation: the moment when getting better first looks like getting worse on purpose.

That is why habit repair is the chapter's real subject. The learner is not simply adding a new skill. The learner is trying to overwrite a pattern that already feels natural. Slowing down matters because the old pattern will usually win if speed remains the priority. The chapter does not treat that slowdown as weakness. It treats it as the cost of giving the corrected pattern enough space to exist at all.

The hardest part is not technical explanation. It is frustration tolerance. A learner can know that the new fingering is cleaner and still panic when the typing drill screen fills with pauses, stumbles, and mistyped bursts. The old habit offers immediate relief because it still feels smoother. Chapter 6 keeps the pressure in view: temporary regression is not proof that the new pattern is failing. It is often proof that the old pattern is being challenged instead of obeyed again.

Corrected repetition is what makes that frustration worth enduring. Repetition alone is not enough. If the learner keeps repeating the old motion faster, the old motion becomes even harder to replace. The chapter's mechanism is stricter than that. Slow down, use the corrected pattern, notice the mistake, try the cleaner movement again. A keyboard can hide bad habits for a long time because output still appears. What matters here is not merely producing letters. It is producing them through the new pattern often enough that the body starts treating that pattern as the one to keep.

This is where consolidation enters the chapter. The learner cannot force the new habit into place through one long angry session. The support stays narrower than pop neuroscience, but the practical lesson is strong enough: repeated corrected practice needs return, spacing, and time to stabilize. Sleep-linked consolidation matters only because something corrected was practiced in the first place. Rest does not rescue sloppy reps. It helps preserve the pattern that was actually reinforced.

The limit keeps the chapter honest. "Slow down" can become its own excuse if the learner moves carefully without installing anything new. Passive waiting is not habit repair. Neither is vague patience. The slowdown is useful only while it is attached to a real corrected pattern that the learner is trying again and again. Otherwise the learner is just moving slowly while the old habit remains in charge.

That is what gives the touch typing chapter its bite. It says that relearning a familiar skill demands temporary humility, not just more effort. The learner has to accept the awkward dip, repeat the corrected pattern, and trust consolidation only after the correction has actually happened. Programming asked the learner to face conceptual sprawl through tighter evidence loops. Touch typing asks a harsher question: what happens when the enemy is not confusion about the skill, but loyalty to the wrong habit already living in your hands?
