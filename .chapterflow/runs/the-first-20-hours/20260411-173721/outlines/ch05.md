Chapter Promise:
Readers will understand that programming improves faster when the learner breaks the work down, builds something small and real, and lets debugging feedback replace research hoarding.

Opening Move:
Open on the way programming can feel like an infinite field of syntax, abstractions, and tutorials before the learner has solved one live problem.

Anchor Allocation:
- anchor 1 used in paragraph(s): P1-P2 for breakdown and anti-bloat framing
- anchor 2 used in paragraph(s): P3-P5 for real-problem iteration and build-test-fix loops
- anchor 3 used in paragraph(s): P5-P7 for avoiding endless research and keeping the work concrete

Paragraph Job Map:
- P1: show the intimidation of programming sprawl
- P2: explain why breakdown changes the entry cost
- P3: show real-problem iteration and small runnable slices
- P4: connect debugging and test output to learning feedback
- P5: attack tutorial paralysis and endless research
- P6: preserve the limit so simplification does not become toy work
- P7: bridge toward touch typing and habit repair

Required Distinction or Mechanism:
The chapter distinguishes useful decomposition from theory-hoarding or shallow toy simplification.

Hard-Depth Tension:
Hard depth must preserve the risk that a learner can keep making the task smaller without ever exposing the work to a real problem or real debugging pressure.

Threshold Question:
When are you reducing complexity so the next build-test-fix loop can teach you, and when are you shrinking the work only to stay safe from real execution?

Takeaway Count Lock:
- Easy: 3 (always)
- Medium: 5
- Hard: 5

Hard Takeaway Topics:
- breakdown as entry control
- real-problem iteration
- debugging as feedback
- tutorial paralysis
- limit between runnable slices and empty toy work

Scenario Lesson Map:
- Scenario 1 (dialogue, work): two coworkers argue over one more tutorial versus running a tiny script now
- Scenario 2 (decision_point, school): a student must choose between reading more syntax notes or building one live assignment slice
- Scenario 3 (postmortem, personal): a hobby coder realizes research replaced execution
- Scenario 4 (predict_reveal, work): prediction about a bug fails and the test output teaches faster than more reading
- Scenario 5 (dilemma, school): a learner has to decide whether a smaller coding task is honest simplification or evasive toy work
- Scenario 6 (before_after, personal): a beginner starts progressing once the project becomes one runnable loop at a time

Scenario Vividness Targets:
At least four scenarios should include a terminal window, test output, a failing script, a help-room whiteboard, bug notes, or a half-working browser tab.
