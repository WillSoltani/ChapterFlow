Chapter Promise:
Readers will understand why the belief that worthwhile learning requires mastery-scale time keeps adults from starting and why the right opening target is usable competence, not expertise.

Opening Move:
Open on ordinary adult overload and the deadening effect of believing that every meaningful skill demands an impossible time budget.

Anchor Allocation:
- anchor 1 used in paragraph(s): P1-P2 for overload and "so much to do... and so little time"
- anchor 2 used in paragraph(s): P2-P4 for the 10,000-hour challenge and the distinction between mastery and competence
- anchor 3 used in paragraph(s): P4-P6 for the frustration barrier and the practical implications of aiming at early competence

Paragraph Job Map:
- P1: make the overloaded-life pressure feel immediate
- P2: show how the 10,000-hour standard freezes action before practice starts
- P3: distinguish elite mastery from good-enough competence
- P4: explain why the first hours feel emotionally expensive
- P5: show how a lower, honest target changes the decision to start
- P6: keep the limit visible so the argument does not collapse into anti-expertise rhetoric
- P7: bridge toward the method chapter by asking what rules make rapid skill acquisition work

Required Distinction or Mechanism:
The chapter distinguishes expert-performance timescales from practical-entry timescales. The mechanism is not motivational magic. It is a target reset: aiming for usable competence changes the expected cost of starting, which makes real practice rational.

Hard-Depth Tension:
Hard depth must preserve the risk that "good enough" can be either a smart scope decision or a self-protective excuse, depending on whether the learner still respects the real cost of getting even moderately competent.

Threshold Question:
When does lowering the target from mastery to competence become strategic realism, and when does it become a polite way of avoiding the hard middle of learning?

Takeaway Count Lock:
- Easy: 3 (always)
- Medium: 5
- Hard: 5

Hard Takeaway Topics:
- overload is real but often misread as lack of discipline
- mastery and competence are different targets
- the 10,000-hour story becomes harmful when applied to every new skill
- the frustration barrier is emotional, not proof of incapacity
- good enough is useful only if the target stays concrete and honest

Scenario Lesson Map:
- Scenario 1 (decision_point, work): a manager delays learning data visualization because she imagines expert-level standards instead of presentation-ready competence
- Scenario 2 (postmortem, school): an evening student quits a language course after early embarrassment and later realizes the target was set at fluency instead of survival-level use
- Scenario 3 (dialogue, personal): two friends argue over whether learning guitar is pointless unless it reaches performance level
- Scenario 4 (predict_reveal, work): a team member predicts coding will take years before usefulness, then discovers a narrower target changes the timeline
- Scenario 5 (dilemma, school): a student choosing between dropping a creative skill or redefining success around competence
- Scenario 6 (before_after, personal): a busy parent reframes drawing from artist-level fantasy to twenty-hour competence and starts practicing

Scenario Vividness Targets:
At least four scenarios should include laptop glare after work, a classroom whiteboard, instrument case scuffs, coffee-shop notebook pages, a dashboard mockup, or a bus ride home after class.

Preview Constraint:
The preview must ask what rules make fast competence possible without listing Chapter 2's principles.
