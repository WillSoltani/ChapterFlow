# Source Sidecar -- Ch10: The Edge Cases and When to Put the Framework Down

## Source strategies covered
Strategies 19 (Annihilation), 25 (Righteous), 28 (One-Upmanship), 30 (Communication), 31 (Inner-Front), 32 (Passive-Aggression), 33 (Chain-Reaction). Part IV closing edge case plus Part V closing cluster and Part VI meta-lessons.

## Strategy 19 -- Annihilation: Close Every Exit (paraphrased)

Annihilation is the high-cost move of surrounding the opponent so completely that every route of retreat, resupply, coalition, or survival is shut at once. In modern terms, it means coordinating legal, financial, reputational, operational, and political pressure so the other side has nowhere useful to turn.

Reversal: this is expensive, exposes the attacker if it fails, and should be reserved for moments of decisive advantage or repeated harm. Used casually, it creates needless wreckage and leaves the operator overcommitted.

## Strategy 25 -- Righteous: Occupy the Moral High Ground (paraphrased)

When third parties are watching, the side read as more just gains structural advantage. The opponent looks like the aggressor. Audience sympathy, legitimacy, and political cost all tilt toward the side that appears cleaner.

Reversal: moral posture is heavy and brittle. One exposed hypocrisy can collapse it. The strategy only holds when the substantive record supports the posture.

## Strategy 28 -- One-Upmanship: Give Them Room to Defeat Themselves (paraphrased)

Some rivals destroy themselves if left alone long enough. The skill is recognizing when attacking would actually help them by unifying their factions or giving them a common enemy. The disciplined operator sometimes waits, watches, and inherits the position after the rival's own contradictions finish the work.

Reversal: waiting becomes cowardice if the rival is not actually collapsing or if their continued presence keeps causing harm. It is a timing skill, not passivity.

## Strategy 30 -- Communication: Penetrate Their Minds (paraphrased)

At its fairest, this is persuasive communication that changes how an audience sees the situation without lying to them. At its dirtiest, it becomes manipulation designed to bypass judgment and steer decisions without informed consent.

Reversal: the teaching line must stay explicit. Persuasion respects the audience's agency and survives scrutiny. Manipulation depends on concealment, distortion, or emotional flooding.

## Strategy 31 -- Inner-Front: Break Them from Within (paraphrased)

Some opponents cannot be beaten from the outside. They collapse only when internal contradictions, factions, or mistrust become unmanageable. The operator identifies those fault lines and amplifies them through pressure, information, or timing.

Reversal: this sits close to subversion. Legitimate use means exposing real contradictions in a dangerous or abusive structure. Illegitimate use means poisoning normal disagreement for private gain.

## Strategy 32 -- Passive-Aggression: Build While Seeming to Submit (paraphrased)

The weaker operator may survive by appearing compliant while quietly building parallel capability, hidden alliances, or independent leverage. The visible surface stays calm while the real balance changes underneath it.

Reversal: this can be prudent self-protection under domination, but it can also become evasive duplicity. It is ethically strongest when direct resistance would bring unfair retaliation and weakest when it is used to dodge honest conflict.

## Strategy 33 -- Chain-Reaction: Use a Small Dramatic Move to Reshape the Field (paraphrased)

The principle is psychological disproportion: one unexpected, dramatic, bounded move causes the opponent or audience to revise their whole strategic picture. In legitimate form, the move is ethical, surprising, and clarifying. It is not a guide to intimidation.

Reversal: if the operator relies on dramatic moves, they become the pattern. Shock decays. A move that once changed the field turns into theater.

## Meta-lessons from Part VI
- Self-mastery comes before any external move.
- Indirect routes are usually cheaper than direct ones.
- Strategy is the long view; tactics are the short view.
- The best fight is the one you do not have to fight.
- Not every situation should be framed as conflict.

## Connecting thread
This closing chapter is not a victory lap. It is the moral and structural limit case for the whole book. These strategies show what pressure, persuasion, subversion, dramatic signaling, and total encirclement can do in legitimate edge cases. They also show where Greene's framing runs thin. The operator needs one more judgment beyond technique: when to stop treating the other side as an opponent at all.

## Moral flag
Highest. The teaching prose must do three things at once: state what the strategies do, name their legitimate applications, and state explicitly where the framework should be put down entirely -- intimacy, grief, genuine collaboration, and any setting where the other side is not actually an opponent.
