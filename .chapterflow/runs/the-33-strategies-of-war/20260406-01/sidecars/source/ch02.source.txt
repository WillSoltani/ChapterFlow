CHAPTER 2 SOURCE SIDECAR — Organizational Warfare (Greene Strategies 5–7)
==========================================================================
Status: PARAPHRASE ONLY. No verbatim text.

OPENING CLAIM
-------------
Most of what an operator accomplishes in any large endeavor is accomplished through other people. The skills of organizational warfare are about how to lead, how to structure a team for adaptive action, and how to hold a group together through long campaigns. The chapter sits between Self-Directed Warfare (Ch1) and the defensive/offensive strategies that follow: nothing later in the book works if the leader cannot get a team to execute it.

STRATEGY 5 — COMMAND-AND-CONTROL: AVOID GROUPTHINK / GIVE MISSION NOT METHOD
----------------------------------------------------------------------------
Greene's diagnosis: a group of competent people in a hierarchy that punishes dissent will produce decisions worse than any individual member would have produced alone. The mechanism is not stupidity. The cost of speaking up is real and the cost of silence is invisible, so silence wins.

The skill is to structure command in a way that allows initiative downward and dissent upward. Greene draws on the German "Auftragstaktik" tradition: tell subordinates the purpose, not the method, and trust them to find the method. The contrast is the micromanaged hierarchy that tells people exactly what to do, gets exactly that, and is helpless when conditions change.

Modern translation: the team lead who gives mission and lets the team solve, vs. the team lead who specifies every step. The first kind of team adapts; the second kind cannot.

Reversal: there are moments — crises, novices, unfamiliar terrain — where command-and-control is the right move and Auftragstaktik would produce chaos. The judgment is whether the people on the ground have enough context to make good decisions; if they do, give them the mission; if they do not, give them the steps.

STRATEGY 6 — CONTROLLED-CHAOS: SEGMENT YOUR FORCES
--------------------------------------------------
A monolithic organization (one big team, one structure, one chain of command) is brittle. A force broken into smaller, semi-autonomous units, each able to act on its own initiative within the broader purpose, is resilient. The smaller units can move faster, take more risks, fail without taking the whole down with them, and recombine in new configurations as the situation changes.

Modern translation: small autonomous product team vs. giant horizontal department. Decentralized network of independent operators vs. centralized hierarchy. The principle scales.

Reversal: segmentation requires that each unit be genuinely capable of acting on its own. A segmented force of incompetents is a slower version of a centralized force of incompetents. The strategy assumes the units are good.

STRATEGY 7 — MORALE: TRANSFORM THE WORK INTO A CRUSADE
------------------------------------------------------
People will work harder, longer, and through more pain for a cause than they will for a job. The skill is to give the work a meaning bigger than the work itself, and to make the team's identity inseparable from the meaning.

Mechanics: a clearly named opposing side (which can be an idea, a problem, or a competitor — not necessarily a person), a clearly named cause that the work serves, and a leader who embodies the cause visibly enough that the team can read it from her behavior.

Modern translation: the startup whose employees describe the work in mission terms vs. the startup whose employees describe it in job-description terms.

Reversal: a manufactured crusade — one where the leader does not believe the cause but uses the language for motivation — is detected by the team within a few months and produces cynicism worse than no crusade at all. The strategy requires sincerity from the leader. **This is the chapter's most important warning and must be preserved.**

THE UNIFYING TENSION
--------------------
The three moves all hinge on a single underlying claim: leadership through other people requires that the leader trust subordinates to do the work the leader cannot do. Mission-not-method trusts them with judgment. Segmentation trusts them with autonomy. Morale trusts them with belief. The chapter's tension is that all three trusts can be misplaced — given to people who do not have the context to act on judgment, the competence to act on autonomy, or the discernment to detect a leader's insincerity. The diagnostic step before each move is whether the trust is earned by the people receiving it, and the discipline is the willingness to use command-and-control when it is not.
