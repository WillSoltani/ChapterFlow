## Strategy 17 — The Divide-and-Conquer Strategy (Defeat Them in Detail)

Core principle: a united opponent is stronger than the sum of its parts; a divided opponent is weaker than the sum of its parts. The skilled operator does not attack a coalition head-on. She identifies the seam between its members -- the issue, interest, or grievance on which they diverge -- drives a wedge into that seam, and then attacks each piece separately, after the alliance has fractured.

The mechanism: find the point of genuine disagreement or competing interest within the opposing coalition, amplify it -- through information, through selective framing of real facts, through action that forces a choice the coalition must disagree on -- and allow the internal tension to do the fracturing work.

Legitimate vs. illegitimate form: the legitimate form identifies and amplifies a genuine divergence of interest that actually exists. The illegitimate form manufactures the appearance of divergence through false information or fabricated conflict. The chapter must name this line explicitly.

Reversal: a clumsy or obvious attempt to drive wedges between coalition members often produces solidarity instead. The revelation that someone is attempting to divide them makes the coalition members circle together around their common interest. The skill is in the delicacy of the wedge, not the force.

Key operational note: divide-and-conquer requires accurate intelligence about what the members of the coalition actually disagree on. Without that intelligence, the wedge targets the wrong seam and either misses or unites. This is another Ch5 prerequisite.

## Strategy 18 — The Turning Strategy (Attack the Soft Flank)

Core principle: every defense has a strong front and a weak flank. The strong front is strong because it is facing the expected direction of attack and has been prepared for it. The weak flank is weak for the opposite reason: it has not been the focus of preparation, or it faces in a direction the opponent did not expect to be attacked from.

The skill is the discipline to look for the flank rather than charging the front, even when the front is the obvious and emotionally satisfying target and the flank requires reconnaissance.

Key mechanism: the flank attack is cheaper and faster than the frontal attack for two reasons. First, the flank is less defended. Second, the opponent's response to a flank attack requires them to partially abandon their frontal defense to redirect resources, which creates a secondary opening.

Modern translation: the competitor who refuses to challenge the incumbent on the incumbent's strongest dimension and instead finds the dimension on which the incumbent is structurally weak and cannot adapt quickly. The negotiator who skips the issue the counterparty has rehearsed their position on and goes after the issue they have not.

Reversal: some positions have no soft flank. Some opponents have already secured every approach. In those cases the only viable attack is the direct one, and the search for a flank is a delay that costs the operator their initiative. The discipline is to look for the flank, but to recognize quickly when the flank does not exist.

## Strategy 20 — The Ripening-for-the-Sickle Strategy (Maneuver into Weakness)

Core principle: sometimes the most efficient attack is not an attack at all but a sequence of small moves that leave the opponent in a position from which they will collapse on their own. The skill is to maneuver the opponent into a position where they have no good options -- where every direction they can move makes things worse -- and then wait for them to move.

The mechanism: rather than attacking the opponent's position directly, remove the conditions that sustain it. Cut off the supply of resources (capital, relationships, attention) that the opponent depends on. Create a situation in which the opponent's own actions produce their decline. Let time and the opponent's own responses do the destructive work.

Modern translation: the negotiator who structures the early phases of a deal so that the counterparty's only remaining option in the final phase is to accept terms they would have refused at the start. The competitor who quietly shapes market conditions over a sustained period so that the rival's eventual collapse looks like bad luck rather than the result of directed action.

Reversal: the strategy requires two conditions that are hard to maintain simultaneously. First, patience that exceeds the patience most operators can sustain. Second, the opponent must not understand what is being done to them. If either condition fails, the strategy collapses. Impatient execution turns ripening into an early assault that the opponent is still in a position to resist. Discovered execution turns ripening into a direct confrontation the operator was not ready for.

## Connecting thread across all three

The three strategies share the same underlying logic: no direct force required. Instead:
- Divide: make the opponent weaker by fracturing what was united.
- Flank: attack from the direction the opponent did not prepare for.
- Ripen: remove the conditions that sustain the opponent and wait for self-collapse.

Each strategy takes more patience than a direct attack. Each strategy produces more efficient outcomes when it works, because the cost of the move is lower than a direct assault while the damage is comparable or greater. Each strategy requires more accurate intelligence about the opponent than a direct attack does.

## Moral flag: MEDIUM

This is the first chapter in the book where the ethical line must be named explicitly in the content. Every indirect move has a legitimate form and an illegitimate form:
- Divide-and-conquer: amplifying a genuine divergence of interest vs. manufacturing false divergence through fabricated information.
- Flank attack: finding a real weakness the opponent has not attended to vs. creating a false impression of strength on the flank you are actually attacking to draw the opponent's defense away.
- Ripening: removing real conditions that sustain the opponent vs. poisoning conditions through dishonest or coercive interference.

The legitimate forms are strategic uses of real information and real situations. The illegitimate forms are manipulation and deception. The hard breakdown of Ch7 must name this line explicitly. The chapter should not present indirection as inherently manipulative; it should be clear that indirection using real facts and genuine intelligence is legitimate strategic action.

## Reversal watchlist

- Divide-and-conquer reversal: clumsy wedge attempts unite rather than divide; the move requires delicacy and genuine intelligence about the seam.
- Flank reversal: some positions have no flank; indefinite search for a flank costs initiative; must recognize when the direct attack is the only option.
- Ripening reversal: requires patience and secrecy simultaneously; if either fails, the strategy collapses into an early forced engagement the operator was not ready for.
- Moral reversal: indirection that uses false information or manufactured conflict crosses from strategy into manipulation; the chapter must name when the move is legitimate and when it is not.
