CHAPTER 1 SOURCE SIDECAR — Self-Directed Warfare (Greene Strategies 1–4)
========================================================================

OPENING CLAIM
-------------
The book opens with the unsettling claim that the first opponent in any
real conflict is the self. Before any external move can land, the operator
must manage their own emotional reactivity, the backward pull of habits
that worked in earlier conditions, and the slow drift of comfort that
softens judgment over time. Self-directed warfare is not preliminary; it
is foundational. Every strategy that follows in the book — defensive,
offensive, unconventional — assumes that the operator running it has
already done this internal work.

Greene treats the framing as deliberately provocative. The point of using
martial vocabulary for internal discipline is to refuse the gentler
register of self-improvement, which Greene believes lets readers off the
hook by making the work feel optional. The strategic register makes the
work feel structural: not something to attempt when you have time, but
something without which nothing else in the book is operable.

STRATEGY 1 — POLARITY: NAME THE OPPOSITION
------------------------------------------
The first self-directed move is the diagnostic one. Most people refuse
to admit who their actual opponents are because admitting it feels uncivil
or paranoid. They mistake politeness for safety. The result is that they
fight the people who are structurally opposed to them late, badly, and
from a position they did not realize they were in.

The skill is to identify, by name, the specific people whose interests
are actively opposed to yours, and to stop pretending the conflict is
something else (a misunderstanding, a stylistic difference, a phase that
will pass). Naming the opposition does not require hostility. It requires
clarity. The point is not to declare war on colleagues; it is to stop
being surprised by people who have been opposing you all along.

Reversal: Treating everyone as an enemy is paranoid, exhausting, and
destroys the collaborative relationships that produce most of a person's
actual results. The strategy is about identifying real opposition, not
manufacturing it. The discipline is to be honest about which conflicts
are real and which are imagined.

Modern translation: In any organization, there are usually two or three
people whose advancement is structurally tied to your stagnation. The
skill is not to attack them. The skill is to know who they are.

STRATEGY 2 — GUERRILLA WAR OF THE MIND: DO NOT FIGHT THE LAST WAR
-----------------------------------------------------------------
The mind defaults to fighting the war it has already learned how to win.
The general who won the last war is often the worst person to fight the
next one, because he reaches for the strategies that worked before, and
the world has moved on since.

The skill is to look at the present situation as if you had no previous
experience of it — to ask "what is actually in front of me?" rather than
"what does this remind me of?" The reminders are usually wrong. The
operator who built a reputation in one era of an industry continues to
deploy that era's playbook against problems that no longer respond to it,
and watches her authority drain away.

Reversal: Not every situation is genuinely new. Some apparently novel
problems are old problems wearing new vocabulary, and the previous
playbook applies. The judgment call is between the two cases — and the
hardest part is that the operator who needs this strategy most is the
operator who has been most successful before, because confidence in the
old playbook is exactly what makes her unable to see when it has stopped
working.

STRATEGY 3 — COUNTERBALANCE: PRESENCE OF MIND UNDER PRESSURE
------------------------------------------------------------
Under stress, the mind narrows. The operator who is angry, frightened, or
embarrassed loses access to the broader picture, the longer time horizon,
and the strategic options they would have seen clearly an hour later.
They make decisions in seconds that they would not make in hours, and
they make them badly.

The skill is to develop a personal practice — Greene treats it almost
ritually — that restores presence of mind under pressure. The specific
practice varies by operator. Some find it in physical exercise. Some in
deliberate slowness of speech. Some in writing the situation down before
acting. Some in a trusted second opinion before any decision made under
emotional load. The practice itself is not the point; the point is that
the operator has a practice and uses it.

The principle: do not act from the narrow place, even when the narrow
place feels like clarity.

Reversal: There are situations where speed is itself the strategy, and
the discipline of waiting is the wrong move. The judgment is between the
urgency that is real and the urgency that is emotional.

STRATEGY 4 — DEATH GROUND: WEAPONIZING CONSTRAINT
-------------------------------------------------
The opposite move from Strategy 3, and one of Greene's favorite
provocations. Comfort makes the mind soft. People with many options,
much time, and no consequences for failure produce mediocre work. People
who are pressed against a wall — financially, reputationally, temporally —
produce extraordinary work because they have to.

Drawn from Sun Tzu and the classical military tradition: a position from
which retreat is impossible forces the operator to either succeed or be
destroyed. The strategic move, when comfort is making the operator slack,
is to deliberately put themselves on death ground. Burn the boats on a
project so that retreat is impossible. Publicly commit to a deadline so
the social cost of missing it is real. Constrain the resources so that
the easy options are off the table.

Reversal: The strategy is dangerous when applied to an operator who is
already at the edge. Adding pressure to a person who is already on death
ground produces collapse, not performance. The strategy is for the
comfortable, not the overwhelmed. This is the failure mode the chapter
must preserve.

THE UNIFYING TENSION
--------------------
The chapter holds two strategies that look like opposites in tension.
Strategy 3 says: do not act from the narrow place. Strategy 4 says:
deliberately create conditions that narrow the field. The resolution is
that they apply to opposite states. Strategy 3 is for the operator
caught in emotional pressure they did not choose. Strategy 4 is for the
operator drifting in comfort they should not be choosing. The discipline
is to know which state you are in. Most operators get this wrong: they
apply the death-ground move when they need presence of mind, and the
slowness move when they need urgency.
