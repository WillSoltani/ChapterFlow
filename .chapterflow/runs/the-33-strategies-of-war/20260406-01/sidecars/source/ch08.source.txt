# Source Sidecar — Ch08: Negotiated Outcomes and Clean Exits

## Source strategies covered
Strategy 21 (Diplomatic-War: Negotiate While Advancing) and Strategy 22 (Exit Strategy: Know How to End Things). Part IV Offensive Warfare, closing pair.

## Strategy 21 — Negotiate While Advancing (paraphrased)

The core principle: talking and fighting are not opposites. The skilled operator does both simultaneously. While pressing the attack, the operator keeps a genuine negotiating channel open, so the opponent faces both the escalating cost of continued resistance and the visible alternative of a settlement that ends that cost. This pairing makes settlement attractive in a way it never is when the only choices are continued fighting or abject surrender.

The face-saving exit is load-bearing infrastructure, not a courtesy. An opponent who has no path to settlement that preserves some dignity will fight to the end regardless of cost. The operator who provides that path turns pressure into leverage; the operator who denies it turns pressure into desperation.

Reversal: the strategy collapses if the negotiating channel is not sincere. An opponent who realizes the offered exit is fake will fight to the end. The operator loses the leverage the exit was supposed to provide and is now in a direct confrontation they structured to avoid. The sincerity of the exit is not optional -- it is the mechanism by which the strategy works.

## Strategy 22 — The Exit Strategy: Know How to End Things (paraphrased)

The core principle: the hardest moment in any campaign is the moment when continuing costs more than stopping but the operator's investment makes stopping feel impossible. Every psychological force pushes against exit: sunk-cost reasoning, public commitment, personal identification with the project, the narrative that just one more push will produce the breakthrough.

The discipline is to know in advance -- before the campaign begins -- what the conditions for exit are, and to honor those conditions when they arrive. The pre-commitment matters because in the moment, the psychology of continued investment will overwhelm the rational assessment.

Honoring the pre-committed exit is doubly important when the exit has been named publicly. An operator who publicly names an exit condition and then fails to honor it damages their credibility for all future negotiations. The opponent and all observers learn that the operator's stated limits are not real limits, and future negotiations begin from that lesson.

Reversal: there are situations where persistence is the right move and the apparent exit conditions are misleading. The judgment is whether the cost of continuing is genuinely greater than the cost of stopping, or whether it merely feels that way. The difficulty is that this judgment must be made inside the very psychology that biases toward continuation.

## Connecting thread
Both strategies share a common structural requirement: credibility. The negotiating channel in Strategy 21 works only if the exit offer is sincere. The exit discipline in Strategy 22 works only if the pre-committed conditions are honored. In both cases, the operator's future strategic capacity depends on whether their stated positions can be trusted.

## Moral flag
Medium-high. The ethical line is whether the exit is real or simulated. A sincere exit offer that gives the opponent a genuine path to settlement is legitimate paired pressure. A fake exit offer designed to waste the opponent's time while the operator continues advancing is manipulation. The distinction matters strategically: the fake exit, once discovered, poisons all future negotiations.
