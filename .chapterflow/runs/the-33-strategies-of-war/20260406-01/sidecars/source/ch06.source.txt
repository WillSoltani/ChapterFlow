## Strategy 14 — The Blitzkrieg Strategy (Speed and Suddenness)

Core principle: speed is itself a weapon, not merely a means of delivery. An operator who moves faster than the opponent can process cycles through multiple decision-action loops before the opponent has completed a single response. By the time the opponent acts, they are targeting a position the operator has already vacated.

The underlying frame is Boyd's OODA loop (observe, orient, decide, act). The side with the faster loop wins the engagement regardless of most other factors, because the slower side's actions are perpetually out of sync with the faster side's current position.

Key mechanism: operational tempo — the sustained speed of decision and execution — is the variable to develop. This is not the speed of a single move but the habitual rhythm of a cycle that runs faster than the opponent's cycle.

Reversal (and most important boundary condition): speed without judgment accelerates failure. The fast operator who is moving in the wrong direction simply arrives at the wrong destination sooner and with less ability to correct, because the momentum of a fast-moving operation is harder to redirect than a slow one. Speed is a force multiplier; it multiplies both good and bad decisions.

Connection to prior chapters: the OODA loop was introduced in Ch1 (Counterbalance strategy) as a frame for understanding how stress narrows the observer's ability to orient accurately. Ch6 formalizes it as an offensive principle. Ch5 (intelligence) is a prerequisite — fast operators who have not done the intelligence work move fast against the wrong targets.

## Strategy 15 — Forcing Strategies (Initiative and Control of the Dynamic)

Core principle: the skilled operator does not respond to the opponent's moves; she makes the opponent respond to hers. She controls the agenda, the sequence, the pace, and the terms of engagement, leaving the opponent in a reactive position throughout.

Key mechanism: initiative — the ongoing act of setting the terms before the opponent can. At every choice point, the relevant question is "who is setting the terms here?" If the answer is the opponent, the next move is to take them back.

The distinction between speed and initiative: speed is about cycle rate; initiative is about who is structuring the engagement. An operator can be fast and reactive (fast but not in control), or slow and in control (setting the terms but slowly). Ideally both operate together.

Reversal: initiative without a goal is random aggression. Setting terms that do not actually serve the long horizon wastes the initiative advantage and can back the operator into positions she did not want to occupy.

## Strategy 16 — The Center-of-Gravity Strategy (Hit the One Target That Decides Everything)

Core principle: every opponent has a center of gravity — the one node whose loss collapses the whole position. The center of gravity is rarely the most obvious target. It may be a supply line, a key relationship, a critical assumption underlying the entire strategy, a single financial dependency, or the confidence of a key decision-maker.

The skilled attacker identifies the center of gravity first and goes for it, ignoring the more visible and expensive targets that would not actually decide anything even if taken.

Key mechanism: asymmetric return on targeting — attacking the center of gravity produces a disproportionate collapse relative to the cost of the attack, because everything built on that foundation collapses with it. Attacking secondary targets is expensive and produces only proportional damage.

Reversal (highest-risk reversal in the chapter): misidentifying the center of gravity is more dangerous than not attacking at all. An attack against what appeared to be the center of gravity but was actually a secondary target leaves the attacker out of position, having committed resources to a sub-optimal target, while the opponent's real center remains intact. Strategy 13 (Intelligence, from Ch5) is the explicit prerequisite for this strategy.

## Connecting thread across all three

The three strategies form a coherent offensive sequence: speed cycles faster than the opponent's response capacity (so you are always acting, they are always reacting); initiative structures the terms of engagement (so you are choosing the terrain, they are navigating it); the decisive point concentrates force against the one target that matters most (so you are using resources efficiently, they are absorbing damage without knowing where the real hit is coming).

The metapoint: all three strategies require the prior conditions from Ch5. Grand strategy tells you which fights are worth entering; intelligence tells you where the center of gravity actually is. Speed, initiative, and decisive-point targeting are the execution layer on top of that foundation.

## Reversal watchlist for the chapter

- Speed reversal: fast bad decisions reach wrong destinations faster; momentum is harder to redirect than direction.
- Initiative reversal: initiative held without goal becomes random aggression; setting wrong terms is worse than reacting.
- Center-of-gravity reversal: misidentification is worse than no attack; intelligence is the prerequisite; secondary-target attacks leave attacker exposed.
- System-level reversal: an operator who only has speed but not intelligence (Ch5) or grand strategy (Ch5) is aggressive and uninformed. The three offensive disciplines in this chapter require the two prior conditions to function.
