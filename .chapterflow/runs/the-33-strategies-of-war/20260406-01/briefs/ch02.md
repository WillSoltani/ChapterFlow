Chapter Title: Leading Through Other People
Position In Book: Second chapter; consolidates Greene's Part II (Organizational Warfare, Strategies 5–7).
Source Sidecar Path: sidecars/source/ch02.source.txt

Core Claim:
Most of what an operator accomplishes in any large endeavor is accomplished through other people, and that fact creates three specific leadership disciplines that are not interchangeable with the self-directed work of Chapter 1. Give people the mission rather than the method when they have enough context to act on judgment, and give them the steps when they do not. Break a monolithic team into small autonomous units that can move, fail, and recombine without bringing the whole structure down — but only when those units are competent enough to deserve the autonomy. Tie the work to a cause large enough that the team's identity becomes inseparable from it — and only when the leader actually believes the cause, because manufactured belief is detected within months and produces cynicism worse than none. The three moves share a single hinge: leadership through other people requires the leader to trust subordinates with the work the leader cannot do herself, and the diagnostic question that comes before any of the moves is whether the trust is earned by the people receiving it.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
Chapter 1 was about the operator's internal contests. Chapter 2 is about the contests that begin the moment the operator stops being the only person in the room. The chapter cannot be confused with any later chapter because the three moves are explicitly about the leader-subordinate relationship under conditions where the subordinates are loyal collaborators, not opponents. None of the offensive or defensive strategies that follow operate in this register. The chapter also carries the book's most important leadership warning (manufactured crusades backfire), which is a sentence that does not appear elsewhere.

Author Logic Chain:
First the author establishes that competent people in hierarchies that punish dissent produce worse decisions than any of them would alone, because the cost of speaking up is real and the cost of silence is invisible, so silence wins. Then the author shows that the structural fix is to push initiative downward (mission, not method), to break monoliths into autonomous units that can adapt, and to bind the team to a cause that gives the long campaign meaning. Then the author concludes that all three moves depend on trust the leader has placed in subordinates who can carry it, and that the leader who places the trust badly produces failures (paralysis, fragmentation, cynicism) that look like the moves themselves but with the wrong people receiving them.

Required Anchors:
- Naya, head of ops at a 200-person logistics company, who runs an experiment: for one quarter, she stops giving her regional managers tactical instructions and gives them only the quarter's three operating goals plus a weekly thirty-minute review. The friction is that her two most senior managers ask for the old instructions back in the first week. She holds the line. By the end of the quarter, on-time delivery has improved in four of six regions, declined in one (the manager there resigns at week six and is replaced from inside), and stayed flat in one. The consequence: she rewrites the regional manager job description for the next year.
- Tomas, the founder of a 90-person climate-software company that has been organized as one engineering org under one VP. He breaks it into three pods of fifteen, each owning a customer segment, each with its own product manager and engineering lead, each with quarterly autonomy on roadmap. The friction: the VP of engineering quits in protest in month two. The consequence: by month nine, the three pods have shipped seven features that the prior monolithic structure would have spent eighteen months coordinating on.
- Camila, an executive director at a midsize advocacy nonprofit who walks into her first all-staff meeting after twelve months on the job and tells the room what she actually believes about the fight they have been losing for three years. She does not use the words "we are going to win this." She tells them, in specific terms, why she came to this organization and what she would resign over if it were taken away. The friction: two senior staff who had been running the operation as a job leave within six weeks. The consequence: the eight who stay produce the most effective campaign the organization has run in a decade. The reversal must be present: this works because Camila already believed it, and would not have worked at all if she had been performing belief.

Specific Applications:
- Operations leadership in distributed teams.
- Founder-stage org design when the original monolithic structure has stopped scaling.
- Mission-driven nonprofits, civic organizations, and political campaigns where the cause is the recruiting tool.
- Any leader managing a team of senior people who have more context on the daily work than the leader does.

Common Misreadings:
- Reading mission-not-method as a license to be hands-off with people who do not have the context to operate without direction.
- Reading segmentation as a structural fix that works regardless of the units' competence.
- Reading "transform the work into a crusade" as a motivational technique rather than a sincerity test on the leader.
- Reading the chapter as license to push all decisions downward in environments where the subordinates lack the context, the resources, or the accountability to make those decisions well.

Counterarguments or Limits:
- Mission-not-method requires that the people receiving the mission have enough context to operate. New hires, novices, and people in genuinely unfamiliar terrain need command-and-control, and giving them mission instead of method is abdication, not leadership.
- Segmentation requires that each unit be genuinely capable. A segmented force of incompetents is just a slower version of a centralized force of incompetents.
- Manufactured crusades produce cynicism worse than no crusade at all. This is the chapter's most important warning, and the reversal must be load-bearing in the prose.

Previous Chapter Bridge:
Chapter 1 was about the four contests the operator wins inside her own head before any external move. Chapter 2 is about the moment the work stops being a contest the operator runs alone. The bridge: every move in this chapter is run by an operator who has done the Chapter 1 work, because a leader who is fighting yesterday's war or acting from the narrow place will mis-place every trust she tries to extend.

Next Chapter Bridge:
Chapter 3 turns from how to lead a team toward how to choose which fights the team will fight. The defensive register begins.

Cross-Chapter Tensions:
- Tension with Ch1: the mission-not-method move requires the leader to do less, but only after she has done the internal work that lets her tell when she should be doing more. Less, well-placed, is harder than less in general.
- Tension with Ch5 (Long View): a leader pushing initiative downward must have a grand strategy clear enough that the downward initiatives serve it. Mission-not-method without a clear mission is just chaos.

Moral Complexity:
Medium. The crusade move is the single most ethically loaded move in the chapter and must be framed as a sincerity test on the leader, not a motivation technique. Manufactured belief is the failure mode, and the reader should leave the chapter unable to use this strategy as a tool of manipulation.

Concept Budget:
- target concept count: 3
- rationale: three Greene strategies, three distinct leadership moves; they share a hinge but they are not the same move.

Hard-Depth Minimum:
The earned-trust diagnostic is the chapter's hard-depth content. Hard depth must hold the question: how does a leader know whether her people are ready to receive the trust each of these moves places in them, and what is the cost of getting that diagnosis wrong in each direction (over-trust → fragmentation, paralysis, cynicism; under-trust → micromanagement, brittleness, attrition)? The threshold question is whether the leader can tell when her instinct to push initiative downward is leadership and when it is abdication.
