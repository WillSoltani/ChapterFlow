Chapter Title: The First Enemy Is You
Position In Book: Opening chapter; consolidates Greene's Part I (Self-Directed Warfare, Strategies 1–4) into one teaching unit.
Source Sidecar Path: sidecars/source/ch01.source.txt

Core Claim:
Before any external strategy can land, the operator has to win four internal contests: name the people whose interests are actually opposed to yours instead of pretending they are not, abandon the playbook that won the last war when the conditions have shifted underneath it, build a personal practice that restores presence of mind under pressure, and weaponize constraint when comfort is making your judgment soft. These four moves are not preparatory exercises. They are the foundation on which every other strategy in the book rests, and an operator who skips them runs every later strategy from a position they have not yet earned.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
This is the only chapter in the book where the opponent is internal. Every other chapter teaches a move against another party; this one teaches four moves against the operator's own habits of mind. The chapter cannot be confused with any other in the book because the entire register is reflexive — the work happens before the conflict starts, not during it. It also carries the unique tension of the book: two of the four moves (presence of mind, death ground) point in opposite directions, and the chapter has to teach the reader how to know which one applies to which state.

Author Logic Chain:
First the author establishes that most operators lose external conflicts because they were already losing the internal one — they were misnaming who their opponents were, fighting yesterday's war, acting from emotional narrowness, or drifting in comfort. Then the author shows that each of these failures has a specific corresponding discipline: diagnostic honesty about opposition, willingness to abandon the previous playbook, a personal practice for presence of mind, and the deliberate creation of constraint. Then the author concludes that the four disciplines are not separable from the rest of the book — that every later strategy presupposes an operator who has already done this work, and that the operator who skips it will run every later move from a position of self-deception.

Required Anchors:
- A founder named Devon whose Series-A playbook (sleep at the office, ship daily, decide in the room) carried his first company to a successful exit and is now slowly killing his second one at Series-C scale. He is fighting the last war. The friction is that the team that grew up under the new conditions cannot tell him what is wrong because his confidence in the old playbook is exactly what made him impossible to argue with. The consequence is that two senior hires have left in six weeks and the lead investor has asked for a check-in.
- A senior counsel named Imani who, after sending an email she regretted at 11:42 PM during a contentious deal, builds a personal practice: any decision made under emotional load gets written down on a single page before it gets sent. She has to share the page with her chief of staff, who has authority to hold it overnight. The friction is that the practice feels excessive to her colleagues until they watch her hold a position through a hostile board call that would have flattened her three years ago. The consequence is that her chief of staff now runs the same practice for two other partners.
- A pre-tenure researcher named Aiden whose third book has been ninety percent finished for fourteen months. The structural problem is comfort: the partial manuscript is impressive enough that nobody is pressing him on the missing chapter, and his teaching schedule absorbs all the remaining hours. He buys a non-refundable ticket to a conference where the manuscript is on the program for delivery and tells two senior colleagues the date. The death-ground move is the ticket. The consequence: the chapter exists by week seven.

Frameworks or Terms Introduced:
- "The first enemy is you" — the chapter's organizing claim, drawn directly from Greene's framing of Part I.
- "Polarity" / naming the opposition — Strategy 1.
- "Do not fight the last war" — a longstanding military aphorism, used by Greene as Strategy 2.
- "Presence of mind" — Strategy 3, the discipline of not acting from the narrow place.
- "Death ground" — Strategy 4, drawn from Sun Tzu and the classical military tradition; the deliberate construction of conditions in which retreat is not an option.

Specific Applications:
- Workplace politics: identifying which colleagues' advancement is structurally tied to your stagnation, without becoming paranoid or aggressive.
- Founder / executive judgment: noticing when the playbook that built your reputation no longer fits the conditions.
- High-stakes communication: building a personal practice that prevents emotionally-loaded decisions from being executed in the same hour they are conceived.
- Long projects: weaponizing public commitment, financial constraint, or removed options to convert comfortable drift into delivery.

Common Misreadings:
- Reading the chapter as self-help. It is not. The register is strategic discipline, not personal growth.
- Reading "presence of mind" and "death ground" as compatible recommendations to apply simultaneously. They apply to opposite states, and the chapter must make this explicit.
- Reading "name your enemies" as a prescription to treat colleagues as opponents. The discipline is diagnostic, not adversarial.
- Reading "do not fight the last war" as a recommendation to discard experience. The judgment is between conditions that have genuinely changed and conditions that look new but are not.

Counterarguments or Limits:
- Self-directed discipline does not substitute for external strategy. An operator who is brilliantly self-managed and tactically incompetent still loses.
- The polarity move fails in genuinely collaborative environments where there is no real structural opposition to identify; applied indiscriminately it becomes paranoia.
- Death ground applied to an operator already at the edge produces collapse, not performance. The strategy is for the comfortable, not the overwhelmed.
- Presence of mind taken too far becomes paralysis; some situations require speed and the discipline of waiting is the wrong move.

Next Chapter Bridge:
Chapter 2 turns from self-directed work to organizational warfare — the work the operator does through other people. The bridge: every move taught in Chapter 2 (mission-driven command, force segmentation, rallying a team around a cause) presupposes a leader who has already done the Chapter 1 work. A leader who is fighting yesterday's war, or who decides in the narrow place, or who cannot tell his real opponents from his collaborators, will not lead other people well no matter how good the organizational technique.

Cross-Chapter Tensions:
- Tension with Chapter 5 (Long View): the death-ground move is short-horizon urgency; grand strategy is long-horizon patience. The operator must run both at once. The chapter should plant this tension as a forward question.
- Tension with Chapter 9 (Line of Least Expectation): the polarity move asks the operator to be honest about opposition, but Chapter 9 will teach indirect moves that include strategic ambiguity. The reader should be aware that internal honesty and external presentation can legitimately diverge.

Moral Complexity:
Low. The chapter is about discipline directed at the self, which carries no third-party harm. The one place where ethical care is needed: the polarity move must not be presented as a license to treat collaborators as enemies. Frame it as diagnostic honesty, not as combative posture.

Concept Budget:
- target concept count: 4
- rationale: The chapter consolidates four of Greene's strategies. Compressing further would lose the unifying tension between Strategy 3 and Strategy 4, which is the chapter's sharpest analytical content. Expanding further would dilute the four moves into a list.

Hard-Depth Minimum:
The unifying tension between presence of mind (Strategy 3) and death ground (Strategy 4) MUST survive into hard depth. Hard depth must explicitly hold the question: how does the operator know which of the two states they are in, and what is the cost of misdiagnosing? The threshold question is whether the operator can distinguish urgency that is real from urgency that is emotional, and comfort that is safe from comfort that is corrosive — and the chapter must concede that this judgment is genuinely hard and that getting it wrong has both directions of cost.
