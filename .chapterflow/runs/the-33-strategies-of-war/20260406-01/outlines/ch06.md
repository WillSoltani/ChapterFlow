# Chapter Outline — Ch06: Speed, Initiative, and the Decisive Point

## Opening question (P1)
Not stated directly. Opens on Phoebe's situation: a company that cannot win a feature war, a pricing war, or a talent war against the incumbent — but that is already operating on the incumbent's next customer before the incumbent has finished processing the previous one. The chapter's live tension: what kind of advantage is speed, and why does a faster loop beat a larger one?

## Paragraph map (7 paragraphs)

### P1 — Hook / Speed as a structural weapon
Opens with Phoebe's company not winning on the obvious dimensions but winning because the incumbent's decisions are always targeting a position Phoebe has already vacated. The key insight introduced: speed is not about moving fast in absolute terms; it is about cycling faster than the opponent's response capacity. The OODA loop introduced briefly as the conceptual frame (callback to Ch1 where this appeared in the self-directed context). The chapter's stakes: three offensive disciplines that decide fast campaigns, each building on the prior conditions established in Ch5.

### P2 — Speed mechanics and the failure mode
Operational tempo spelled out: the sustained rhythm of observe-orient-decide-act that runs faster than the opponent's loop. Not the speed of a single move but the habitual cycle rate that compounds over an engagement. Then the reversal: speed multiplies direction. An operator moving fast toward the wrong destination gets there sooner and with less ability to correct, because momentum is harder to redirect than direction. The practical implication: speed is the last thing you should develop if you have not done the prior work (strategy, intelligence) that determines what direction is correct.

### P3 — Initiative as distinct from speed
Introduces the second discipline. Phoebe's speed cycles faster; Kofi's initiative is different — he chooses the terms, the agenda, the sequence, and the pace of a negotiation that the counterparty thought they had structured. The distinction made explicit: speed is about cycle rate; initiative is about who is structuring the engagement. A fast reactive operator is not controlling the terms; a slow initiating operator is. The two disciplines are independent: you can have both (ideal), one, or neither. The skill in the initiative discipline is the diagnostic question: at every choice point, who is setting the terms? If the answer is the opponent, the next move is to take them back.

### P4 — Anchor: Phoebe at the point of maximum speed leverage
Returns to Phoebe. The incumbent has noticed her and is moving to block. The blocking move is targeting the account she secured six weeks ago and the distribution channel she locked in three weeks ago. The incumbent's product team has shipped a competitive response to the interface Phoebe launched last quarter. By the time the blocking move reaches the market, Phoebe is three moves past where the block is aimed. This paragraph names why: the incumbent's decision cycle is a quarter; Phoebe's is a week. The incumbent is always fighting the previous war. The paragraph ends with the practical implication: the speed discipline is not about any single fast decision; it is about building an organizational culture in which the cycle rate is itself the structural advantage.

### P5 — Decisive point: the center of gravity
Introduces the third discipline through Declan. He does not attack the legacy provider's interface, pricing, or service depth. He attacks the assumption underneath the provider's entire competitive position: that compliance reviews require human sign-off. Everything the provider does well — their depth of expertise, their client relationships, their documentation — rests on that assumption. When Declan's platform automates the compliance layer, those strengths become irrelevant. The concept named: center of gravity. The one node whose loss collapses everything built on it. The relationship to intelligence (Ch5) made explicit: Declan spent six weeks with the legacy provider's customers before identifying the target. The decisive point is not obvious; it requires the intelligence work to find.

### P6 — Hard-depth analytical climax: the distinction between secondary targets and the center, and the risk of misidentification
This is the chapter's analytical spine. Three things that operators routinely mistake for centers of gravity: the most visible target (expensive to attack, produces only proportional damage), the most defended target (well-defended specifically because it is not the center), and the target that would be most satisfying to take (emotionally loaded, not strategically decisive). The center of gravity is usually none of these. It is the dependency that is invisible to the opponent because it has never been tested. The misidentification risk named: an operator who attacks a secondary target believing it to be the center has committed resources to an inefficient fight and left their own position exposed for the duration. The meta-point: the three disciplines (speed, initiative, decisive point) all require the prior conditions from Ch5. Grand strategy tells you which fights are worth entering; intelligence tells you where the center of gravity is. Without those, speed accelerates in the wrong direction, initiative sets the wrong terms, and the decisive point is misfired. This chapter is the execution layer, not the foundation.

### P7 — Bridge
Returns briefly to the cost of getting the targeting wrong, then bridges forward. Speed and initiative are how you win the engagement. The decisive point is how you win efficiently. The combination produces campaigns that are over before the opponent has oriented to what is happening. The forward bridge: the next move is not about direct force at all — there are offensive disciplines that do not require speed, initiative, or a single decisive hit, because they operate through indirection entirely.
