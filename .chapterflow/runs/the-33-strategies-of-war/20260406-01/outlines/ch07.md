# Chapter Outline — Ch07: Indirection: Flank, Wedge, Long Maneuver

## Opening question (P1)
Not stated directly. Opens on the image of a well-funded coalition presenting a united front. The coalition is strong; a direct attack would fail. The chapter's live tension: what do you do when the opponent is stronger at the front and you cannot or will not go directly?

## Paragraph map (7 paragraphs)

### P1 — Hook / The three disciplines of indirection
Opens on Maisie's situation: a 12-member industry coalition, a unified public position, apparently impenetrable. The chapter's thesis introduced not as a statement but as a question made visible: what does the skilled operator do when the front is too strong? Three disciplines, each avoiding direct force: divide the coalition at its internal seam, attack the unprepared flank rather than the defended front, or remove the conditions the opponent depends on until they collapse on their own. The chapter locates these in the offensive sequence -- after Ch6's speed/initiative disciplines, these are the moves that do not require any of those, because they do not require force at all.

### P2 — Wedge: finding and widening the real seam
The divide-and-conquer discipline explained. A united coalition is stronger than its parts; a divided one is weaker. The skill is not to attack the coalition but to find the genuine divergence of interest within it -- the issue on which members actually disagree but have agreed to paper over -- and to make that divergence visible and costly to maintain. Maisie's research: three members of the coalition have a specific problem with one provision. She finds this through public filings and conversations, not invention. She provides information that makes the conflict of interest more visible to those three members and to the larger coalition members who might care. The coalition fractures on that provision. Named point: the legitimate form of this move amplifies a real divergence; the illegitimate form manufactures a false one through fabricated information. One sentence on the line.

### P3 — Wedge reversal and intelligence prerequisite
The reversal named explicitly: a clumsy or obvious wedge attempt produces solidarity rather than fracture. When coalition members realize someone is trying to divide them, they circle together around the shared interest. The skill is delicacy -- the wedge must not be visible as a wedge. The intelligence prerequisite (callback to Ch5): the entire strategy depends on knowing where the real seam is. Without that knowledge, the wedge targets the wrong seam and either misses or alerts the coalition. The diagnostic question the divide-and-conquer operator must answer before acting: is there a genuine divergence here, and do I understand it well enough to amplify it without triggering unity?

### P4 — Flank: the discipline of not charging the front
The turning strategy explained. Every position has a strong front and a weak flank. The front is strong because it was prepared for the expected attack; the flank is weak because it was not. Leif's company enters a market by targeting the incumbent's pricing model rather than its product -- the front is the product (well-defended, the incumbent's published competitive advantage), the flank is the pricing architecture (not a focus of the incumbent's public positioning, not something they can change quickly without disrupting their existing customer base). The flank attack is cheaper and faster because it is less defended; it also requires the opponent to partially abandon their frontal defense to respond, creating a secondary opening. Reversal: some positions have no soft flank. Prolonged search for a non-existent flank costs the initiative advantage from Ch6. The discipline includes recognizing when the direct attack is the only remaining option.

### P5 — Ripening: removing conditions, not attacking directly
The long-maneuver discipline explained through Theron. The national org entering the territory is stronger. Theron does not fight them. Over 18 months, he builds the relationships the national org did not build, joins the standards committee, and shifts the preferred funder metrics slightly. None of these are attacks on the national org. All of them remove the conditions that would have made the national org's entry easy. By the time the national org looks at the territory's economics again, the entry cost is higher and the credibility cost is more visible. They de-prioritize. Key concept: ripening is not patience in a passive sense. It is the active sequence of removing sustaining conditions, one by one, until the opponent's own assessment leads them away. Named point: the legitimate form removes real conditions using real actions; the manipulative form poisons conditions through dishonest interference.

### P6 — Hard-depth: the ethical line in indirection, and when not to use these moves
This is the chapter's analytical and moral spine. Three moves, three ethical lines, stated explicitly:
- Wedge: amplifying a real divergence that you found through research and that actually exists is legitimate strategic framing. Inventing the divergence, fabricating information to create the appearance of conflict, or spreading false information about one coalition member to another is manipulation.
- Flank: finding a real weakness the opponent has not attended to is legitimate intelligence-based targeting. Creating a false impression about where your attack is aimed in order to draw defense away from the real target is deception.
- Ripening: removing real conditions through legitimate participation (building relationships, serving on committees, providing honest input) is legitimate positioning. Deliberately interfering with the opponent's conditions through dishonest or coercive means -- poisoning relationships, sabotaging processes, bribing gatekeepers -- is coercion.

The structural point: the legitimate forms all share the same property -- they work with what is real, not with what has been manufactured. The manipulative forms manufacture the situation rather than working within it. This distinction is not only ethical; it is strategic. Manufactured situations are fragile; they require ongoing maintenance and collapse when they are discovered. Real situations, once activated, are self-sustaining.

### P7 — Bridge
The three indirection disciplines close the chapter on the insight that the most durable offensive moves do not require direct force. The chapter looks forward: the next chapter introduces a different pair of disciplines -- diplomatic pressure paired with a genuine exit for the opponent, and the discipline of honoring pre-committed exit conditions -- which require both the offensive capacity established here and the self-directed discipline from the book's opening.
