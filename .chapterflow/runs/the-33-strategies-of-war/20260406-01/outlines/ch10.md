# Chapter Outline -- Ch10: The Edge Cases and When to Put the Framework Down

## Opening question (P1)
Not stated directly. Opens on Leona with a fully prepared email package that would finish a supplier in one coordinated strike. The live tension: if you can win by crushing, should you?

## Paragraph map (7 paragraphs)

### P1 -- Hook / The last judgment
Concrete opener on Leona's unsent email. She has the legal packet, regulator memo, customer notice, and lender briefing ready. This is the kind of decisive edge move earlier chapters prepared the reader to understand. The tension is new: the problem is no longer whether she can win, but whether total victory is warranted. Set the closing frame: these are the highest-risk strategies in the book because they work on exits, perception, internal fracture, and fear.

### P2 -- Annihilation, righteousness, one-upmanship
Use Leona to teach three edge tools in one sequence. Annihilation closes every exit and is only justified when the operator has decisive advantage and repeated harm must be stopped. Righteous posture matters because third parties are watching, but it only holds if the record can survive scrutiny. One-upmanship appears when the rival is already destroying themselves and further attack would rescue them by giving them a common enemy. Leona chooses a bounded case, not a spectacle.

### P3 -- Communication, inner-front, passive-aggression, chain-reaction
Shift from battlefield control to mind and system control. Communication can be persuasion or manipulation. Inner-front amplifies real contradictions inside an opposing structure. Passive-aggression, in Greene's strategic sense, means seeming compliant while quietly building parallel leverage. Chain-reaction is a small dramatic move with outsized psychological effect. Name legitimate and illegitimate forms. These are dangerous because they operate inside people and groups, not only against positions.

### P4 -- Elias and the wrong frame
Second anchor. Elias, days after his father's funeral, realizes he could win the estate dispute by splitting the family from within, appearing compliant while moving papers, and waiting for grief to push others into mistakes. He recognizes that every move would probably work. He also recognizes that this is grief, not war. He chooses a mediator, shared records, slower pace, and plain language. The point is not softness. The point is that strategic victory here would be a moral and relational defeat.

### P5 -- Explicit no-framework zones
Name them without euphemism: intimacy, grief, genuine collaboration, and any context where the other side is not actually an opponent. Give a practical test: if the goal depends on trust being thicker after the conversation than before, do not reach for these tools. Also name the legitimate use zones: abusive structures, repeat bad actors, public conflicts with third-party audiences, and situations where leaving exits open predictably reproduces harm.

### P6 -- Meta-lessons closing the loop
Weave in Part VI. Self-mastery before external moves. Indirect usually beats direct. Strategy is long view, tactics short view. Best fight is often the one avoided. These lessons narrow, rather than widen, the legitimate use of the dirty strategies. If an operator needs dramatic shocks every week, righteous theater every month, or covert pressure as a default style, they are already strategically lost.

### P7 -- Final bridge back to Ch1
Close the book's loop. Ch1 began by naming the self as the first enemy. Ch10 ends by naming the self as the last check on permission. The final strategic skill is not harsher aggression but disciplined refusal: knowing when a win would deform the thing you were supposed to protect. End with the line that the reader's hardest call is sometimes not how to fight, but whether to fight at all.
