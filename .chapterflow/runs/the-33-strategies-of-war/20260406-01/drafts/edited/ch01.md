# The First Enemy Is You

Devon noticed it the third time. His head of engineering paused before answering his question, glanced once at the project manager, and gave him the kind of careful, half-corrected reply you give to someone whose temper you have learned. It was a Tuesday standup at his second company, the one that had just closed its Series C, and the room had been quietly rearranging itself around him for at least a month. Devon was forty-one, had built one company to a successful exit, and had spent that first company sleeping on a couch in the office two nights a week because in 2014 that was what shipping a product meant. The couch had become a kind of identity. He was still trying to use it.

The first opponent in any real conflict is not the person across the table. It is the operator about to walk into the room. Four internal contests have to be won before any external move can land, and an operator who has not won them runs every later strategy from a position they have not yet earned. The four contests are not preparation. They are foundation. Skip them and the rest of the work in this book becomes technically correct and quietly useless.

The first contest is diagnostic, and Devon was failing it. There was a board member, the one who had insisted on the new CFO, who had spent the last two months building a patient, evidence-rich case that Devon's operating style did not scale past a certain headcount. Devon knew this. He also refused to know it, because admitting it felt uncivil, and uncivil felt the same as wrong. The discipline being asked of him was not hostility. It was clarity. Most operators lose to specific people whose interests are structurally tied to their failure, and most operators refuse to name those people because naming them feels like manufacturing a war. The war gets fought anyway, on the opponent's schedule, from a position the operator did not realize they were defending. The reversal matters: applied indiscriminately, this move turns into paranoia, and paranoia is exhausting and expensive. The work is not to find enemies. It is to stop pretending the ones who already exist are something else.

The second contest is harder to see, because the playbook killing you is the same playbook that built you. Devon's instincts at his first company had been correct for that company. Ship daily. Decide in the room. Sleep on the couch. The conditions had since changed in every direction: a workforce that did not consider couch-sleeping a virtue, a product surface that could no longer be debugged by the founder personally, a board that needed forecasts and not war stories. The instincts that had been correct were now driving away the people hired to do work he could no longer do himself. The hardest part of this contest is that the operator who needs it most is the one who was most successful at the previous version, because the same confidence that made the old playbook work is what makes the new conditions invisible. Some apparently new problems are old problems wearing new vocabulary, and the previous playbook applies. The judgment call is real. But the operator running it honestly is rare, and the operator running it from inside the success of the old playbook is rarer still.

The third contest is about a single moment. Imani was a senior counsel at a regional firm and the moment was 11:42 on a Wednesday night, mid-deal, when she sent an email she spent the next four hours wishing she had not sent. Nothing in it was factually wrong. It was just narrower than she would have written it at noon the next day, when the same situation looked clearly different. Under stress the mind narrows, and the operator who is angry or frightened or embarrassed loses access to the broader picture they would have had at any other hour. Imani built a personal practice in response. Any decision she wanted to execute under emotional load got written on a single page, by hand, and given to her chief of staff with explicit instruction not to send it until the next morning. The practice felt excessive to her colleagues for about six months. Then they watched her hold a position through a hostile board call that, three years earlier, would have flattened her. The practice itself is not the point. The point is that she has one and uses it. Some operators find theirs in physical exercise, some in deliberate slowness of speech, some in a trusted second opinion before any decision made under emotional load. The discipline is to never act from the narrow place, even when the narrow place feels like clarity.

The fourth contest is the one Greene takes most directly from Sun Tzu and the classical tradition: when comfort is making the operator slack, the move is to deliberately put themselves on death ground. Aiden was a researcher whose third book had been ninety percent finished for fourteen months. The problem was structural comfort. The partial manuscript was impressive enough that nobody was pressing him on the missing chapter, and his teaching schedule absorbed every remaining hour with work that felt productive and was not the chapter. He bought a non-refundable conference ticket, six weeks out, on which the talk title named the unfinished book, and he told two senior colleagues the date. The chapter existed by week five. The reversal matters more here than anywhere else in this material. This move is for the comfortable, not for the overwhelmed. Aimed at someone already at the edge, the same instrument produces collapse instead of delivery. A person who is drowning does not need a deeper end of the pool.

That last reversal is where the chapter's genuine difficulty lives. The third contest says: do not act from the narrow place. The fourth contest says: deliberately create a narrower place. They look like contradictory advice. They are not, because they apply to opposite states. The third move is for the operator caught in pressure they did not choose. The fourth move is for the operator drifting in comfort they should not be choosing. The discipline is the diagnostic step that comes before the move. Which state am I in, and what does it cost me if I guess wrong? The cost is not symmetric. Death ground applied to overwhelm collapses a person; presence of mind applied to drift extends the drift indefinitely. Most operators get this exactly backwards: they reach for deep breaths when they should be burning the boats, and they burn the boats when they should be putting the email down for twelve hours. The operator who can tell the difference is the operator the rest of this book is written for.

Everything that follows in this book happens through other people, against other people, or around other people. None of it works if the person running it has skipped the four contests above. The next chapter turns from the work the operator does inside their own head to the work they do through a team that did not necessarily do the same work. That is its own discipline. But it sits on this one.
