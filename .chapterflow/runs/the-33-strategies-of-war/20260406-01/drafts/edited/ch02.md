# Leading Through Other People

Naya was at her desk on a Friday evening, drafting a memo she had rewritten four times. The cursor sat in the middle of a sentence that was supposed to tell her six regional managers, plainly, that for the next quarter she would not be sending them tactical instructions. She would tell them the three operating goals for the quarter and meet with each of them for thirty minutes a week. The rest was theirs. She knew before she sent it that two of her senior managers would ask for the old instructions back inside a week, and she had decided in advance to hold the line. The harder thing she had decided was that, in one or two of the regions, the experiment would fail badly enough that she would have to either replace someone or rewrite the memo halfway through. She still hit send.

The first opponent in any conflict is the operator about to walk into the room. The second is the room itself, full of people the operator cannot replace and whose work she cannot do. Most of what an operator accomplishes in any large endeavor is accomplished through other people, and the moment she stops being the only person in the room, three different leadership disciplines begin. None of them is the same as the self-directed work of the previous chapter. Each places a specific trust in subordinates: trust in their judgment, trust in their autonomy, trust in their belief. And each fails in a specific way when the trust is misplaced. The work of leadership through other people is not the work of any one of those moves. It is the diagnostic that comes before any of them, the question of whether the people about to receive the trust are actually ready to carry it.

The first move is the structural one. A group of competent people, in a hierarchy that punishes dissent or rewards quiet compliance, will produce decisions worse than any one of them would have produced alone. The mechanism is not stupidity. The cost of speaking up is real and immediate. The cost of silence is invisible and deferred. So silence wins, the decisions get worse, and nobody in the room is exactly to blame because none of them did anything they would have called wrong. Naya's memo was an attempt to interrupt that mechanism by removing the part of her own job that had taught her regional managers to wait for instructions. She gave them the mission, not the method, and committed to thirty minutes a week. The two senior managers who asked for the old instructions back were not lazy and not weak. They were doing what the previous version of the job had trained them to do. The reversal matters here too. There are people on her team for whom mission-without-method is not leadership but abandonment: a new hire who has not yet seen a quarter, a manager taking over a region in crisis, a unit operating in unfamiliar territory. To those people, the right move is the older one. The skill is the diagnostic that tells the difference. The cost of getting it wrong is symmetrical: under-trust produces a brittle, micromanaged team, and over-trust produces a fragmented one.

The second move is structural in a different direction. Tomas had built a 90-person climate-software company organized as one engineering organization under one VP. By month thirty the structure was eating his calendar. Every cross-team decision routed through the same three meetings. Every customer segment had to fight for roadmap space against every other customer segment. The features that mattered most to the most-loved customers were the features that took longest to ship, because they were the ones that touched the most other parts of the system. He broke the organization into three pods of fifteen people, each owning one customer segment, each with its own product manager and engineering lead, each with the right to set its own quarterly roadmap. The VP of engineering quit in protest in the second month, told his exit interviewer that segmentation was just chaos with extra meetings, and was correct in the abstract and wrong about Tomas's actual company. By month nine, the three pods had shipped seven features that the prior monolithic structure would have spent eighteen months coordinating on.

The principle Tomas was using is older than software companies. A monolithic structure with one chain of command is brittle. A force broken into smaller, semi-autonomous units, each able to act on its own initiative within a shared purpose, is resilient. Smaller units move faster, take more risks, fail without bringing the whole down with them, and recombine as conditions change. The reversal is the same shape as the first one. Segmentation only works when each unit is genuinely capable. A segmented force of incompetents is a slower version of a centralized force of incompetents, with more meetings. Tomas's three pods worked because the pod leads had been with the company for years and had the context to set their own roadmaps. The same move applied to a company whose middle managers had not earned that level of trust would have produced three small disasters instead of one large one.

The third move is the most ethically loaded one in the chapter, and it is the one most easily turned into manipulation. Camila was twelve months into her job as executive director of a midsize advocacy nonprofit when she walked into her first all-staff meeting and told the room what she actually believed about the fight they had been losing for three years. She did not say "we are going to win this." She did not use the language of motivation. She told them, in specific terms, why she had come to this organization instead of the better-paid jobs she had turned down to come, and what she would resign over if it were taken away from her. The room went quieter than usual. Two senior staff who had been running the operation as a job, well, for a long time, left within six weeks. The eight who stayed produced the most effective campaign the organization had run in a decade. The reversal is the most important sentence in this material, and it is this: Camila's move worked because she meant it. A leader who uses the language of cause without believing the cause is detected by her team within a few months, and the cynicism that follows is worse than no crusade at all and harder to repair. Manufactured belief is not a softer version of belief. It is a poison that closes off the move for whoever follows.

What unites the three moves is not the moves themselves. It is the trust the leader is placing in the people receiving them. Mission-not-method trusts subordinates with judgment. Segmentation trusts them with autonomy. Cause trusts them with belief. The question that comes before any of these moves is whether the trust is earned by the people about to receive it. From inside, leadership and abdication can feel the same. Pushing initiative downward feels generous when the team can carry it, and feels generous in exactly the same way when the team cannot, and the leader who skips the diagnostic does not find out which case she is in until the costs are sunk. The next chapter turns from how to lead a team to which fights the team will be asked to spend itself on, and the discipline of choosing fights is its own work. But it sits on this one. A leader who has not learned how to extend trust well will spend her team on the wrong fights even when she has chosen the right fight to fight.
