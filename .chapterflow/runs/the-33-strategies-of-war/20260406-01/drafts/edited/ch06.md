# Speed, Initiative, and the Decisive Point

The incumbent's product team shipped a competitive response on a Tuesday. By Friday, Phoebe had already closed two pilots with accounts the incumbent had assumed were in their pipeline, and the distribution channel she locked three weeks earlier was running its first batch of integrations. When the blocking move reached the market, it was aimed at a position Phoebe had occupied six weeks ago. She had not moved faster because she had better technology or more money. She had moved faster because her decision cycle was a week and the incumbent's was a quarter, and a cycle time advantage of that magnitude means the attacker is perpetually fighting the previous war.

This is the principle behind Boyd's OODA loop, which appeared earlier in the context of managing your own decision-making under stress: observe, orient, decide, act. An opponent who cycles faster does not merely move first -- they move through several decisions before the slower side completes one response. By the time the response lands, it is targeting a position that no longer exists. The concept: operational tempo. Not the speed of a single move but the sustained rhythm of the cycle, compounded over an engagement, rendering the slower side's actions structurally obsolete. Phoebe's speed was not a talent. It was an organizational discipline: short decision windows, small approval chains, willingness to act on good-enough information rather than perfect information.

Speed multiplies direction. An operator moving fast in the wrong direction arrives at the wrong destination sooner, with more momentum than a slower operator, which makes correction harder. The practical sequence is: establish the long-horizon plan, identify the real opponent and their actual constraints, then deploy speed against the right target. Without those prior conditions, a faster cycle is a faster path to the wrong place.

The second discipline is distinct. Speed is about cycle rate. Initiative is about who is structuring the engagement. Kofi walked into a procurement negotiation and found that the counterparty had set the agenda, chosen the room, framed the first discussion item, and established the timeline. They had initiative. He could run faster than them without having it. The diagnostic the initiative discipline requires at every choice point is one question: who is setting the terms? If the answer is the opponent, the next move is not to respond faster -- it is to take the terms back. Introduce a new agenda item, shift the sequence, reframe the opening premise. Initiative and speed are independent. The ideal is both; many operators have one and mistake it for the other.

Declan had both, but what decided the campaign was neither. He was VP of product at a mid-size fintech challenging a dominant legacy compliance provider. The legacy system had better documentation, deeper expertise, more client relationships, and a larger sales team. Declan did not attack any of these. He had spent six weeks the previous year talking to the legacy provider's customers about their actual workflows, the same kind of intelligence investment described in the prior chapter. What he found: the provider's entire value proposition rested on one assumption no one had questioned because no one had needed to. Compliance reviews required human sign-off. Every piece of the provider's product, every relationship, every year of documentation, assumed that. Declan's platform automated the compliance layer. The assumption dissolved, and everything built on it dissolved with it.

The third discipline is finding the decisive point -- what military strategists call the center of gravity: the one node whose loss collapses everything built on it. It is almost never the most obvious target. The most obvious target is obvious because it is visible and defended. The center of gravity is usually invisible because it has never been threatened. The interface bug the legacy provider's customers complained about was a secondary target: take it and you have won one feature comparison. The compliance assumption was the center. Hit it, and the structural advantage of everything downstream fails. Attack secondary targets and the opponent rebuilds around their intact core.

The misidentification risk is the hardest part of this discipline. Operators routinely mistake three kinds of targets for centers of gravity: the most visible target, the most emotionally satisfying target, and the most heavily defended target. None are reliable proxies. Heavy defense often signals the opponent knows a target matters -- which hints the real center is elsewhere, in a place that has not yet warranted defense because it has not yet been attacked. Declan found the compliance assumption not by studying the provider's defenses but by working backward from customers' actual workflows until he found the dependency no one had questioned. That work is the intelligence discipline from the prior chapter. Speed and initiative are the execution layer. The decisive point is what the execution is aimed at. Without those prior conditions -- the long view and the intelligence investment -- speed accelerates in the wrong direction and the decisive point is a guess.

The three disciplines resolve into a sequence: speed means the opponent is always reacting; initiative means the operator chooses the terrain; the decisive point means the blow lands where it decides something. Together they produce engagements that are over before the opponent has oriented to what is happening. The next chapter's disciplines operate in a different register entirely -- no speed required, no decisive blow, because they work through indirection: the flank, the wedge, and the long maneuver that removes the conditions the opponent needs before they know removal has begun.
