# Speed, Initiative, and the Decisive Point

The incumbent's product team shipped a competitive response on a Tuesday. By Friday, Phoebe had already closed two pilots with accounts the incumbent had assumed were in their pipeline, and the distribution channel she locked three weeks earlier was running its first batch of integrations. When the incumbent's blocking move reached the market, it was aimed at a position Phoebe had occupied six weeks ago. She had not moved faster because she had better technology or more money. She had moved faster because her decision cycle was a week and the incumbent's was a quarter, and a cycle time advantage of that magnitude means the attacker is perpetually fighting the previous war.

This is the principle behind Boyd's OODA loop, which appeared earlier in the context of managing your own decision-making under stress: observe, orient, decide, act. An opponent who cycles faster does not merely move first -- they move through several decisions before the slower side has completed one response. By the time the response lands, it is targeting a position that no longer exists. The concept has a name: operational tempo. Not the speed of a single move but the sustained rhythm of the cycle, and the way that rhythm, compounded over an engagement, renders the slower side's actions structurally obsolete. Phoebe's speed was not a talent. It was an organizational discipline: short decision windows, small approval chains, willingness to act on good-enough information rather than perfect information.

Before the reversal disappears, it is worth naming. Speed multiplies direction. An operator who moves fast in the wrong direction arrives at the wrong destination sooner, and with more momentum than a slower operator would have built up, which makes correction harder. Speed is a force multiplier; it amplifies both good decisions and bad ones. The practical sequence is not: develop speed, then figure out direction. It is: establish the long-horizon plan (as in the last chapter), identify the real opponent and their actual constraints, then deploy speed against the right target. Without the prior conditions, a faster cycle is a faster path to the wrong place.

The second discipline is different from the first. Speed is about cycle rate -- how fast you complete the loop. Initiative is about who is structuring the engagement. Kofi walked into a procurement negotiation and found that the counterparty had set the agenda, chosen the room, framed the first discussion item, and established the timeline. They had initiative. He could be faster than them without having it. The diagnostic question the initiative discipline requires at every choice point is a single one: who is setting the terms here? If the answer is the opponent, the next move is not to respond faster. It is to take the terms back -- introduce a new agenda item, shift the sequence, reframe the opening premise, establish a different timeline. Initiative and speed are independent. An operator can have one without the other. The ideal is both.

Declan had both, but what he used to win was neither. He was VP of product at a mid-size fintech challenging a dominant legacy compliance provider. The legacy system had better documentation, deeper expertise, more client relationships, and a larger sales team. Declan did not attack any of these. He had spent six weeks the previous year talking to the legacy provider's customers about their actual workflows -- the same kind of research described in the last chapter, applied here to locating a specific target rather than just understanding an opponent. What he found: the provider's entire value proposition rested on a single assumption that no one had questioned because no one had needed to. Compliance reviews required human sign-off. Every piece of the provider's product, every relationship they had built, every year of documentation they had accumulated, assumed that this was a permanent feature of the regulatory environment. Declan's platform automated the compliance layer. The assumption dissolved, and with it, the structural advantage of everything built on it.

This is the third discipline: the decisive point, or what military strategists call the center of gravity -- the one node in an opponent's position whose loss collapses everything built on it. It is almost never the most obvious target. The most obvious target is obvious because it is visible and defended. The center of gravity is usually invisible because it has never been threatened. The well-documented interface bug the legacy provider's customers complained about was a secondary target: take it and you have won one feature comparison, not a structural advantage. The compliance assumption was the center. When the attacker identifies and hits the true center, the result is disproportionate -- everything downstream fails. When the attacker goes for secondary targets, the result is proportional: you have taken ground, but the opponent's core position remains intact and they will simply rebuild around it.

The misidentification risk is the hardest part of the third discipline. Operators routinely mistake three kinds of targets for centers of gravity: the most visible target, the most emotionally satisfying target, and the most heavily defended target. None of these are reliable proxies for the center. In fact, heavy defense is often a signal that the opponent knows a target matters -- which is a hint that the real center is elsewhere, in a place that has not yet warranted defense because it has not yet been attacked. Declan did not find the compliance assumption by looking at the provider's defenses. He found it by working backward from the provider's customers' workflows, from the bottom up, until he found the dependency that none of them had ever questioned. That work is the intelligence strategy from the prior chapter. Speed and initiative are the execution layer. The decisive point is where that execution is aimed. Without the prior conditions -- the long view that tells you which fights to enter, and the intelligence work that tells you what to aim at -- speed accelerates in the wrong direction and the decisive point is a guess.

All three disciplines resolve into one coherent offensive sequence. Speed means the opponent is always reacting, never setting. Initiative means the operator chooses the terrain. The decisive point means the blow lands where it actually decides something. The combination produces engagements that are over before the opponent has oriented to what is happening. The next chapter's disciplines are entirely different in register -- they do not require speed, initiative, or a single decisive blow, because they operate through indirection: the flank, the wedge, and the long maneuver that removes the conditions the opponent needs before they know removal has begun.
