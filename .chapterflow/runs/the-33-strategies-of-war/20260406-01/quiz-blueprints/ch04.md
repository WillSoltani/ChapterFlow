## Q01 — Bloom: remember | Depth: easy | correctIndex target: 2

**Prompt:** The chapter's central claim about the counterattack is that the best moment to counter is when?

**Choices:**
A. The moment the opponent signals they are about to attack, so you can preempt.
B. Immediately after the initial hit lands, before the opponent can regroup.
C. After the opponent has fully committed and extended, when they have no recovery position.

**Key:** C. Maximum extension is the window. A is preemptive, not counterattack. B is the too-early failure mode the chapter names.

## Q02 — Bloom: remember | Depth: easy | correctIndex target: 0

**Prompt:** Why does the chapter say deterrence is not bluff?

**Choices:**
A. Because a reputation without real capacity behind it collapses the first time it is tested, and cannot be rebuilt with the same audience.
B. Because bluffing is unethical and deterrence must be honest.
C. Because bluffs can work in the short term but deterrence only works over long periods.

**Key:** A. The structural argument: bluff collapses under test, and the collapse is permanent with that audience. B is a category error; C is vague and not the chapter's claim.

## Q03 — Bloom: understand | Depth: easy | correctIndex target: 1

**Prompt:** The "absorption window" is best described as which of the following?

**Choices:**
A. The delay caused by an operator who is too cautious to counter-attack at all.
B. The period during which the operator takes damage from an attack while reading its shape — a necessary precondition of the well-timed counter.
C. The time between deciding to counter-attack and executing the counter-attack.

**Key:** B. The absorption window is active (reading the shape), not passive (just suffering). A mischaracterizes it as hesitation; C describes the execution gap, not the observation period.

## Q04 — Bloom: understand | Depth: easy | correctIndex target: 0

**Prompt:** What is "deterrence capital" composed of?

**Choices:**
A. The accumulated pattern of past behavior that is visible to the audiences deciding whether to attack — built by actually following through at cost, not by claiming you will.
B. The financial and legal resources available to mount a deterrent threat.
C. The formal statements and documented positions that define what actions you will take in response to various attacks.

**Key:** A. Behavioral pattern + visibility + following through at cost. B mistakes resources for the capital itself. C describes posturing, not the underlying substance.

## Q05 — Bloom: apply | Depth: medium | correctIndex target: 2

**Prompt:** Dara absorbs a public attack at the all-hands and waits six weeks before countering. Which of the following best explains why timing was more important than the evidence itself?

**Choices:**
A. Waiting gave her time to gather more evidence than she had at the all-hands.
B. The board would not have believed her at the all-hands because she was too junior.
C. By the time she countered, the VP Sales had overcommitted to a narrative he could not walk back — the same evidence that would have been a defense in week one was a counter against a locked position in week six.

**Key:** C. The opponent's overcommitment is what the timing operates on. A is false (she had the evidence from the start). B introduces a hierarchy claim the chapter does not make.

## Q06 — Bloom: apply | Depth: medium | correctIndex target: 1

**Prompt:** A startup founder has been warned by three investors that a larger competitor is planning to enter her market. She is preparing a response. Which question does the chapter's diagnostic say she should answer first?

**Choices:**
A. How quickly can she fortify her existing customer base before the competitor arrives.
B. Has the competitor actually moved yet, or are they still deciding — because the available disciplines are different depending on the answer.
C. Whether she has the resources to mount a counterattack if the competitor enters directly.

**Key:** B. The diagnostic question (moved or deciding?) determines which discipline applies. A and C are both tactics inside an already-chosen response; the chapter says the prior question is which response to choose.

## Q07 — Bloom: analyze | Depth: medium | correctIndex target: 0

**Prompt:** Petra's deterrence reputation collapsed in a single negotiation call. What structural property of deterrence does her failure illustrate?

**Choices:**
A. Deterrence capital is asymmetrically fragile: years of building can be eliminated by one tested-and-failed bluff, because the audience now has direct evidence the deterrent is not real.
B. Deterrence requires periodic public displays of willingness to fight, and Petra had not maintained enough visible confrontations to keep her reputation credible.
C. The counterparty in Petra's negotiation was unusually sophisticated and would have seen through any deterrence posture.

**Key:** A. The asymmetric fragility: the collapse is not proportional to the build — one failure with the right audience eliminates the credible-threat dimension. B invents a maintenance requirement about "visible confrontations" the chapter doesn't specify. C is a counterparty explanation that removes the structural lesson.

## Q08 — Bloom: analyze | Depth: medium | correctIndex target: 2

**Prompt:** An executive receives a public written criticism of her work from a peer in a company all-staff channel. She wants to counter. Which of the following is the failure mode the chapter warns against?

**Choices:**
A. Responding in the same channel the attack happened in, rather than escalating to a more formal forum.
B. Involving her manager in the counter without giving the peer a chance to respond privately first.
C. Countering immediately, before the peer has fully committed to the position, when the peer could still retreat — making the counter land on a position that was never truly extended.

**Key:** C. The too-early failure mode: counter before maximum commitment and the opponent can still retreat, neutralizing the counter. A and B introduce response-format questions the chapter doesn't address.

## Q09 — Bloom: evaluate | Depth: hard | correctIndex target: 1

**Prompt:** A mid-level manager has decided to wait for a counter-attack opportunity against a peer who has been undermining her in meetings. She has been waiting eight months. The peer continues to undermine her, but never with a single overcommitted move — the damage is gradual and distributed. Which of the following applies, and why?

**Choices:**
A. She should continue waiting; the chapter says the counter-attack window always arrives eventually.
B. The counter-attack strategy is parasitic on the opponent making an exposed, committed move. Against an opponent running an attrition strategy — distributed, gradual, never fully committed — the counter-attack opening may never arrive, and waiting for it is losing by erosion. A different discipline is required.
C. She should force the opponent into a committed move by escalating the confrontation, which will create the opening she is waiting for.

**Key:** B. The reversal: counter-attack requires the opponent to extend, and attrition opponents deliberately refuse to extend. A denies the reversal; C is an escalation move the chapter doesn't endorse and which may accelerate the erosion.

## Q10 — Bloom: evaluate | Depth: hard | correctIndex target: 0

**Prompt:** Connecting to Chapter 3: how does the deterrence discipline relate to the strategic depth condition that nonengagement requires?

**Choices:**
A. Both require real underlying capacity — deterrence needs genuine executable threat capacity, and nonengagement needs actual ground to trade. An operator with no real capacity can neither deter credibly nor nonengagement without losing everything. The same structural condition (real reserves) is what makes both available.
B. They are unrelated disciplines from different chapters — deterrence is offensive in posture and nonengagement is defensive, so they apply to entirely different conditions and have no common foundation.
C. Deterrence makes nonengagement unnecessary because a credible deterrent prevents the attack that would have forced the nonengagement in the first place.

**Key:** A. The connecting insight: real underlying capacity is the structural prerequisite for both. B mischaracterizes deterrence as offensive (it is defensive/consolidating). C is a clever-sounding false synthesis — deterrence and nonengagement address different opponent states (one deciding vs. one already engaging), and either might fail if the capacity underneath is absent.
