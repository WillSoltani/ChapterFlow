## Q01 -- Remember/Understand | Easy | correctIndex: 1
**Concept:** Legitimate use of annihilation
**Prompt:** When does the chapter treat annihilation as strategically legitimate?
**Choices:**
  0: Whenever the operator has enough anger to justify making an example of the opponent.
  1: When decisive advantage exists, repeated harm must be stopped, and leaving exits open would predictably reproduce the damage.
  2: Only when the opponent has already apologized and lost public support.
**Key:** 1 -- The chapter treats annihilation as an edge-case containment move, not a routine expression of dominance.
**Bloom:** remember/understand
**Depth:** easy

## Q04 -- Apply/Analyze | Medium | correctIndex: 1
**Concept:** One-upmanship timing
**Prompt:** A rival executive is already alienating the board through their own erratic behavior. Your attack would likely unify their allies behind them. What does the chapter recommend?
**Choices:**
  0: Escalate immediately so the board sees strength.
  1: Consider one-upmanship: avoid rescuing the rival with a premature attack and let their contradictions keep doing the work.

## Q09 -- Evaluate | Hard | correctIndex: 2
**Concept:** Strategic reason to put the framework down
**Prompt:** Why does the chapter argue that putting the framework down can be the strongest strategic move, not only the nicest one?
**Choices:**
  0: Because direct honesty always guarantees better results
  1: Because conflict disappears whenever people decide to trust one another
  2: Because some relationships and situations depend on trust, care, or shared problem-solving, and treating them as battlefields destroys the very conditions the operator needs
**Key:** 2 -- The no-framework rule is structural: applying war logic in the wrong setting degrades the thing you are trying to preserve.
**Bloom:** evaluate
**Depth:** hard
