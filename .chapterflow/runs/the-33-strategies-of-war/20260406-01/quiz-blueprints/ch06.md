**Prompt:** An operator cycles through observe-orient-decide-act faster than her opponent can process a single response. What strategic advantage does this create?
**Choices:**
  0: The opponent tires faster from trying to keep pace.
  1: The opponent's actions always target a position the operator has already left.
  2: The operator accumulates more information per unit of time.
**Key:** 1 — The core mechanism: faster loop means the opponent is perpetually out of sync.

## Q04 — Apply/Analyze | Medium | correctIndex: 0
**Concept:** Speed vs. initiative distinction
**Prompt:** Amara runs a product team that ships weekly. Her counterpart at a rival firm ships quarterly but controls the procurement process at every major account they share. Which operator has speed, which has initiative, and what does Amara need to recognize?
**Choices:**
  0: Amara has speed; her counterpart has initiative. Speed without initiative means cycling fast within terrain the opponent has already structured.
  1: Amara has both speed and initiative; shipping faster automatically sets the terms of competitive engagement.
  2: Amara's counterpart has speed because controlling procurement effectively means faster access to customers.
**Key:** 0 — Speed and initiative are independent. Cycling fast in the opponent's terrain is a real limitation.
**Bloom:** apply/analyze
**Depth:** medium

## Q05 — Apply/Analyze | Medium | correctIndex: 1
**Concept:** Initiative — the diagnostic question
**Prompt:** Kofi is preparing for a negotiation. He realizes the counterparty has already set the agenda, chosen the meeting location, and framed the first item for discussion. According to the initiative discipline, what is Kofi's most useful next move?
**Choices:**
  0: Accept the existing frame and respond within it, since the counterparty having initiative at the start is normal and expected.
  1: Introduce a new item before the agenda is confirmed, propose a sequence shift, or reframe the first item, to take back at least one of the terms.
  2: Request a delay until he can do more research on the counterparty's actual interests.
**Key:** 1 — The initiative discipline says: at every choice point, if the opponent is setting the terms, the next move is to take them back.
**Bloom:** apply/analyze
**Depth:** medium

## Q06 — Apply/Analyze | Medium | correctIndex: 2
**Concept:** Identifying center of gravity vs. secondary targets
**Prompt:** Declan is analyzing a legacy competitor. The competitor's most-discussed weakness is their outdated interface, which customers complain about. Declan spends six weeks interviewing customers and finds that the interface complaints are real but that the competitor's real structural vulnerability is their assumption that compliance requires human sign-off. What does this finding suggest about where Declan should focus?
**Choices:**
  0: The interface, because it is the most complained-about issue and will produce the most visible win with customers.
  1: Both equally, since addressing the most visible weakness signals responsiveness while the structural attack proceeds.
  2: The compliance assumption, because it is the center of gravity — attacking it collapses the competitor's entire value proposition, not just one feature.
**Key:** 2 — Interface is a secondary target. The compliance assumption is the center of gravity.
**Bloom:** apply/analyze
**Depth:** medium

## Q07 — Apply/Analyze | Medium | correctIndex: 1
**Concept:** Speed requires prior conditions (Ch5 connection)
**Prompt:** A startup founder moves very fast — weekly pivots, instant responses to market signals. She has no long-horizon strategy and has not researched her main competitors' actual constraints. What is the most accurate diagnosis of her situation?
**Choices:**

  2: She needs to slow down to develop strategy before using speed as a tool.
**Key:** 1 — Speed multiplies direction, good or bad. The prior conditions determine whether speed is an advantage or an accelerant of bad decisions.

## Q08 — Apply/Analyze | Medium | correctIndex: 0
**Concept:** Decisive point — misidentification risk
**Prompt:** Idris is competing in a math olympiad and concludes that his opponent's weakness is arithmetic speed. He structures his approach to be faster on calculation. His opponent wins by finding a conceptual shortcut that bypasses most arithmetic entirely. What did Idris get wrong?
**Choices:**
  0: He attacked a secondary target. Arithmetic speed was a real dimension but not the one that decided the competition. The opponent's center of gravity was the capacity to reframe the problem.
  1: He used the right strategy but with insufficient commitment; a faster calculator would have closed the gap.
  2: His opponent had both speed and the shortcut; Idris needed both too, not just one.
**Key:** 0 — Classic secondary-target error. The real decisive point was problem-framing capacity, not calculation rate.
**Bloom:** apply/analyze
**Depth:** medium

## Q09 — Evaluate | Hard | correctIndex: 2
**Concept:** Speed without initiative — a combined diagnosis
**Prompt:** An organization is known for extremely fast execution. They ship faster than anyone in their market. But they consistently find themselves responding to competitor moves rather than creating them — they ship fast responses to the features competitors release. A senior strategist argues that their speed is a competitive liability, not an asset. Is the strategist right, and why?
**Choices:**
  0: The strategist is wrong. Shipping fast responses to competitor moves is a valid use of speed; what matters is the quality of execution, not who moves first.
  1: The strategist is partially right: speed is neutral and only becomes a liability if the organization lacks quality control.
  2: The strategist is right. Speed without initiative means cycling fast within terrain the opponent has structured. The organization is operating the speed discipline while the opponent is operating the initiative discipline. The opponent is setting the terms; the organization is executing fast within those terms.
**Key:** 2 — Speed + reactive position = fast follower. Initiative is the missing discipline.
**Bloom:** evaluate
**Depth:** hard

## Q10 — Evaluate | Hard | correctIndex: 1
**Concept:** Center of gravity misidentification and the intelligence prerequisite
**Prompt:** Wren has identified what she believes is the center of gravity in a competitive situation: the lead partner at a rival firm whose reputation underpins the firm's client relationships. She has not done extensive research into the rival firm's internal structure or client base. She makes a move targeting the partner directly. The partner leaves, but the firm's client relationships hold, and a replacement partner takes over smoothly. What does this outcome reveal, and what should Wren have done differently?
**Choices:**
  0: The move was correct but poorly executed; the partner needed to be targeted more decisively to prevent orderly succession.
  1: The partner was a secondary target, not the center of gravity. The real center was something in the firm's structure that the partner's reputation masked — probably a systematic relationship architecture or institutional process. The intelligence work of Ch5 was the missing prerequisite.
  2: No center of gravity was available; some competitive positions are genuinely resilient and the right move was to compete directly on quality.
**Key:** 1 — Misidentification is the classic center-of-gravity error. The intelligence prerequisite would have revealed the structural dependency, not the visible face.
**Bloom:** evaluate
**Depth:** hard
