# Chapter Quiz Blueprint — Chapter 3

Chapter: Choosing Not to Fight

Core concepts to test:
- concept 1: Resources are finite; the operator who fights every fight arrives at the one that matters with nothing left.
- concept 2: The two-question test (what does winning get me; what does losing cost me) is the operational form of perfect economy.
- concept 3: Nonengagement is the deliberate trade of space for time when the opponent is stronger.
- concept 4: Nonengagement requires strategic depth.
- concept 5: Some declines are read as weakness by audiences whose opinion matters.
- concept 6: The two disciplines apply to opposite conditions, and confusing them is the central failure mode.

Bloom ceiling: evaluate (q09–q10).

Question plan:
- q01: recall — the chapter claims that two of the most important moves are about what?
- q02: anchor — Junichi's two-column sheet teaches which discipline?
- q03: term — perfect economy of force is best characterized as which kind of discipline?
- q04: scenario application — a PM weighing whether to fight a low-stakes process change should ask which two questions?
- q05: distinction — Aanya's pivot from espresso to pour-over is which move?
- q06: limit — under what condition does nonengagement fail?
- q07: transfer — a graduate student deciding whether to challenge a senior professor in print should weigh which reversal?
- q08: judgment — given an operator who has been declining all fights for six months and feels unusually free, which failure may be in progress?
- q09: synthesis — why are perfect economy and nonengagement not two versions of the same instinct?
- q10: cross-chapter — connect to Ch1: how does the death-ground move relate to the perfect-economy move (deliberately removing options vs. deliberately preserving them)?

Distractor notes:
- Tempting wrong answer type 1: pacifism/boundaries misread.
- Tempting wrong answer type 2: collapsing both disciplines into "avoid conflict".
- Tempting wrong answer type 3: ignoring the strategic-depth condition for nonengagement.
- Tempting wrong answer type 4: treating the two-question test as universal rather than as the operational form of one specific move.

Explanation diversity notes: same as Ch1/Ch2 — no repeated openers; no canned phrases.
