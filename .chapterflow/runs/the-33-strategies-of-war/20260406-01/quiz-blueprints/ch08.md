## Q01 -- Remember/Understand | Easy | correctIndex: 1
**Concept:** Why paired pressure and exit work together
**Prompt:** Why does keeping a genuine settlement channel open while applying pressure make the opponent more likely to settle?
**Choices:**
  0: The opponent becomes confused by receiving contradictory signals.
  1: The opponent faces both the escalating cost of resistance and a visible, face-saving path to ending that cost.
  2: The opponent assumes the pressure is not serious because a settlement offer is on the table.
**Key:** 1 -- The pairing works because the exit gives the pressure somewhere to go. Without the exit, pressure produces desperation, not movement.
**Bloom:** remember/understand
**Depth:** easy

## Q02 -- Remember/Understand | Easy | correctIndex: 0
**Concept:** Why pre-committed exit conditions matter
**Prompt:** Why does the chapter argue that exit conditions should be defined before the campaign begins?
**Choices:**
  0: Because in the moment, sunk-cost psychology and personal investment overwhelm rational assessment and make stopping feel impossible.
  1: Because defining conditions early impresses stakeholders and increases the project's political support.
  2: Because early conditions guarantee the project will not fail if the exit is triggered.
**Key:** 0 -- The pre-commitment exists to protect the decision from the psychology of the moment, not to impress observers.
**Bloom:** remember/understand

## Q03 -- Remember/Understand | Easy | correctIndex: 2
**Concept:** What the face-saving exit actually does
**Prompt:** Why does the chapter call the face-saving exit "load-bearing infrastructure" rather than a courtesy?
**Choices:**
  0: Because it makes the operator look generous to third-party observers.
  1: Because it forces the opponent to accept terms immediately without negotiation.
  2: Because an opponent who sees no path to settlement that preserves some dignity will fight to the end regardless of cost, eliminating the leverage the pressure was supposed to create.
**Key:** 2 -- The exit is structural. Without it, pressure produces desperation rather than movement toward settlement.
**Bloom:** remember/understand
**Depth:** easy

## Q04 -- Apply/Analyze | Medium | correctIndex: 1
**Concept:** Identifying a fake exit
**Prompt:** An executive applies legal pressure on a competitor while offering settlement terms that are deliberately unacceptable -- designed to look like an offer while being impossible to take. The competitor discovers this. What is the most likely outcome?
**Choices:**
  0: The competitor settles anyway because the legal pressure is real and the terms do not matter.
  1: The competitor fights to the end because the fake exit eliminated the leverage the strategy depended on, and the operator's credibility for future offers is damaged.
  2: The competitor ignores both the pressure and the offer and waits for a third party to intervene.
**Key:** 1 -- The fake exit is the classic reversal. Once the opponent discovers it, the strategy collapses into the direct confrontation the operator structured to avoid.
**Bloom:** apply/analyze
**Depth:** medium

## Q05 -- Apply/Analyze | Medium | correctIndex: 0
**Concept:** Sunk-cost psychology at the exit point
**Prompt:** Vaughn set a cost-overrun threshold of 40% as an exit condition for a platform migration. The project has reached 42% overrun, but his team argues that they are close to a breakthrough and stopping now would waste everything invested. What does the chapter suggest Vaughn should do?
**Choices:**
  0: Honor the pre-committed condition, because the psychology of continued investment is exactly what the pre-commitment was designed to override.
  1: Continue the project, because the team's proximity to a breakthrough changes the original conditions.
  2: Renegotiate the threshold upward so the project is no longer technically over the limit.
**Key:** 0 -- The pre-commitment exists because the moment always feels like the wrong time to stop. Honoring it is the discipline.
**Bloom:** apply/analyze
**Depth:** medium

## Q06 -- Apply/Analyze | Medium | correctIndex: 2
**Concept:** Public credibility damage from broken exit conditions
**Prompt:** A founder publicly announces that she will shut down a product line if it does not reach profitability by Q3. Q3 arrives, the product is not profitable, and she continues it anyway, citing "emerging opportunities." What is the strategic cost the chapter identifies?
**Choices:**
  0: The product line will now certainly fail because the deadline has passed.
  1: The team loses motivation because they see the founder as indecisive.
  2: Every future opponent and observer learns that her publicly stated limits are not real limits, structurally reducing her credibility in all subsequent negotiations.
**Key:** 2 -- The credibility cost is not a one-time event. It is a structural reduction in the operator's capacity to make credible commitments.
**Bloom:** apply/analyze
**Depth:** medium

## Q07 -- Apply/Analyze | Medium | correctIndex: 1
**Concept:** Paired pressure and sincere exit in practice
**Prompt:** Hana is escalating legal pressure on a competitor's patent claim. At the same time, she offers specific settlement terms that include face-saving concessions for the competitor. Why does the chapter argue this pairing is more effective than pressure alone?
**Choices:**
  0: Because the settlement offer distracts the competitor from the legal threat.
  1: Because the competitor now faces both the cost of continued resistance and a visible alternative that ends the cost on tolerable terms, making settlement attractive rather than humiliating.
  2: Because the settlement offer signals weakness, which causes the competitor to lower their guard.
**Key:** 1 -- The pairing works because the exit provides a destination for the pressure. Pressure without an exit produces desperation; pressure with a credible exit produces movement.
**Bloom:** apply/analyze
**Depth:** medium

## Q08 -- Apply/Analyze | Medium | correctIndex: 0
**Concept:** Exit discipline vs. quitting
**Prompt:** A colleague tells Vaughn that honoring his exit conditions is "just quitting dressed up as strategy." How does the chapter distinguish the exit discipline from quitting?
**Choices:**
  0: The exit discipline is a pre-committed decision that serves a larger strategic plan, while quitting is an unplanned abandonment driven by frustration or exhaustion.

**Key:** 0 -- The distinction is structural. Exit is pre-committed, planned, and serves the long view. Quitting is reactive. The grand strategy (Ch5) gives meaning to the exit.

## Q09 -- Evaluate | Hard | correctIndex: 2
**Concept:** The boundary condition -- when persistence past exit conditions is correct
**Prompt:** Vaughn's pre-committed exit condition has been triggered, but since setting it, a major competitor has exited the market, fundamentally changing the economics. Should he honor the original exit condition?
**Choices:**
  0: Yes, always. Pre-committed conditions must be honored regardless of changed circumstances, because any exception opens the door to rationalization.
  1: No. Changed circumstances automatically invalidate pre-committed conditions, so he should continue without further analysis.
  2: It depends on whether the change is genuine and material or whether it is the sunk-cost psychology producing a convenient reason to continue. The discipline is to distinguish between a truly changed situation and the rationalization that makes every exit feel premature.
**Key:** 2 -- The chapter names this boundary condition explicitly. The difficulty is making the judgment inside the very psychology that biases toward continuation. Neither rigid adherence nor automatic override is correct.
**Bloom:** evaluate
**Depth:** hard

## Q10 -- Evaluate | Hard | correctIndex: 1
**Concept:** Credibility as structural infrastructure
**Prompt:** An operator has built a reputation over years for honoring stated commitments. She now faces a situation where breaking her stated exit condition would produce a large short-term gain. What does the chapter's analysis suggest?
**Choices:**
  0: The short-term gain justifies the exception because reputations can be rebuilt quickly.
  1: The credibility accumulated over years is structural infrastructure that enables all future strategies. Breaking it for a single gain trades a durable asset for a temporary one, and the loss is not recoverable at the same cost.
  2: Reputations do not matter in strategic contexts because opponents make decisions based on immediate incentives, not past behavior.
**Key:** 1 -- The chapter treats credibility as load-bearing strategic infrastructure, not a soft virtue. Once damaged, the cost of rebuilding exceeds the gain from breaking it.
**Bloom:** evaluate
**Depth:** hard
