# Forcing Strategies: Control the Dynamic

Selene's counterpart arrived ready to argue price. Instead it found a room already arranged around Selene's sequence: agenda circulated the night before, decision points numbered, supporting documents attached in the order she wanted discussed, and the first question framed around delivery risk instead of cost. Before anyone raised their voice, the meeting was already happening on her terrain.

Greene's forcing chapter is about that kind of initiative. The skilled operator does not wait to see what the other side does and then respond beautifully. She shapes the engagement so the other side is responding inside conditions she set first. Time, place, terms, pace, and order become tools. The real question is not only who has the stronger argument or the faster move. It is who is defining what counts as the argument, what gets discussed first, what gets delayed, and what kind of response is even possible.

That is why control of the dynamic is subtler than aggression. Many people hear forcing and imagine pressure, domination, or visible intensity. Greene's point is sharper. Initiative often looks like design. It is the agenda sent before the meeting. It is the frame chosen before the debate begins. It is the sequence that makes the other side answer your priorities before it can introduce its own. A strong operator keeps asking a simple question: who is setting the terms here? If the answer is the opponent, the next move is to take those terms back.

Selene does that by refusing to let the negotiation begin at the counterparty's preferred entry point. If the room starts with price, the discussion narrows around concession. If it starts with delivery risk, implementation burden, and timing, the whole structure changes. She is not winning because she is louder. She is winning because the other side must react within an order that already favors her purpose. The forcing strategy works when initiative reduces the opponent's options without theatrics. It fails when control becomes so obvious or so self-serving that resistance hardens before the real discussion begins.

Keaton runs into the same lesson in an undergraduate urban planning studio. A rival team keeps dominating critiques by speaking first and scattering the room's attention across side issues. Keaton's group regains ground only when it structures the review before it starts: site logic first, pedestrian flow second, budget assumptions third, then open critique. Once that sequence is established, the discussion stops drifting into the rival team's preferred distractions. Keaton learns that initiative is not about occupying the most airtime. It is about deciding what the room is deciding and in what order the evidence will matter.

The reversal matters because forcing strategy can curdle fast. Initiative held without a goal becomes random aggression. People start controlling meetings, imposing sequence, or grabbing the first move simply to feel dominant. That is not strategy. It is ego wearing a sharper suit. A bad frame imposed early is still a bad frame. A manipulative agenda may win a moment and poison the relationship that had to carry the result. Greene's serious limit is that the terms you set must serve the larger objective. If they do not, control of the dynamic becomes noise with authority attached.

That makes Chapter 15 the initiative chapter of offensive warfare. Chapter 14 showed how speed can keep an opponent late. Chapter 15 asks who controls the field before those exchanges even start. Once the dynamic is yours, the next problem is narrower and harsher: where is the single point whose loss weakens everything else? Chapter 16 moves there next.
