# The Counterattack Strategy: Turn the Tables

Mara watched the accusation spread for six hours before she answered it. A rival on the funding-review committee had posted a polished thread implying that her team hid cost overruns until the last minute. Her first instinct was speed. Answer now, deny everything, and prove she was not rattled. Instead she kept collecting screenshots, forwarding messages, and watching which version of the claim people were repeating. By late afternoon the rival had stretched the accusation so far that it now included a timeline Mara could disprove line by line.

Greene's point begins there. An attack is not only a threat. It is also a disclosure. The opponent who moves first has committed energy, exposed intention, and often shown where their confidence outruns their evidence. They are leaning forward. The counterattack works because it lands in that lean. Instead of treating the first blow as a command to respond instantly, the disciplined operator absorbs it long enough to read the shape of the move and see what opening the attack itself has created.

That requires timing discipline, which is the real skill of the chapter. Counter too early and the opponent has not fully committed. They can soften, revise, or retreat before paying much for the move. Counter too late and the opening closes because they have regained balance, reframed the exchange, or made your silence look like surrender. Greene's demand is emotional control under pressure. You have to survive the first hit without panicking into haste, but you also have to resist turning patience into drift.

Mara's return works because she waits for overcommitment, not for comfort. Once the rival's claim expands beyond what the record can support, she answers with the audit trail, the meeting notes, and the timestamp showing that the committee had been warned earlier than the rival admitted. The counterattack is stronger because it is built from the rival's own extension. She is not merely defending herself. She is showing that the attacker's confidence depended on exaggeration. That shift changes the room. The rival now looks reckless instead of forceful, and Mara's restraint reads as control instead of weakness.

Quentin sees the same structure in a graduate public-interest clinic. The opposing team opens with a sweeping procedural objection that sounds dangerous because it arrives first and loudly. Quentin wants to answer immediately, but the objection keeps expanding as the other side keeps talking. By the time the faculty panel starts asking questions, the objection now claims defects broad enough to undermine the other side's own filing standard. Quentin uses that breadth against them. He does not swat at the first version. He lets the objection extend until its overreach becomes visible, then counters on the contradiction it created.

The reversal matters because counterattack is parasitic on being attacked. It needs the other side to commit first. That means the strategy fails when the opponent is not making one sharp move but is instead grinding slowly through attrition, delay, leakage, or small repeated pressure. In that kind of campaign, waiting forever for a dramatic overextension can become its own mistake. Greene's chapter is disciplined, not romantic. The operator must judge whether the incoming attack is ripening into a real opening or whether patience is simply allowing the loss to spread across time.

That makes Chapter 9 the harder twin of Chapter 8. Chapter 8 taught the discipline of refusing weak fights. Chapter 9 asks what to do once the strike has already landed and revealed its shape. Sometimes the best answer is not immediate defense and not passive endurance, but a timed reversal built from the attacker's own imbalance. Chapter 10 then shifts the frame again by asking how to make that first attack feel so costly that the opponent thinks twice before starting it.
