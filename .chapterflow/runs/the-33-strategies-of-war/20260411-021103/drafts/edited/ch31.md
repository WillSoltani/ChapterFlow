# The Inner-Front Strategy: Destroy from Within

Sorin kept assuming the rival bloc needed one more hard push to crack. Each time he applied it, the opposite happened. People who distrusted one another closed ranks, postponed their disagreements, and acted like a unified front for just long enough to absorb the attack. The problem was not that the bloc lacked fractures. The problem was that his pressure kept giving those fractures a common enemy.

That is the entrance into Greene's inner-front strategy. Some opponents are hardest to weaken from the outside because outside pressure creates forced cohesion. They can be weakened only when their own contradictions are allowed to pull against one another without being repaired by your attack. The operator's first task is diagnosis: where are the real fault lines, which pressures deepen them, and which interventions accidentally heal them?

The chapter matters because not every divided system is vulnerable in the same way. A group can be split by incentives, timing, distrust, ego, or incompatible goals, yet still rally when threatened. Sorin changes course once he sees that pattern. He stops feeding the bloc a clean external threat and instead lets its internal strains stay visible. The result is not dramatic. It is slower and less theatrical. But the rival bloc becomes less able to hide its contradictions behind defensive unity.

That is the fair reading of the strategy. Sometimes the honest move is to stop rescuing a system from conflicts already living inside it. Sometimes surfacing real contradictions is more truthful than suppressing them. But the chapter sits close to subversion, which is why it needs an explicit limit. Working with what is already there is one thing. Manufacturing rot is another.

Anwen names that break in a graduate organizational psychology seminar. Classmates talk as if any internal fracture were usable if it created advantage. She corrects them. If the contradiction is real and the target is a genuine opponent, exposing that contradiction can be strategically intelligible. If the operator starts planting false suspicions, feeding lies, or turning ordinary disagreement into engineered mistrust, the move has changed character. The strategy is no longer diagnostic. It is corrosive.

The reversal matters because internal-fracture tactics can spread damage beyond the intended target. Distrust is not a clean instrument. Once seeded carelessly, it can outlive the conflict and poison institutions, partnerships, and people who were not proper opponents in the first place. That is why the hard version of the chapter cannot stop at effectiveness. It has to ask whether the contradiction was real, whether the context truly justified pressure, and what collateral cost the destabilization will leave behind.

Chapter 30 entered the other side's frame through communication. Chapter 31 works more obliquely by asking whether the target contains strains that will do the work from within. The next chapter will shift again, from internal fracture to the weak operator's tactic of seeming to submit while building parallel leverage out of sight.
