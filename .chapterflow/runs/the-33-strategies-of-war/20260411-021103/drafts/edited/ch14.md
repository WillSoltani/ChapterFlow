# The Blitzkrieg Strategy: Overwhelm Resistance with Speed and Suddenness

Rhea did not wait for the rival's response memo. By the time the larger company finished debating her first release, her team had already shipped the second change and had the third one lined up. From the outside it looked like constant motion. From inside the sequence it was something sharper. Each move forced the rival to spend time understanding a position that no longer existed. Their response kept landing on old ground while Rhea's team kept moving the fight forward.

Greene's blitzkrieg chapter treats that lag as a weapon. Speed is not just convenience. When one side can observe, decide, and execute faster than the other side can process what is happening, resistance forms too late. The slower side ends up reacting to a stale picture and burns energy on problems the faster side has already abandoned. Offensive force here comes from tempo as much as from raw resources.

That is why the chapter is really about operational tempo rather than generic urgency. Fast movement matters only when perception, judgment, decision, and execution are linked tightly enough that the next move arrives before the opponent has stabilized around the last one. A team can look busy and still be slow if every decision waits on drag, translation, or fear. Greene's modern version is structural. The advantage belongs to the organization whose loops are shorter, cleaner, and easier to repeat under pressure.

Rhea's case shows how tempo becomes leverage. Her team does not simply move fast for the sake of feeling intense. It reduces decision lag, shortens release cycles, and uses rapid feedback to keep the product one beat ahead of the incumbent's process. The rival has more money, more people, and more brand weight. None of that closes the gap if its reaction arrives late every time. Blitzkrieg works because Rhea turns speed into a repeated mismatch between where the rival thinks the fight is and where the fight has already gone.

Petra sees the same logic in a graduate operations strategy lab. Another team has better resources and more polished slides, but Petra's group wins ground by cycling through decisions faster. They test, update, and reframe before the other side has finished defending the previous assumption. The chapter's point is not that thoughtfulness should disappear. It is that judgment has to be built into the loop instead of treated as a pause that stops the loop. The team with the faster useful loop keeps forcing everyone else to react from behind.

The reversal matters because fast movement can be dumb movement. Greene is explicit that speed by itself is not wisdom. If the operator moves fast in the wrong direction, the error compounds sooner and spreads farther. Tempo only helps when the underlying decisions are good enough to deserve acceleration. That is why Chapter 14 belongs after Chapter 13. First you need a usable picture of the opponent and the field. Then speed turns that picture into pressure. Without judgment, blitzkrieg becomes a quicker route to a worse mistake.

That makes Chapter 14 the acceleration chapter of offensive warfare. Chapter 13 aimed at the real opponent. Chapter 14 adds the ability to keep that opponent reacting too slowly to regain balance. The next chapter widens the lens again. It asks how to seize the terms, agenda, and pace so the other side is reacting to you even before sheer speed enters the picture.
