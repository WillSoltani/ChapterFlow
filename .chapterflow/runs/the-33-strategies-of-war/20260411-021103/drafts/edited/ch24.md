# The Ordinary-Extraordinary Strategy: Take the Line of Least Expectation

Junia's team was waiting for her late dramatic move. So was the counterparty. For three weeks she had answered provocation with a visible escalation at the end of the round, and now everyone in the room was arranging themselves for the fourth version of the same scene. Instead she made the smallest request of the month, delivered it calmly, and let the counterparty overprepare for an attack that never came.

That is the logic of Greene's ordinary-extraordinary strategy. The point is not surprise as a personality trait. The point is forecast failure. Opponents learn from your recent texture, then start reacting to the move they think must be next. Once that happens, your own habits become handles the other side can grip. The skill is to vary the texture so the prediction fails.

That is why the chapter does not simply praise the extraordinary. Sometimes the strongest break is the most ordinary move available, precisely because everyone has braced for spectacle. Sometimes the break has to be sharp and unusual because ordinary behavior has become too legible. The operator is not chasing novelty. The operator is refusing to let one visible pattern harden into a script.

Junia's small request works because it scrambles the counterparty's timing. It has staffed up for resistance, framed her as the person who always peaks late, and prepared rebuttals for the dramatic ask. When none of that arrives, the room loses rhythm. Later, once that new calm has been normalized, she uses one sharp deviation and gets more effect from it because the forecast has already been bent twice.

Kester finds the classroom version in an undergraduate behavioral economics seminar. Classmates keep calling the strategy randomness, which makes it sound either childish or reckless. He changes the frame. The goal is not chaos. The goal is to deny the other side a usable pattern while keeping your own objective stable. Once the room sees that difference, the move stops looking like style and starts looking like control over prediction.

The reversal matters because undisciplined unpredictability damages the operator first. Teams cannot execute around pure volatility. Partners stop trusting motives. Rivals may even benefit if your need to be surprising makes you abandon the strongest ordinary move on the board. Strategic variance only works when something underneath it stays fixed: the objective, the standard, or the logic of the campaign. Without that anchor, broken expectation turns into drift.

Chapter 23 shifted the opponent's picture of reality. Chapter 24 shifts the opponent's forecast of the next move. Both work on the mind, but they do different jobs. The next chapter will widen the frame again by moving from the opponent's expectations to the watching audience, where moral positioning can change the whole structure of the fight.
