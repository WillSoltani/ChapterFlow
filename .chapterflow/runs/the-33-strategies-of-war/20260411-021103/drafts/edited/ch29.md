# The Fait Accompli Strategy: Take Small Bites

Oriana stopped asking for the full operating shift in one meeting. Every time she framed the change as a whole, opposition organized fast because the scale was obvious. She broke it apart instead. One smaller approval adjusted reporting lines. Another changed the review cadence. Another moved sign-off authority. Each step looked too limited to trigger a large fight. Together, they produced almost the same result as the original proposal.

That is Greene's fait accompli strategy in its practical form. The operator builds major change through a sequence of small moves that each stay below the threshold at which the other side would fully react. The move is not about one dramatic deception. It is about sequencing and aggregation. Opponents often judge each bite by itself and fail to ask what the bites add up to.

The chapter works because reaction thresholds are usually built around single visible moves. A large shift looks threatening enough to oppose. A minor adjustment often looks tolerable, temporary, or not worth the cost of a full response. By the time several minor changes have accumulated, the field has already moved. Reversal is harder because the new position is not one event. It is a structure assembled in parts.

Oriana succeeds because she manages the order of change as carefully as the content of change. She does not ask for one large permission that would clarify the stakes too early. She stacks smaller permissions that feel manageable alone. By the time opponents read the aggregate clearly, the working reality has already changed. Position comes from accumulation, not from one triumphant push.

Dashiell finds the classroom version in a graduate public administration seminar. Classmates keep treating incrementalism as if smallness automatically made it harmless. He changes the question. What do the small steps add up to, and who would object if the whole were stated plainly at the start? Once the room sees the aggregate, the chapter stops looking neutral. Small steps can be disciplined sequencing, but they can also be a way to hide the true scale of change from the people absorbing it.

The reversal matters because cumulative strategy becomes ethically unstable when the operator relies on fragmentation to avoid informed resistance. Some changes should be debated as wholes. Some incremental builds are honest because each step remains defensible even after the full sequence is visible. Others are not. The operator therefore needs two kinds of judgment at once: tactical awareness of thresholds and moral awareness of what the concealed total would mean if exposed.

Chapter 28 gained by refusing the attack that would save a rival from self-destruction. Chapter 29 gains by moving in steps small enough to avoid full reaction until the aggregate becomes hard to reverse. The next chapter will turn from cumulative structure to communication, where the operator works more directly on perception, attention, and mental framing.
