# The Alliance Strategy: Seem to Work for the Interests of Others While Furthering Your Own

Imogen stopped calling the project a chance for senior leadership to support younger talent. The line sounded warm, but it never moved anyone with real power. She changed the pitch. Her team's pilot would cut rollout time on the exact initiative one executive was already being judged on. Support arrived quickly after that, not because the executive had become generous, but because success for the project now counted as success for the sponsor too.

That is Greene's alliance strategy in its cleaner form. The operator advances by finding genuine overlap between their own aims and the aims of a stronger party. The move is not pure flattery and not friendship theater. It is incentive design. A weaker operator gains leverage when a stronger actor can push them forward while still feeling, accurately, that they are serving themselves.

The chapter works because stronger parties rarely move on sentiment alone for long. Shared values may help at the margin, but durable support usually depends on real self-interest. A senior sponsor becomes useful when your progress helps them hit a target they already care about. A large customer becomes a structural advocate when your success solves a problem that matters inside their institution. The alliance holds when the overlap is real enough to survive pressure.

Imogen succeeds because she stops asking for endorsement as a favor. She makes the executive's ambition part of the architecture of support. That changes the quality of backing. The relationship no longer depends on mood, charisma, or temporary goodwill. It depends on a result the sponsor wants for their own reasons. Borrowed leverage becomes sturdier when it is tied to a stronger actor's incentives instead of their identity.

Rourke finds the classroom version in a graduate organizational politics seminar. Classmates describe alliances as networking skill, as if the main task were reading personalities and trading attention. He cuts against that. The harder question is whether the other side's goals are truly served by your progress or whether you are only staging the appearance of overlap. Once the room sees that distinction, alliance stops sounding like charm and starts sounding like structure.

The reversal matters because fake overlap can buy motion early. A sponsor may like the story, the language, or the optics before they test the underlying incentives. But support built on staged overlap decays quickly when costs rise or priorities shift. The operator then learns a harsh lesson: borrowed leverage is not loyalty, and alignment borrowed from appearance can collapse faster than support built on honest interest.

Chapter 26 denied the enemy a clean target. Chapter 27 changes the mechanism. Instead of surviving pressure by diffusion, the operator advances by making stronger actors carry part of the effort because doing so serves them. The next chapter will leave alignment behind and move into one-upmanship, where rivals are tempted into overreach that damages them more than any direct blow could.
