# The Center-of-Gravity Strategy: Hit Them Where It Hurts

Inara's team kept proposing feature-for-feature attacks on the rival's flagship product. She stopped them. The flagship was visible, expensive, and tempting, but it was not the point that held the rival's position together. The real support was quieter: the assumption that premium customers would keep tolerating a rising price gap. Once Inara saw that, the target changed. She no longer needed to beat the flagship everywhere. She needed to weaken the belief that made the whole premium stack feel safe.

Greene's center-of-gravity chapter is about that kind of diagnosis. Every opponent has a load-bearing point: the support, assumption, supply line, credibility source, alliance, or confidence structure whose weakening makes the rest of the system harder to sustain. The decisive target is often less visible than the obvious target, which is why many attacks waste energy on the wrong thing. People hit what looks strongest, loudest, or most symbolic. Greene's point is harsher. The best strike is aimed at what the visible strength quietly depends on.

That is why the chapter is not simply about aggression. It is about selective concentration. A weaker operator can still gain leverage if force is applied at the one point whose damage spreads. A stronger operator can still fail if it spends too much attention battering a target that looks impressive but does not decide anything. The center-of-gravity question is therefore diagnostic before it is tactical: what, if broken, makes the rest of this harder for the opponent to hold together?

Inara's case shows the logic clearly. Attacking the flagship product head-on would have made her team look bold and left the rival's broader pricing story untouched. Attacking premium retention changes more. If customers begin questioning the value of the price gap, the flagship weakens, the surrounding offers weaken, and the whole strategic posture loses stability. The move matters because it shifts effort away from visible strength and toward the condition that keeps that strength convincing.

Lucan finds the same lesson in a graduate health policy workshop. Another team presents a polished proposal with strong moral language, attractive design, and a confident tone. Most of the room wants to argue with the surface claims. Lucan notices that the whole case depends on one disputed data premise. If that premise fails, the moral language still sounds good but the policy architecture stops carrying weight. He learns that a decisive point can be one fact, one assumption, or one support whose collapse drains authority from everything built on top of it.

The reversal matters because misidentifying the center of gravity is fatal. A forceful strike on a secondary target can leave the attacker exposed, self-satisfied, and out of position. Greene ties this chapter back to intelligence for that reason. Better targeting depends on a truer map first. If you guess wrong about what is load-bearing, you may spend your best move proving that you hit something real but not decisive. That is a strategic loss hidden inside a tactical success.

That makes Chapter 16 the decisive-point chapter of offensive warfare. Chapter 15 established who controls the dynamic. Chapter 16 asks where that control should be focused to matter most. Once the load-bearing point is visible, the next move is not always to hit one center harder. Sometimes it is to split the opponent into weaker pieces and beat them separately. Chapter 17 moves there next.
