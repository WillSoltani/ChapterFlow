# The One-Upmanship Strategy: Give Your Rivals Enough Rope to Hang Themselves

Maren told her side to stop preparing the attack memo. The rival executive they wanted to confront was already doing the damage alone: alienating board members, overplaying authority, and turning small concerns into private resentment across the company. A public strike would have made the situation cleaner for him. He would have gained a visible enemy, a sharper story, and a reason for hesitant supporters to close ranks. Maren refused to rescue him that way.

That is Greene's one-upmanship strategy in its more serious form. Some rivals are already defeating themselves. The operator gains by recognizing that outside attack would interrupt the self-destruction rather than accelerate it. The move is not laziness and not fear. It is disciplined noninterference. You leave the rival enough room to keep making the choices that weaken the rival's own position.

The chapter works because internal fracture often looks messy right before collapse. When a rival is still dividing supporters, overreaching in public, or loading more weight onto a failing posture, an external attack can simplify the field in the rival's favor. Factions that were drifting apart suddenly unite against a common threat. Confusion turns into purpose. A self-inflicted crisis becomes a mobilizing fight. The wrong blow can save the person you meant to remove.

Maren wins by preparing for succession instead of confrontation. She keeps relationships with board members steady, stays ready to absorb responsibility, and refuses the temptation of immediate catharsis. Her rival keeps worsening the position without help. By the time the break becomes formal, Maren looks less like an aggressor and more like the obvious person to stabilize what remains. The inherited opening exists because she did not interrupt the fall.

Hale finds the classroom version in a graduate corporate governance seminar. Classmates keep treating action as if it were automatically stronger than restraint. He changes the question. Will this intervention weaken the rival, or will it rescue the rival by providing unity, purpose, and a common enemy? Once the room sees the rescue risk, patience stops looking passive and starts looking diagnostic.

The reversal matters because waiting is only strategic when the rival is actually moving toward collapse and the surrounding field can bear the delay. Some rivals recover, some damage others too deeply while they wobble, and some only look unstable from a distance. The operator still needs judgment. Strategic restraint can become passive drift if you stop testing whether the rival is really weakening or whether your own inaction is just buying time for the rival to recover.

Chapter 27 gained force through genuine overlap with stronger actors. Chapter 28 gains position by refusing to save a rival from self-inflicted damage.
