# The Righteous Strategy: Occupy the Moral High Ground

Leonie cut the draft statement in half before the strike vote went public. Two of her allies wanted hotter language, a broader accusation list, and one line designed purely to humiliate management on camera. She killed all three. If their side wanted observers to read it as measured and justified, it could not spend its sympathy budget before the real collision had even started.

That is the logic of Greene's righteous strategy. In any conflict watched by third parties, the side read as just gains structural advantage. Attacks against it look harsher. Its losses produce more sympathy. Its opponent pays more for escalation because observers interpret the same force differently depending on who seems to deserve protection.

The chapter only works when the posture is built two ways at once. One part is substantive. The operator actually has to be standing on conduct or demands that an audience can recognize as defensible. The second part is performative. That conduct has to be framed in language the audience already knows how to honor. Messaging matters, but it cannot carry what the conduct cannot support.

Leonie's discipline gives her side that support. By narrowing the demands and refusing retaliatory rhetoric, she makes management's next aggressive move easier for outsiders to read. The point is not saintliness. The point is cost structure. Once observers read one side as measured and the other as excessive, the same escalation lands differently and the aggressive side pays more for it.

Phelan finds the classroom version in a graduate constitutional law seminar. Classmates keep treating moral high ground as branding, as if the right phrase can manufacture legitimacy from thin air. He changes the frame. Moral high ground is a credibility structure. It takes real consistency to build and almost no time to lose. Once the room starts looking at hypocrisy risk instead of slogan quality, the strategy stops sounding decorative and starts sounding expensive.

The reversal is the chapter's warning label. A single exposed contradiction can collapse the posture faster than a dozen good statements can repair it. If your side claims discipline and then acts vindictively, claims fairness and then cheats, claims restraint and then overreaches, the audience updates fast. Moral high ground is heavy because it restrains your own conduct first. If you want the advantage, you have to carry the burden.

Chapter 24 broke the opponent's forecast. Chapter 25 changes how the wider audience assigns legitimacy and blame. The next chapter will reverse the visual logic altogether. Instead of being seen as just, the operator will try to become hard to fix in place at all.
