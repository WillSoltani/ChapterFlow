# The Intelligence Strategy: Know Your Enemy

Corin did not start by polishing the pitch. He started by finding out why the other side wanted the meeting at all. On paper the partnership looked simple: a clean expansion move, a sensible price point, a public story about alignment. Under that surface sat the real leverage. One executive needed a fast win before budget review. Another wanted access to Corin's data more than the public partnership itself. A third was quietly worried about internal duplication. If Corin had walked in responding only to the official story, he would have negotiated with a brochure instead of an opponent.

Greene's intelligence chapter begins there. Most attacks fail because they are launched against a fantasy. The operator thinks they understand the other side because they have a headline, a press statement, or one dramatic behavior to point at. But the real opponent is built out of motives, fears, constraints, alliances, rivalries, and vulnerabilities that rarely sit in public view. Action taken against the public version often lands on the wrong target and then looks mysteriously ineffective.

That is why intelligence work is not decorative preparation. It is the work that makes the move real. Before acting, the operator has to know what the other side actually wants, what it is trying to protect, what resources it truly has, who it is aligned with, and where the internal tensions are. Greene is blunt about the mood of this labor. It is slower and less glamorous than action. It looks like listening, cross-checking, noticing who is not aligned with whom, and asking what incentive would make the visible behavior make sense. But that unglamorous work is what prevents the operator from striking at appearances.

Corin's meeting changes once he stops treating the counterpart as a single block. He goes in knowing that the public partnership case matters less than the private budget pressure and the internal duplication fear. So he structures the proposal around phased adoption and internal credit rather than headline language. The move lands because it answers the real motives in the room. Intelligence does not make him passive. It lets him make an offer aimed at the actual pressure points instead of the story everyone is reciting.

Iris discovers the same pattern in a graduate cybersecurity policy lab. A rival team presents a unified hard-line position, but the surface confidence is hiding a split. The technical half wants a realistic implementation path while the political half wants symbolic toughness. If Iris responds only to the loud public line, she strengthens the posture she is trying to weaken. Instead she addresses the implementation split, forcing the team to reveal that its internal incentives are not aligned. The chapter's lesson is not that everyone is lying. It is that visible coherence is often a mask stretched over different motives.

The reversal matters because intelligence can become a way to postpone risk forever. Some operators hide inside research. They keep gathering facts, collecting signals, and waiting for certainty as if more information will one day remove the need for judgment. Greene rejects that fantasy. The point of intelligence is not to know everything. It is to know enough to stop attacking a fiction. At some point the picture is usable and the move has to be made. If the operator keeps researching after the answer has become actionable, caution turns into paralysis and the chapter defeats itself.

That makes Chapter 13 the aiming chapter of offensive warfare. Chapter 12 chose the larger war. Chapter 13 says the war still fails if the operator cannot see the enemy as they are. The next chapter adds the second half of offensive force. Once the picture is good enough, speed and suddenness can become weapons in their own right.
