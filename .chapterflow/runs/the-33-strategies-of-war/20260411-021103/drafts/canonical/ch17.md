# The Divide-and-Conquer Strategy: Defeat Them in Detail

Aveline listened to the vendor coalition speak as if it were one body, then stopped her team from answering it that way. The loud version of the conflict was simple: the coalition opposed the city's new data standard. The useful version was narrower. Two members of the coalition disagreed sharply about liability exposure if the standard changed. That seam mattered more than the speech they had prepared together.

Greene's divide-and-conquer chapter begins with that kind of refusal. A united opponent is stronger than the sum of its parts because each member lends weight, confidence, and cover to the others. If you attack the whole coalition head on, you let that combined strength stay intact. The strategic move is to study where the unity is thinner than it looks. Coalitions usually contain different risks, incentives, and thresholds for pain. The alignment is real, but it is rarely uniform.

That is why the strategy is not mere aggression. It is seam detection. The operator looks for the issue one member cannot absorb as easily as the others, the term that makes one partnership uneasy, or the priority that is being papered over by public solidarity. Once the seam is located, the wedge has to be precise. The point is not to insult the whole coalition into chaos. The point is to widen an existing discomfort until one part sees advantage in stepping away.

Aveline uses the strategy by moving the conversation from public principle to liability allocation. As long as the coalition can keep talking about the standard in broad language, it sounds disciplined and unified. Once liability exposure is put on the table, one member realizes that the coalition position protects the others more than it protects them. That does not destroy the coalition by force. It changes the math inside it. After one member peels away, Aveline is no longer bargaining with a single block. She is negotiating with weaker, separate pieces.

Maceo sees the same pattern in a graduate coalition governance seminar. A student reform bloc presents itself as unanimous, and most of the room treats it as one negotiating actor. Maceo notices that the bloc's members agree on the goal but not on enforcement. Some want automatic penalties, others want review discretion. Once he presses that distinction, the bloc can no longer hide inside one slogan. It has to reveal internal priorities, and the apparent mass starts separating into negotiable parts.

The reversal is what keeps the chapter honest. A clumsy wedge can strengthen the very coalition it tries to split. If the operator pushes too loudly, attacks the wrong seam, or fabricates conflict that was not truly there, the members may close ranks out of shared resentment. Greene's point is that delicacy matters more than drama. The best wedge feels like pressure on a real fault line, not a theatrical attempt to manufacture betrayal.

Chapter 16 asked what load-bearing point holds a system together. Chapter 17 asks what happens when the system is held together by several actors moving in concert. The answer is not to smash the concert head on. It is to make the alignment stop carrying equal weight. Once the coalition breaks into pieces, the field changes. Chapter 18 narrows further and asks how to attack the soft flank instead of the strong front.
