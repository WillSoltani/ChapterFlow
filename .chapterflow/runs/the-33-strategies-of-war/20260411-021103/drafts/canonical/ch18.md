# The Turning Strategy: Expose and Attack Your Opponent's Soft Flank

Ariadne watched everyone compare her product to the incumbent on the incumbent's strongest dimension, then refused to join them. Feature depth was the front. Onboarding was the flank. The incumbent could win the public comparison on depth for months, but it could not fix its slow, messy first-use experience without ripping through several parts of its own system. That weaker side mattered more.

Greene's turning strategy begins with that kind of discipline. Every defense has a place where it is strongest and a place where it is thinner. The strong front is obvious, which is why people keep charging into it. The soft flank usually takes more reconnaissance. It may be a process weakness, an adaptation lag, a rehearsed blind spot, or a dimension the opponent has not built to defend well. The advantage comes from finding that weaker side before the direct clash drains your force.

That is why the chapter values reconnaissance so highly. The flank is rarely announced. It has to be inferred from where the opponent is overprepared, overcommitted, or slow to adapt. Ariadne sees that clearly. Competing on feature depth would reward the incumbent's strongest preparation. Competing on onboarding forces a different fight. It turns the contest toward the place where the incumbent has less room to respond quickly.

Nikolai finds the same lesson in a graduate transportation policy studio. One proposal is airtight on funding because the team knows everyone will challenge the numbers. Most of the room stays there. Nikolai notices that implementation sequencing is vague, underrehearsed, and easier to destabilize. Once he presses the weak side instead of the strong front, the proposal stops looking balanced. The issue was not hidden because it was irrelevant. It was hidden because the team had built its defense somewhere else.

The chapter's edge is therefore not just indirectness. It is disciplined target choice. A flank attack works when the weak side is real and when the opponent cannot reinforce it quickly enough. That is what makes the move cheaper and faster than a frontal contest. The operator is not avoiding difficulty. The operator is refusing waste.

Greene's reversal matters because the search for the flank can become its own form of self-deception. Some defenses really are balanced. Some opponents have already secured every approach that matters. In those cases, the hunt for a soft side becomes delay, and delay costs initiative. The operator has to stay honest enough to admit when the front is the only viable route.

Chapter 17 broke the united wall into smaller parts. Chapter 18 asks where to strike when a defense is still standing but not equally strong everywhere. The answer is to stop admiring the front and study the side that cannot be held as well. Chapter 19 will push offense to its harshest limit and test what happens when the goal is no longer position but total encirclement.
