# The Command-and-Control Strategy: Avoid the Snares of Groupthink

Raina could feel the launch plan hardening for the wrong reason. The vice president had proposed it first, which meant every objection now sounded like disloyalty instead of useful pressure. Nobody in the room was foolish. The analysts had seen the same weak assumption. The engineers knew the timeline was soft. The product lead had already spotted the customer segment that would break the forecast. Still, the meeting kept moving as if the plan had been proven. Greene's opening claim lands here: good groups can produce worse decisions than any one person in them when the social cost of speaking is obvious and the cost of silence stays hidden.

That is why the chapter treats groupthink as a structure problem instead of a personality problem. A hierarchy can gather smart people and still train them into public passivity. When rank owns the room, disagreement starts looking like risk while compliance starts looking like professionalism. The result is not unity. It is frightened consensus. Greene's answer is a command style that keeps judgment moving upward and initiative moving downward. Leaders still lead. They set the objective, define the boundary, and decide what cannot be compromised. What they do not do is script every step so tightly that the people closest to the changing facts become little more than careful spectators.

The modern translation is mission before method. Tell the team what must be achieved, why it matters, and what constraints are real. Then let the people on the ground solve the path. A micromanaged group can move neatly while conditions remain stable, but the moment new information arrives, that neatness becomes a trap. Everyone waits for instructions. Nobody adjusts in time. A mission-based group looks looser from the outside, yet it often thinks faster because each member knows the purpose well enough to improvise without abandoning the standard. Greene likes this model because it creates two channels at once: dissent can travel upward, and adaptation can travel sideways.

Raina gets the room unstuck by changing the terms of the meeting. She stops asking whether everyone agrees and starts asking each lead what risk they would raise if titles disappeared for five minutes. That small move changes the incentive pattern. The weak assumption surfaces, then the backup data, then the implementation issue that had been sitting in three different heads without ever becoming one spoken warning. After the meeting, she shifts the launch work from instruction chains to mission ownership. Each function gets the goal, the constraints, and the decision rights for its own slice. When the market signal changes two days later, the team adapts without waiting for a ceremonial permission loop. The plan gets smarter because command stopped treating people as extensions of rank.

Malcolm sees the same idea in a smaller setting. His adviser at the undergraduate public-health lab paper gets weak stories whenever she hands students a full reporting script with every call, question, and angle preselected. The students complete the checklist, but they do not really report. When she assigns the reporting goal, the publication standard, and the deadline instead, stronger writers surprise her with better interviews and sharper structure. Newer writers still need more help, so she gives them more scaffolding without taking away the purpose of the piece. That is the chapter's discipline in a teaching register. Autonomy is not the absence of standards. It is responsibility sized to the person's actual context and skill.

The reversal keeps the strategy from turning into management vanity. There are moments when command-and-control is exactly right: an emergency, unfamiliar terrain, a novice crew, or a task where one mistake carries irreversible cost. In those situations, handing down mission without enough procedure is not empowering. It is lazy. Greene's real test is whether the people closest to the action know enough to judge well. If they do, give them room. If they do not, give them steps until they can carry more judgment safely. The failure on either side looks similar from a distance. One leader overcontrols capable people and suffocates adaptation. Another leader underguides unready people and calls the resulting confusion freedom.

That is what makes the chapter harder than a simple vote for flat organizations. Greene is not asking leaders to disappear. He is asking them to design command so truth can travel and action can adjust. Chapter 4 dealt with pressure inside the self. Chapter 5 moves pressure into the team and asks whether the structure lets intelligence survive contact with rank. The next chapter pushes the idea further by asking how the team itself should be built. Once command stops smothering initiative, the organization has to decide whether one large unit can ever move as well as several smaller ones.
