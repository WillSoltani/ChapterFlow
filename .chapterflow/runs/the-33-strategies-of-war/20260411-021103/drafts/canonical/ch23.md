# Misperception Strategies: Weave a Seamless Blend of Fact and Fiction

Salome refused the lie her team wanted. They wanted a cleaner deception campaign, something sharp enough to send the counterparty in the wrong direction fast. She chose a narrower move. She released accurate information in a sequence that pulled attention toward one implication and away from another. The counterparty still misread the situation, but it misread it without being directly fed a false claim.

That is where Greene's misperception strategy gets dangerous. The operator gains leverage by shaping what the other side thinks is happening. In a fair reading, that can mean strategic framing: emphasizing selected true facts so the audience lands on a conclusion you want. In an unfair reading, it becomes propaganda: building false belief by hiding core reality or blending truth with enough fabrication that the audience no longer knows what world it is in.

That distinction matters because this chapter opens the dirty-war section. Earlier chapters could still pretend that pressure, timing, and maneuver were mainly about force and sequence. Here the contested terrain becomes perception itself. The operator is no longer just shaping the field. The operator is shaping the map in the other side's head.

Salome stays on the defensible side by refusing fabrication. Her counterparty reacts to the wrong implication because it overweights one true signal and underweights another, not because she has invented a false world for it to inhabit. That still carries risk. Trust can erode even when the facts are technically accurate. But the move remains different from pushing the other side into a belief that is simply false.

Torin finds the classroom version in a graduate media ethics seminar. Classmates want to flatten everything into one verdict: either all framing is propaganda or all clever messaging is fair play. He rebuilds the distinction by asking a harder question. Is the audience being guided through selective emphasis among true facts, or is it being induced to believe something untrue about the core reality? That shift makes the room judge the move by its effect on truth and consent instead of by whether it sounds sophisticated.

The reversal is where the chapter stops being merely clever. Once the move hides core reality or creates false belief, the gain becomes ethically expensive and often strategically fragile. If the audience discovers it has been pushed into the wrong world, trust collapses harder than it would have under ordinary pressure. The operator may win the moment and lose the field that follows it.

Chapter 22 ended by asking when to stop before ego and sunk cost take over. Chapter 23 asks a different restraint question: how much control over another person's perception are you willing to claim? The next chapter will move from bent perception to broken expectation, where the goal is not to distort the map but to shatter the forecast.
