# The Exit Strategy: Know How to End Things

Mireya closed the initiative after the third failed review, even though everyone in the room could list one more reason to wait. More user research, one more pricing test, one more quarter. She had heard all of it before. The problem was no longer uncertainty. The problem was that every extra month was burning resources that still had somewhere better to go.

That is the logic of Greene's exit strategy. The hard part is rarely noticing that some campaigns should end. The hard part is acting while workable terms still exist. Operators wait too long because sunk cost, public commitment, and identity all argue for staying. By the time they finally admit the campaign is broken, the good exit terms are often gone.

That is why the chapter treats exit as a discipline decided partly in advance. If you wait until the campaign is fully emotional, your judgment gets bent by pride and prior investment. Clear stop conditions give the operator something firmer than mood to rely on. They do not remove judgment, but they stop every review from becoming a referendum on ego.

Mireya's decision protects the wider portfolio for exactly that reason. Her team has attached status to the project, so every benchmark failure gets reframed as a reason to try harder. She returns to the agreed conditions instead: three failed cycles, no cost recovery path, rising spillover damage. Because she exits while the losses are still containable, the rest of the organization keeps its room to maneuver.

Stellan finds the academic version in a graduate labor economics seminar. A negotiation team keeps pushing a dead proposal because nobody wants to admit how much time has already been spent on it. He changes the frame. The issue is not how much effort has gone in. The issue is what one more round is likely to return compared with what it is likely to cost. Once the room starts looking at marginal value instead of accumulated pride, the proposal stops looking noble and starts looking expensive.

The reversal matters because not every hard stretch is a real stop signal. Some campaigns feel unbearable right before they turn. The operator still has to judge whether continuing truly costs more than stopping or whether fatigue is only making it feel that way. Exit discipline is not panic and it is not surrender. It is the refusal to let vanity persistence or cowardly discomfort make the decision for you.
