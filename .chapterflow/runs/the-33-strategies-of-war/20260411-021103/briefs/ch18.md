Chapter Title: The Turning Strategy: Expose and Attack Your Opponent's Soft Flank
Position In Book: Eighteenth chapter of the native 33-strategy structure. Indirect offensive chapter after divide-and-conquer.
Source Sidecar Path: sidecars/source/ch18.source.txt

Core Claim:
The operator avoids the strongest front and looks for the weaker side the opponent cannot defend as well or adapt to quickly.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
Chapter 17 fractured a coalition. Chapter 18 assumes the opponent still has a defense but asks where that defense is structurally thinner.

Author Logic Chain:
First the chapter contrasts the expensive front with the cheaper flank. Then it makes reconnaissance central because the flank is rarely obvious. Then it modernizes the move through incumbent weaknesses and unrehearsed issues. Finally it gives the reversal: some defenses have no real soft flank, and hunting one can cost initiative.

Required Anchors:
- Ariadne, a platform strategist, refuses to challenge an incumbent on feature depth and instead attacks the onboarding problem the incumbent cannot fix quickly. The friction is that everyone else wants the visible front-on comparison. The consequence is that the incumbent is forced to defend a dimension it did not center.
- Nikolai, in a graduate-transportation-policy-studio, notices that a proposal is fully rehearsed on funding but weak on implementation sequencing. The friction is that the room keeps returning to the strongest prepared point. The consequence is that once the weak side is pressed, the proposal loses its stable front.

Specific Applications:
- challenging an incumbent where it cannot adapt well
- steering negotiation toward the issue the counterparty has not prepared for
- reconnoitering for the weaker side instead of charging the obvious front
- knowing when no soft flank exists and speed matters more than search

Common Misreadings:
- assuming the flank is always available
- confusing a less visible issue with a genuinely weaker side
- attacking the front because it is emotionally satisfying
- delaying too long in search of a flank that does not exist

Counterarguments or Limits:
- some defenses really are balanced across approaches
- reconnaissance can take too long and cost initiative
- a flank may appear weak but still be quickly reinforced
- the strategy depends on accurate reading of adaptability, not surface novelty

Previous Chapter Bridge:
Chapter 17 split the coalition. Chapter 18 asks where to strike when the defense is still standing but not equally strong everywhere.

Next Chapter Bridge:
Chapter 19 will move to the book's most extreme offensive move and shift the moral register sharply.

Cross-Chapter Tensions:
- Tension with Chapter 17: divide and conquer breaks the wall into pieces, while turning strategy finds the side the wall defends least well.
- Tension with Chapter 19: turning strategy prefers selective weak-side attack, while annihilation pursues total encirclement.

Moral Complexity:
Medium. The strategy is legitimate when interests are genuinely opposed and the weak side is real, but it becomes manipulative if the operator pretends a flank exists only to avoid direct scrutiny.

Hard-Depth Minimum:
Hard depth must preserve the reconnaissance burden and the reversal that some defenses have no soft flank at all.
