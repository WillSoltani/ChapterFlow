Chapter Title: The One-Upmanship Strategy: Give Your Rivals Enough Rope to Hang Themselves
Position In Book: Twenty-eighth chapter of the native 33-strategy structure. It follows alliance by showing a different way to gain position: not by recruiting force, but by refusing the attack that would save a self-destructive rival.
Source Sidecar Path: sidecars/source/ch28.source.txt

Core Claim:
When a rival is already on a path toward self-damage, the operator may gain more by withholding attack, letting the rival keep overreaching, and stepping into the opening after the collapse.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
Chapter 27 advanced through genuine overlap with stronger actors. Chapter 28 advances through restraint. The operator wins by not interrupting a rival's self-inflicted damage.

Author Logic Chain:
First the chapter defines one-upmanship as disciplined noninterference, not laziness. Then it explains why attacking the wrong rival at the wrong moment can rescue that rival by creating unity. Then it modernizes the move through leadership succession, committee conflict, and competitive overreach examples. Finally it gives the reversal: waiting is only strategic if the rival is actually collapsing and the field can absorb the delay.

Required Anchors:
- Maren sees colleagues gearing up to confront a rival executive who is already alienating the board through unchecked ego and overreach. She argues against the attack because a public fight would unify the rival's fractured support. The friction is that her own side wants the satisfaction of striking now. The consequence is that the rival keeps worsening the position alone, and Maren becomes the obvious successor when the break finally comes.
- Hale, in a graduate-corporate-governance-seminar, hears classmates confuse patience with passivity. He reframes the chapter around rescue risk: the wrong intervention can stabilize the rival by giving that rival a clear external enemy. The friction is that the room wants action to sound inherently stronger than waiting. The consequence is that the class can separate strategic noninterference from avoidance.

Specific Applications:
- withholding a challenge that would unify a rival's internal faction
- allowing an overreaching executive to worsen board relations without interference
- staying out of a competitor's internal conflict when outside attack would stabilize them
- preparing to inherit responsibility once a failing rival loses standing

Common Misreadings:
- treating the strategy as laziness or fear
- assuming every delay is strategic
- enjoying a rival's collapse without preparing to inherit the opening
- waiting so long that the rival recovers or harms the field too deeply

Counterarguments or Limits:
- some rivals do not self-destruct and must be actively opposed
- waiting can become complicity if the damage spreads too far
- the operator still needs succession readiness when the opening appears
- an early attack can sometimes be necessary if the rival's collapse is too slow or too costly to others

Moral Complexity:
Medium-high. Strategic waiting can be disciplined judgment, but the chapter becomes ethically unstable when restraint turns into enjoyment of preventable damage or refusal to act where real harm is spreading.
