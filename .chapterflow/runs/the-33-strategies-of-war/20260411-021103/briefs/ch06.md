Core Claim:
The chapter argues that one large, centralized team often becomes brittle, slow, and overdependent on a few bottlenecks. Greene's answer is segmentation: smaller semi-autonomous units operating inside a shared purpose. These units can move faster, take bounded risks, fail locally, and recombine as conditions change. The reversal is strict: segmentation only works when the units are actually capable.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
Chapter 5 focused on command style. Chapter 6 shifts from how leaders brief to how the organization itself is built. The chapter has to keep the structural advantage clear without romanticizing fragmentation or startup chaos.

Author Logic Chain:
First the chapter claims that monoliths are brittle. Then it argues that smaller units create resilience because they can act, fail, and reconfigure without collapsing the whole. Then it translates that logic into product teams, field teams, and project cells. Then it draws the reversal line: if the units cannot really think and execute, segmentation just spreads incompetence around.

Required Anchors:
- Liora, a product director, notices that every decision in her company flows through one weekly committee, so teams move slowly and all miss together. The friction is that centralization looks coordinated from above. The consequence is that once she breaks the work into smaller product cells with shared metrics and real decision rights, experiments run faster and failures stay local.
- Mateo, a graduate urban-design studio lead, sees stronger work when teams split a city project into smaller site pods that can test, revise, and recombine, instead of keeping the whole studio locked to one giant sequence. The friction is that not every pod is equally capable. The consequence is that the chapter can mark the competence requirement and the need for a clear common frame.

Specific Applications:
- product organizations broken into smaller accountable teams
- operations groups that need bounded experimentation
- studio or lab teams that need multiple parallel work cells
- deciding when to centralize because units are not yet strong enough

Common Misreadings:
- treating segmentation as a synonym for disorder
- confusing decentralization with absence of shared metrics
- assuming more teams always means more speed
- ignoring the competence burden each unit has to carry

Counterarguments or Limits:
- weak units can make segmentation slower, not faster
- some work truly needs central coordination
- shared purpose and standards become more important, not less
- local autonomy without recombination can create silos

Moral Complexity:
