Core Claim:
The chapter argues that stress makes people stupid in a predictable way. Anger, fear, embarrassment, and urgency shrink the field of view until the next move feels self-evident even when it is badly misread. Greene's answer is not generic calmness. It is a repeatable personal counterweight that interrupts the narrow state before action hardens into consequence. The skill is to recognize when the feeling of urgency is only the physiology of pressure, then use a practiced ritual to widen perception long enough to recover judgment.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
Chapter 2 corrected the map. Chapter 3 protects the map from emotional collapse. Its specific tension is that waiting is sometimes wise and sometimes weak. The chapter has to keep both truths alive: emotional urgency is often false clarity, and some moments genuinely punish delay.

Author Logic Chain:
First the chapter names the mechanism: stress narrows the mind and shortens time horizon. Then it insists that presence of mind is not a personality trait but a practice that can be built. Then it translates that practice into modern scenes like the angry email, the frightened announcement, and the pressure-loaded decision. Then it preserves the reversal by admitting that speed can sometimes be strategically correct, which makes the real task discrimination rather than blanket slowness.

Required Anchors:
- Leena, a customer-support director, watches a critical vendor outage spill into social media and starts drafting the furious email that would publicly blame the vendor before the internal facts are settled. The friction is that the adrenaline feels like moral clarity. The consequence is that her counter-ritual of writing the timeline down and calling one trusted operations lead saves her from escalating on partial information.
- Omar, an undergraduate engineering capstone team lead, is humiliated during a prototype demo when a faculty judge tears into the team's safety assumptions. The friction is embarrassment masquerading as certainty that he should defend the design immediately and aggressively. The consequence is that by slowing his speech and asking for a short reset, he hears the judge's actual concern instead of fighting the sting.

Specific Applications:
- holding an angry email overnight or routing it through a second reader
- delaying frightened announcements until the underlying facts are stable
- building a personal reset sequence for stressful meetings or public critique
- separating physiological pressure from actual time sensitivity

Common Misreadings:
- treating the chapter as advice to be passive or endlessly reflective
- assuming composure means suppressing emotion rather than managing action
- using delay as avoidance when the real situation genuinely requires speed
- copying someone else's ritual without testing what actually restores perception

Counterarguments or Limits:
- some crises really do punish delay
- a ritual that is too elaborate can become another form of avoidance
- not every intense feeling is distortion; sometimes pressure is accurate data
- the chapter supports recovery of judgment, not permanent hesitation
