Core Claim:
The chapter argues that success leaves a trap behind it. People keep using the strategy that previously worked because memory makes similarity feel safer than re-reading the present. Greene's discipline is to treat the live situation as its own problem instead of as a replay of the last one. The move is not novelty for its own sake. The move is sharper perception. If the conditions have changed, the old playbook becomes ballast. If the conditions have not changed, abandoning the old playbook becomes vanity.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
Chapter 1 corrected who the opponent is. Chapter 2 corrects the reader's habit of importing yesterday into today. Its distinctive tension is that both errors are dangerous: stale pattern-matching produces misfit action, while novelty addiction discards useful experience for no reason.

Author Logic Chain:
First the chapter identifies the bias: the mind reaches for the war it already knows how to win. Then it translates that bias into modern settings where leaders and founders repeat the conditions that originally made them successful. Then it names the demanded discipline: look at what is actually in front of you rather than what the situation resembles. Then it preserves the reversal by admitting that some problems only look new because the vocabulary around them changed.

Required Anchors:
- Nadia, a startup COO, keeps using the improvisational style that saved the company's first twenty employees even though the company now has compliance burdens, layered teams, and customers who punish chaos. The friction is that the old style is tied to her identity as the person who once saved the company. The consequence is that she starts misreading process needs as bureaucracy instead of as changed terrain.
- Rowan, a law student preparing for moot court, assumes a new judge panel can be handled with the same aggressive cadence that worked in the qualifying round. The friction is that the new panel rewards narrower answers and punishes over-arguing. The consequence is that Rowan mistakes memory of past success for present reading.

Common Misreadings:
- treating the chapter as anti-experience
- assuming novelty itself is evidence of better judgment
- throwing away old methods too quickly because the reader wants to look adaptive
- ignoring that some seemingly new situations are genuinely old structures with updated vocabulary

Counterarguments or Limits:
- prior experience remains useful when the underlying structure is actually stable
- some mature systems need continuity more than reinvention
- readers can overcorrect into restless experimentation
- the chapter asks for re-reading, not for permanent self-distrust
