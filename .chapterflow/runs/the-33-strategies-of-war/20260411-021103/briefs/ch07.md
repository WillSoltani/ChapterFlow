Core Claim:
The chapter argues that segmented teams still need a cause larger than procedure or compensation. People work harder, longer, and through more pain for meaning than for a task list. Morale becomes decisive when the work has a named cause, a named opposing problem, and a leader whose conduct visibly proves belief.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
Chapter 6 explained how to build resilient units. Chapter 7 explains why those units would still feel like parts of one campaign instead of efficient fragments. The chapter has to keep morale concrete and behavioral, not sentimental.

Author Logic Chain:
First the chapter says cause outperforms mere job framing in long campaigns. Then it identifies the mechanics of morale: a named opposing side, a named cause, and visible leader embodiment. Then it translates that into startup, civic, and school settings. Then it draws the reversal line: fake crusades are detected quickly and produce cynicism.

Required Anchors:
- Naima, a transit-software operations lead, notices that her segmented teams sound efficient but detached. They describe their work as ticket volume and backlog burn, not as making commutes more reliable for riders. The friction is that mission language will sound fake if leadership behavior does not change. The consequence is that morale improves only after Naima ties daily choices to rider trust and starts making visible sacrifices that prove she means it.
- Julian, an undergraduate community-history clinic lead, sees students work harder once the project is framed as preserving neighborhood memory instead of just fulfilling course requirements. The friction is that some students suspect branding. The consequence is that belief rises only when Julian's behavior, deadlines, and public standards clearly match the cause.

Specific Applications:
- reconnecting segmented work to a shared mission
- naming the problem the team exists to solve
- using leader behavior, not just slogans, to make belief visible
- detecting when mission language is manipulative or hollow

Common Misreadings:
- treating morale as vague inspiration instead of structural meaning
- assuming the opposing side must be a person rather than a problem or idea
- confusing a sincere cause with manipulative branding
- thinking enthusiasm can replace standards or hard tradeoffs

Counterarguments or Limits:
- fake cause language accelerates cynicism
- some teams need clearer proof before they trust mission claims
- morale without discipline can become self-flattery
- the leader's conduct matters more than the speech itself
