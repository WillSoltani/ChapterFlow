Core Claim:
A united coalition is harder to beat than the individual members inside it. The operator gains leverage by finding the seam that already exists between partners, widening it carefully, and then dealing with the separated pieces one by one.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
Chapter 16 narrowed offense to one load-bearing point. Chapter 17 shifts from one decisive support to multiple aligned actors and asks how to make the alignment stop holding.

Author Logic Chain:
First the chapter defines coalition strength and why head-on attack is wasteful. Then it moves to seam detection: disagreements, incentive gaps, and uncomfortable obligations. Then it shows how a wedge weakens the united front. Finally it gives the reversal: a crude wedge attempt can harden solidarity instead.

Required Anchors:
- Aveline, a civic-affairs operator, faces a vendor coalition blocking a city data standard. She notices two members disagree over liability exposure, presses that seam, and peels one member away. The friction is that the loudest fight is with the whole coalition. The consequence is that once one member defects, the rest become easier to bargain with separately.
- Maceo, in a graduate-coalition-governance-seminar, sees a student reform bloc look united until he spots a split over enforcement. The friction is that the bloc speaks in one voice at first. The consequence is that once the enforcement split is surfaced, the bloc no longer negotiates as a single mass.

Specific Applications:
- peeling one coalition member away over a point of incentive mismatch
- identifying contract terms or enforcement terms that make partners uneasy
- negotiating with fragmented pieces instead of a unified block
- choosing delicacy over obvious provocation when widening a seam

Common Misreadings:
- treating the strategy as generic manipulation
- assuming every coalition contains an easy break point
- driving the wedge too loudly and creating solidarity instead
- confusing public unity with private alignment

Counterarguments or Limits:
- some coalitions are genuinely coherent and hard to fracture
- a bad wedge attempt can create a stronger opponent
- the strategy requires patience and intelligence about internal incentives
- defectors can realign if the operator pushes too hard after the split

Moral Complexity:
Medium. The strategy is legitimate when interests are genuinely opposed and the seam already exists, but it turns dirty when the operator fabricates conflict that was not there.

Hard-Depth Minimum:
Hard depth must preserve the delicacy requirement and the reversal that crude wedge work can unify the opponent.
