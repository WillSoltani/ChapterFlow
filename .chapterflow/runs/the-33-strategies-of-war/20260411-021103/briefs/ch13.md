Core Claim:
The chapter argues that offensive action fails when it targets a fantasy version of the opponent. Before moving, the operator has to understand what the other side actually wants, fears, has, and hides.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
Chapter 12 chose the larger war. Chapter 13 asks what is required before any serious move inside that war: an accurate picture of the opponent rather than projection or wishful thinking.

Author Logic Chain:
First the chapter says most attacks fail because the opponent was misunderstood. Then it defines intelligence as disciplined knowledge of motives, resources, alliances, and vulnerabilities. Then it modernizes the move through negotiation and competitive research. Finally it gives the reversal: waiting for perfect information can become paralysis.

Required Anchors:
- Corin, a founder preparing for a partnership negotiation, spends a week mapping the counterparty's real incentives and internal tensions before the meeting. The friction is that the research feels slow next to action. The consequence is that he makes proposals aimed at the real leverage points instead of the public story.
- Iris, in a graduate-cybersecurity-policy-lab, realizes a rival team's loud position is masking a split between technical and political priorities. The friction is that acting on the obvious story would be faster. The consequence is that she addresses the internal split and changes the outcome.

Common Misreadings:
- assuming visible behavior reveals real motives
- collecting facts without building a decision picture
- treating intelligence work as passive or secondary
- waiting for certainty and never moving

Counterarguments or Limits:
- too much research can become avoidance
- some signals are noisy or intentionally deceptive
- intelligence matters only if it changes the move
- action still has to happen before certainty is complete

Moral Complexity:
