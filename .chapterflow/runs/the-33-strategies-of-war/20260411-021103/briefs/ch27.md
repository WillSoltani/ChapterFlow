Chapter Title: The Alliance Strategy: Seem to Work for the Interests of Others While Furthering Your Own
Position In Book: Twenty-seventh chapter of the native 33-strategy structure. It follows denied targets by shifting from avoiding concentrated attack to borrowing stronger momentum through aligned incentives.
Source Sidecar Path: sidecars/source/ch27.source.txt

Core Claim:
The operator gains leverage by finding a genuine overlap between their interests and the interests of a stronger actor, then building the relationship so the stronger actor advances them while pursuing its own aims.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
Chapter 26 survived pressure by removing a clean target. Chapter 27 moves differently. It does not dissipate force. It recruits outside force by making advancement mutually useful.

Author Logic Chain:
First the chapter defines alliance as interest alignment rather than loyalty theater. Then it explains why weaker operators benefit when stronger parties see their own ambitions inside the arrangement. Then it modernizes the move through sponsorship, enterprise advocacy, and coalition examples. Finally it gives the moral limit: fake overlap can buy short motion while planting a later rupture.

Required Anchors:
- Imogen notices that her team keeps pitching senior sponsors in terms of gratitude and visibility instead of in terms of the sponsors' actual incentives. She reframes the effort around one executive goal that her project's success would materially advance. The friction is that colleagues want a cleaner story about mentorship and shared values. The consequence is that support becomes more durable because it is tied to real self-interest instead of sentiment.
- Rourke, in a graduate-organizational-politics-seminar, hears classmates describe alliances as networking charm. He corrects the frame. The serious question is whether the other party's goals are genuinely served by your progress, or whether you are only staging the appearance of overlap. The friction is that the room wants alliance to sound cleaner than it is. The consequence is that the class can distinguish durable alignment from manipulative pseudo-alignment.

Specific Applications:

- building coalitions where stronger parties benefit materially from your success

Common Misreadings:
- treating alliance as pure charm or networking theater
- assuming shared language means shared incentives
- confusing temporary convenience with durable overlap
- reading borrowed leverage as permanent loyalty

Counterarguments or Limits:
- the strategy can become manipulative if the overlap is manufactured rather than real
- overdependence on a sponsor can leave the operator exposed when incentives shift
- some actors will extract more from the arrangement than they contribute
- alignment can decay quickly if it is not maintained by real mutual benefit

Moral Complexity:
Medium-high. Genuine overlap can produce honest cooperation, but the strategy becomes ethically unstable when the overlap is staged, hidden, or used to mask extraction.

Hard-Depth Minimum:
Hard depth must keep the difference between genuine incentive overlap and manipulative pseudo-alignment, plus the risk of confusing borrowed leverage for loyalty.
