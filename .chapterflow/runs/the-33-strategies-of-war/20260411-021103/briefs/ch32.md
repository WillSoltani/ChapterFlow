Chapter Title: The Passive-Aggression Strategy: Dominate While Seeming to Submit
Position In Book: Thirty-second chapter of the native 33-strategy structure. It follows inner-front fracture by moving into the weak operator's problem: how to build power under a stronger force without being crushed too early.
Source Sidecar Path: sidecars/source/ch32.source.txt

Core Claim:
When open defiance would fail, a weaker operator may need to appear compliant while quietly building parallel capacity and leverage out of sight.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
Chapter 31 weakened the target through contradictions already inside it. Chapter 32 shifts perspective to the weaker side, where the problem is survival under asymmetry and the tactic is hidden preparation rather than visible challenge.

Author Logic Chain:
First the chapter defines passive-aggression here as outward submission combined with hidden capacity building. Then it explains why direct defiance by a weaker operator often fails too early. Then it modernizes the move through workplace hierarchy and asymmetric competition. Finally it gives the reversal: hidden capacity can be prudent under domination, but where honest disagreement was available the same move becomes corrosive duplicity.

Required Anchors:
- Kestrel is trapped under a stronger manager who punishes visible challenge and blocks formal channels. Instead of forcing a public confrontation, Kestrel quietly builds relationships, competence, and options until dependence shifts. The consequence is that the chapter teaches concealed preparation rather than theatrical resistance.
- Noor, in a graduate-conflict-resolution-lab, hears classmates praise hidden undermining as if weakness alone made it justified. She draws the line: covert leverage may be defensible where direct challenge would be crushed, but using the same tactic when open, good-faith disagreement was possible turns prudence into bad faith.

Common Misreadings:
- treating all indirect preparation as manipulative
- assuming weakness morally excuses any duplicity
- confusing patience with passivity
- ignoring contexts where direct good-faith disagreement was available

Counterarguments or Limits:
- hidden preparation can still damage trust if overused
- some hierarchies should be exited, not endured through concealment
- covert leverage can become self-corrupting if the operator never returns to open action
- the tactic does not belong where the other side is not truly coercive or dominant
