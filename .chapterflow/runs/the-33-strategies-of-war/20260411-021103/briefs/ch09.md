Core Claim:
The chapter argues that the best return often comes after an opponent has attacked, exposed intent, and overcommitted. The skill is to absorb the first strike without panic, read the opening it creates, and hit before the attacker regains balance.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
Chapter 8 was about declining weak fights. Chapter 9 is about the moment when a real attack has already started and restraint must shift into timed retaliation.

Author Logic Chain:
First the chapter says attacks reveal information and create imbalance. Then it argues that the operator should not answer instantly. Then it defines the timing window: not too early, not too late. Finally it marks the reversal: counterattack depends on the opponent making the first move, so waiting indefinitely against slow attrition is a losing posture.

Required Anchors:
- Mara, a communications director, is hit with a rival's public accusation during a funding review. The friction is that instant response would look confident but would also answer before the rival fully commits. The consequence is that she waits, lets the claim spread to its widest unsupported form, and then answers with documentation that turns the accusation back on its source.
- Quentin, a graduate-public-interest-clinic advocate, faces an opposing team that opens with an extreme procedural objection. The friction is that he wants to answer immediately, but the objection gets weaker as it grows broader. The consequence is that he lets the objection extend, then uses its overreach as the basis for a cleaner counterargument before the faculty panel.

Specific Applications:

- deciding when an attack has fully revealed its shape

Common Misreadings:
- confusing counterattack with instant retaliation
- waiting so long that the opening closes
- romanticizing patience when the opponent is only grinding slowly
- missing the information value of an incoming attack

Counterarguments or Limits:
- some opponents never overcommit in a single visible move
- slow attrition can punish operators who wait for a dramatic opening
- the strategy requires emotional control under pressure
- not every attack creates a useful opening

Previous Chapter Bridge:
Chapter 8 taught selective refusal. Chapter 9 begins when refusal is no longer enough because the attack is already underway.

Next Chapter Bridge:
Chapter 10 studies deterrence and asks how to make the opponent hesitate before attacking at all.

Cross-Chapter Tensions:
- Tension with Chapter 8: declining a fight preserves force, but once the strike lands the better economy may be to return it at the moment of overextension.
- Tension with Chapter 10: counterattack answers aggression after the fact, while deterrence tries to price the attack out of existence beforehand.

Moral Complexity:
Medium. The chapter values restraint under attack, but the counter itself can become opportunistic if the operator enjoys reversal more than proportional response.

Hard-Depth Minimum:
Hard depth must preserve both timing errors and the attrition reversal.
