Strategy 30 presents communication as strategic entry into another person's thinking. Greene's version points to speech, writing, and signals that get past defenses and keep shaping decisions after the exchange ends. The source frame has a split moral reading that the chapter should keep explicit. In the fair reading, this is disciplined persuasion: speaking in terms the audience can actually hear, moving them by relevance, clarity, and structure rather than by self-expression alone. In the unfair reading, it turns into manipulation: bypassing reflection, planting frames, and moving people in ways they would reject if the mechanism were visible. The useful modern lesson is not to romanticize rhetorical power. It is to learn how communication lands, why audiences resist messages built from the speaker's perspective alone, and where persuasion stops being respectful because it depends on distortion, concealment, or emotional override. The chapter should connect backward to Strategy 29 by shifting from cumulative structural moves into direct work on perception and judgment.
