Greene's grand-strategy chapter says that short-term wins matter only when they serve a long-term design. The operator with a real grand strategy can lose individual battles without panic because those losses are being spent in service of a larger campaign. The operator without one treats each fight as if it were the whole war and ends up collecting victories that do not add up.

The practical skill is to name the long-horizon outcome clearly enough that each short-horizon decision can be tested against it. A move may feel impressive now and still be wrong if it pulls effort away from the larger design. The chapter's modern translation is the difference between a career, company, or institution built around a coherent multi-year direction and one built around whatever looks best in the current season.

The reversal keeps the strategy from becoming doctrine. A grand plan that is never revised turns rigid and blind. The operator needs both a stable long horizon and the willingness to revise the route when reality reveals something new.
