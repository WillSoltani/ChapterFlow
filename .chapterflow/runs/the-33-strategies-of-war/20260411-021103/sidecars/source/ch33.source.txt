Strategy 33 is the book's sharpest moral edge. Its raw form is psychological shock: a small but unexpected move produces outsized fear because the audience starts imagining what else could happen. The source itself warns that this should not be taught as intimidation. The legitimate modern version is narrower and cleaner: an ethical, surprising move that forces the other side to revise its strategic picture because it was not predicted, not because it created terror. The teaching prose has to hold that distinction hard. Surprise can be fair. Panic induction is not. The chapter should therefore center on pattern break, recalibration, and psychological disproportion while explicitly rejecting intimidation, terror, and threat theater. It also needs the built-in reversal: overreliance on dramatic moves makes routine moves impossible and eventually makes the dramatic move expected.
