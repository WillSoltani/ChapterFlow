Greene's nonengagement strategy says that when the opponent is stronger, the mistake is fighting on their chosen terms. Instead of standing and absorbing superior force, the operator yields ground, withdraws, and trades territory for time. That time is then used to rebuild, find better ground, or wait for the stronger side to overextend under the cost of holding what it took.

The chapter's logic depends on strategic depth. The side using nonengagement must have something it can afford to give up without losing the campaign outright. Time becomes an ally only when withdrawal preserves future capacity instead of ending the contest immediately.

The modern translation is retreat from a market segment, a high-status fight, or a position where the stronger side dictates conditions. The reversal is severe: if there is no space to trade, there is no nonengagement strategy. A single-contract business or a fragile one-relationship reputation may not survive yielding at all.
