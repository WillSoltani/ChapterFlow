Greene's deterrence strategy says the cheapest victory is the one where the opponent decides not to attack. The operator cultivates a visible reputation, posture, or capability that makes the cost of striking look too high to a rational opponent. Deterrence therefore works before the exchange begins. It shapes the other side's calculation so the battle never opens.

The chapter is careful about one hard condition: deterrence is not bluff. A threatening presence that is not backed by real capacity collapses the first time it is tested, and once that collapse is public the reputation is harder to rebuild. The threatened response has to be credible.

The modern translation is strategic presence in markets, negotiations, and professional settings. The reversal matters too. Deterrence is defensive. If someone lives in deterrence mode forever, they stop growing into new ground. The strategy fits periods of consolidation better than periods of expansion.
