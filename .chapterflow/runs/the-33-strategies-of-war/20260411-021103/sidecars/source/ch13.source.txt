Greene's intelligence chapter says that most failed attacks collapse because the operator moved against an imagined opponent rather than the real one. The other side's motives, resources, alliances, fears, and vulnerabilities usually differ from the surface story, and action taken on the surface story often strikes the wrong target.

The practical skill is to spend time before the move learning what the opponent actually wants, what they are trying to protect, what they can truly do, and where their internal tensions sit. This work feels slow and unglamorous compared with the move itself, but Greene treats it as the thing that makes the move land.

The reversal keeps the chapter from becoming paralysis. Intelligence-gathering can become a way to postpone action forever. At some point the operator has to move on incomplete knowledge, using the best picture available rather than waiting for impossible certainty.
