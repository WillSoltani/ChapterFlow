Core Claim:
The first control trap shows that once people gain enough leverage to pursue more control, outside forces may resist that move. Newport argues that attractive freedom moves do not happen in a vacuum. Markets, employers, and other stakeholders can push back when a person's autonomy would cost them value. The chapter should make resistance visible without drifting into grand theory beyond the narrow leverage-and-control logic already established.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
This is the first concrete failure mode of Rule 3. It shifts from why control matters to why gaining control can trigger resistance.

Author Logic Chain:
First the author identifies a control move that looks reasonable from the inside.
Then the author shows that others may resist because the person has become valuable.
Then the author concludes that resistance can be evidence of leverage rather than proof the desire for control was foolish.

Required Anchors:
- the first control trap as resistance to gained autonomy
- the idea that value attracts pushback when someone tries to leave or reshape terms
- the link between leverage and resistance

Frameworks or Terms Introduced:
First control trap plus how it works: when a person tries to convert leverage into more control, the value they provide gives others reasons to resist the change.

Specific Applications:
- interpreting employer or stakeholder resistance
- distinguishing resistance from evidence of leverage
- seeing why freedom moves can become contested

Common Misreadings:
- resistance always means the move is wrong
- resistance means control should never be pursued

Counterarguments or Limits:
Do not imply that all resistance proves the move is wise. Keep the chapter focused on resistance as a signal to interpret carefully, not as automatic validation.
