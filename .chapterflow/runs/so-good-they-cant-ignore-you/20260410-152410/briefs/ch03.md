Core Claim:
After showing that preexisting passion is weak evidence and rare as a starting condition, this chapter turns to harm. Newport argues that the passion mindset makes people monitor work for signs of perfect fit, which amplifies anxiety, dissatisfaction, and restless comparison. The problem is not that people want meaningful work. The problem is that passion-first logic gives ordinary friction too much diagnostic power.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
This is the emotional-cost chapter. It is about what the passion mindset does to perception while you are still working, learning, and enduring normal ambiguity.

Author Logic Chain:
First the author names the passion mindset as a way of looking at work.
Then the author shows how that mindset turns uncertainty and friction into evidence of mismatch.
Then the author concludes that the mindset itself can make work feel worse and keep people restless.

Required Anchors:
- Newport's framing of the student passion problem, including stress and burnout caused by treating early interests as obligations.
- The contrast between developing passions and assuming they already exist.
- The pattern of over-monitoring jobs for signs of perfect fit.

Specific Applications:

- reducing job-hopping driven by identity panic
- choosing development over constant calling checks

Common Misreadings:

Counterarguments or Limits:
