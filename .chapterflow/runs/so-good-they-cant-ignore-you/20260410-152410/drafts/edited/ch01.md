Steve Jobs stood before Stanford graduates and gave them the kind of sentence people want from a hero: find work you love. The line is clean, portable, and perfectly shaped for a career panic. Even when people do not quote Jobs directly, they often inherit his script. Somewhere out there is the right calling. Your job is to identify it early, then arrange your life around it.

The advice feels convincing because Jobs seems to embody it. His public story has all the right ingredients for a modern origin myth: dropping out, wandering into calligraphy, building Apple, getting fired, returning, and ending as a founder whose history can be retold as destiny. Success does that to memory. The experiments begin to look like clues. The detours start reading like obedience to an inner design that was visible from the beginning.

Newport's opening move is to interrupt that reading. Jobs may be an inspiring figure, but he is weak evidence for the passion hypothesis. A gripping story can still be bad methodology. Jobs could describe what he loved after the path had worked. That does not tell a young worker what to trust before they have skill, leverage, or even a decent sample of what different kinds of work feel like. It tells them what a successful survivor sounds like once the mess has been reorganized into narrative.

That matters because the passion hypothesis demands certainty too early. It tells people to locate a preexisting calling and use it as the main filter for career decisions. If they cannot name it, they read the uncertainty as failure. They keep searching, keep second-guessing, or keep abandoning work the moment discomfort arrives. Jobs's story intensifies that pattern because it makes early certainty look noble. An inspiring biography starts doing the work of a diagnostic instrument.

The trouble is not admiration. The trouble is inference. Even in the narrow facts frozen for this run, Jobs's path was nonlinear. It involved sampling, building, loss, return, and later reinterpretation. That makes it a strong story about exploration under changing conditions. It does not make it strong proof that satisfying work begins with a clearly named passion. The smoother the story sounds in retelling, the less useful it becomes as early-stage guidance.

Newport is not arguing that interest is fake or that good work should feel hollow. He is making a tighter point. When the evidence is weak, passion should stop acting like a prerequisite. Deep interest may show up later, but later is the crucial word. It may grow after competence, after exposure, after progress begins to feel real. Treating it as the starting requirement turns ordinary uncertainty into a personal defect.

So the chapter ends by removing a false certainty, not by offering a finished replacement. If Steve Jobs is the wrong mascot for passion-first planning, what kind of evidence should guide you before your path feels obvious? That question is more demanding than the slogan it replaces. It is also more honest. Once the myth loosens its grip, the next issue becomes impossible to avoid: how rare is the preexisting passion people are being told to discover?
