Core Claim:
Chapter 9 argues that interest is not strong enough to sustain execution when the work becomes costly, inconvenient, or uncomfortable. The chapter says commitment is different because it holds when preference weakens and comfort starts bargaining. Its job is to separate casual wanting from durable follow-through.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
This is the commitment-distinction chapter, not the ownership chapter and not yet the later commitments-to-self-and-others chapter. It has to show why execution depends on something stronger than interest without drifting into later commitment systems, promises-to-others mechanics, or generic motivational slogans.

Author Logic Chain:
First the chapter separates interest from commitment.
Then it shows why interest collapses when conditions turn unfavorable.
Then it frames commitment as what continues to act anyway.
Then it prepares the reader for present-tense execution in the next chapter.

Required Anchors:
- The chapter title itself: Interest versus Commitment.
- The skeleton intent that the chapter separates casual preference from durable commitment.
- The Chapter 8 bridge question about whether ownership holds when comfort pushes back.
- The broader execution logic that durable action needs something stronger than temporary desire.

Frameworks or Terms Introduced:
- interest: preferring the outcome while conditions feel favorable

Specific Applications:
- a manager follows through on a key task after enthusiasm fades
- a student studies when the mood is gone but the work still matters
- a personal habit survives the point where it stops feeling attractive

Common Misreadings:
- treating commitment as intense emotion
- assuming interest automatically becomes execution
- confusing short bursts of motivation with durable resolve

Counterarguments or Limits:
The chapter should preserve the limit that commitment is not theatrical intensity. It is a steadier willingness to act when comfort resists.
