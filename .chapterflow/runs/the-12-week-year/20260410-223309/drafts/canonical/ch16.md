Execution can feel intense and still remain dishonest if nothing keeps score. That is the pressure Chapter 16 takes up. Moran and Lennington move from process control into visible measurement because routines can create the appearance of seriousness while hiding whether the work is actually producing results.

The chapter title matters because it strips away the comfort of impression management. Keeping Score says the reader needs more than effort, activity, or a disciplined mood. The reader needs a visible signal that reveals whether the system is working. The chapter treats scorekeeping as a thinking shift because honest measurement changes behavior in a way that vague self-report rarely does.

That shift matters at the individual level first. A reader can feel committed, can work hard, and can still protect weak execution by trusting effort instead of evidence. Scorekeeping matters because it makes progress harder to romanticize. Once the score is visible, the reader has less room to confuse motion with advancement.

The chapter widens through Team Application. Shared work becomes easier to distort when everyone relies on anecdotes, confidence, or selective updates. A visible score gives the team a common point of contact with reality. It makes drift easier to spot and excuses harder to preserve.

The limit matters too. The chapter is not glorifying numbers for their own sake. It is not defending vanity metrics, decorative dashboards, or measurement that looks precise while saying nothing useful about execution. Scorekeeping matters only when the score reveals something the reader or team would otherwise prefer to blur.

That is why Common Pitfalls and Success Tips belongs here. The pitfall is not only avoiding scorekeeping altogether. It is also choosing a score that flatters effort instead of exposing results. The success side is not data obsession. It is a willingness to let visible feedback correct the story the reader wants to tell about progress.

The harder warning sits underneath the chapter. Readers often avoid score because score removes interpretation room. As long as effort, intention, and optimism stay unmeasured, the system can feel better than it is. Visible measurement threatens that comfort by asking whether the process is actually producing what the plan promised.

That pressure leads directly into the next chapter. Once scorekeeping makes reality visible, what tradeoffs and intentional imbalance does the reader have to accept to keep the most important commitments moving?
