Seeing the truth about execution still does not protect the next week. That is the pressure Chapter 7 takes up. Moran and Lennington move from scorekeeping into intentionality because a reader can know what matters and still keep letting weaker demands crowd it out. Truth without protection stays vulnerable.

Intentionality names the chapter because execution has to be guarded deliberately. It is not enough to admire priorities after the scorecard exposes the gap. The reader has to decide what gets access to time and attention and what does not. Otherwise every interruption, request, and lower-value opportunity keeps negotiating its way back into the week.

The Importance of Yes and No makes that mechanism explicit. Saying yes is not neutral when execution capacity is limited. Every yes gives something permission to occupy time that could have belonged to the higher priority. The chapter therefore treats no as part of follow-through rather than as a social inconvenience. Selective refusal is one way important work survives.

Blocking Your Time extends the same logic into the calendar. A protected block turns intention into something the week can actually defend. Without that protection, important work keeps waiting for leftovers. The chapter is not arguing for decorative blocks that disappear at the first inconvenience. It is arguing that what matters needs time claimed for it in advance.

This does not mean rigid control for its own sake. The chapter is strongest when it treats intentionality as selective protection rather than as obsession. The point is not to become mechanically unavailable. The point is to stop letting every demand pretend it deserves equal access to execution time.

That is why Chapter 7 belongs after truth-telling. Chapter 6 exposed the gap between effort and evidence. Chapter 7 asks what will now protect the right work so the score changes honestly. It moves the reader from knowing the problem to defending the solution.

The chapter leaves one more question alive. Once the work has been protected by better choices and better use of time, who owns the follow-through when the week gets difficult anyway? That is the accountability question Chapter 8 raises next.
