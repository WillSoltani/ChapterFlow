Protecting the work still does not answer the whole execution problem. That is the pressure Chapter 8 takes up. Moran and Lennington move from intentionality into accountability because a calendar block and a defended priority can still fail when the reader treats follow-through as something another person, another deadline, or another burst of pressure is supposed to create.

The chapter title matters because it reframes accountability as ownership. Accountability is not strongest when it means supervision, blame, or the fear of getting caught falling short. It is strongest when the reader treats the action and the result as personally owned. The work stops being something that should happen and becomes something the reader is responsible for carrying.

That distinction changes the role of outside pressure. External reminders, expectations, and consequences can expose a gap, but they do not create durable ownership by themselves. A reader who is waiting to be pushed is still acting as though responsibility lives somewhere else. The chapter therefore shifts accountability inward without pretending that circumstances stop being hard.

Ownership matters most when execution becomes uncomfortable. Protected time can still feel inconvenient. The task can still feel boring, uncertain, or costly. Chapter 8 says accountability becomes real at that point, when the reader stops asking who will make the action happen and starts acting as though the answer is already settled.

This does not mean harsh self-judgment. The chapter is not arguing that accountability is a permission slip for shame. Ownership is a stance toward action, not a ritual of self-attack. The point is to remove the excuse that follow-through depends on someone else applying enough pressure.

That is why Chapter 8 follows intentionality so closely. Chapter 7 protected the work from being crowded out. Chapter 8 asks who owns doing the work once the protection is there. It turns accountability from an external force into an internal responsibility.

The chapter leaves another sharper question waiting. If accountability is now owned, what separates a passing interest from a real commitment when comfort and inconvenience collide? That is the line Chapter 9 will draw next.
