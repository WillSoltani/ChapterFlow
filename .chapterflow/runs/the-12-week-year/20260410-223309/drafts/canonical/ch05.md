A 12 week plan can be strong and still fail to govern action when the week begins. That is the pressure Chapter 5 addresses. Moran and Lennington move from the cycle-level plan to the current week because execution does not happen at the level of broad intention alone. A quarter priority can remain perfectly clear and still lose ground if nothing pulls it into this week's decisions.

The chapter title, One Week at a Time, names that shift in unit. The point is not to shrink ambition. The point is to make execution current. A 12 week plan creates focus across the cycle, but the weekly plan is what makes that focus operational. Without a weekly bridge, the plan stays real in theory and weak in the moment where competing demands actually arrive.

The Weekly Plan is the chapter's central mechanism. It translates the larger priorities into actions that belong to the present week. That translation matters because a 12 week goal is still too far away to govern the week by itself. The reader needs a structure that says what this week is for before the week gets filled by noise, urgency, and convenience.

That is why weekly planning is more than making a list. A weak weekly list simply records everything that seems pressing. A strong weekly plan reflects the 12 week priorities and gives them current form. It answers a practical question: what has to live inside this week if the 12 week plan is going to stay alive at all? The chapter's logic is selective rather than exhaustive. The week should be shaped by priorities, not overwhelmed by every possible task.

The chapter also protects itself from drifting into a different error. Weekly planning is not yet scorekeeping. The week is where the plan becomes active, but Chapter 6 still has to test whether execution is actually happening. Here the chapter's job is narrower. It makes the plan current enough to guide real choices while there is still time to act on them.

This weekly bridge matters because real demands do not arrive quarterly. They arrive on Monday, Wednesday, and Friday. They arrive in meetings, interruptions, low-energy hours, and unexpected requests. A 12 week plan that never enters the week remains vulnerable to all of them. A weekly plan gives the reader a chance to decide in advance what the current week should protect.

The chapter leaves one practical pressure in place for the next step. Once the week has been shaped around the right priorities, how does the reader confront whether the week actually produced the intended results? That is the truth-telling problem Chapter 6 picks up.
