A written 12 week plan can still die quietly if nothing in the week forces it back into contact with action. That is the pressure Chapter 15 takes up. Moran and Lennington move from plan design into process control because execution weakens when commitments stay on paper and never enter a repeatable operating rhythm.

The chapter title matters because it names the missing move. Installing Process Control says the reader does not need more admiration for the plan. The reader needs a structure that keeps the plan alive while the week gets crowded. Making Sure Things Get Done frames the chapter as a problem of control, not intention alone.

That is why Weekly Plans and The Weekly Routine matter so much. A 12 week plan points the direction, but weekly plans force the reader to decide what must move now. The routine matters because execution decays when planning becomes occasional, improvised, or mood-based. Process control means the plan keeps reappearing at a regular interval instead of fading behind urgency.

The chapter sharpens further with Don't Go It Alone. Readers often protect themselves by keeping commitments private. A private plan can stay flattering long after it stops guiding behavior. Accountability matters here because another person, or another team, makes drift easier to see and harder to excuse.

The limit matters too. The chapter is not praising ritual for its own sake. It is not arguing for bureaucratic check-ins, calendar theater, or rigid repetition detached from results. Process control matters only when the weekly plan, the routine, and the accountability structure keep the real commitments exposed to action.

That is where Thinking Shift, Team Application, and Common Pitfalls do useful work. The shift is from hoping the plan will hold to assuming it will decay unless something keeps touching it every week. Team Application widens the point beyond individual discipline. Common Pitfalls keeps the chapter honest by implying that routine can become ceremonial if it loses contact with execution.

The harder warning sits underneath the chapter. Readers can write a strong plan and still fail because they never install the weekly controls that test whether the plan is alive. They keep treating the plan as evidence of seriousness while avoiding the repeated exposure that would show whether the work is actually moving.

That pressure leads directly into the next chapter. If weekly process control is finally in place, what score tells the reader whether the system is working or merely producing the appearance of movement?
