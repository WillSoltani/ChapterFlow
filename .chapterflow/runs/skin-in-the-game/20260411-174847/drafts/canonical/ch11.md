# Facta non Verba (Deeds Before Words)

Taleb's point in this chapter is that talk is cheap precisely because it can be produced without much exposure to consequence. A person can declare conviction, loyalty, courage, competence, or generosity with very little cost. The chapter keeps returning to a harder test: what does the person actually do when action carries downside, sacrifice, or risk?

That is why deeds outrank words here. Speech may still communicate intention, but it does not reveal much until the speaker is forced to pay for what they claim. Taleb treats action under consequence as a better signal because it is harder to fake. Once a person must give something up, absorb risk, or enter the blast radius, the claim begins to acquire real evidentiary weight.

This chapter follows naturally from the earlier attacks on theater, prestige, and borrowed preference. Chapter 9 distrusted cosmetic expertise. Chapter 10 distrusted preferences imported from status. Chapter 11 now asks what remains once speech itself is put under audit. Taleb's answer is that revealed preference lives in action. What a person actually chooses under pressure tells us more than what they say in safety.

So the chapter is not generic praise of action for its own sake. It is about evidence. A deed matters because it exposes the actor to consequence in a way words often do not. Loyalty, belief, competence, and sincerity all become more legible when something real is at stake.

That is why "deeds before words" fits the book's wider symmetry rule. Taleb keeps searching for signals that are expensive to fake and constrained by reality. In this chapter, action under risk is one of the strongest of those signals.
