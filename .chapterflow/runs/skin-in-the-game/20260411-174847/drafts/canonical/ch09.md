# Surgeons Should Not Look like Surgeons

Taleb's point in this chapter is not really about clothes. It is about how easily people confuse the theater of expertise with expertise itself. In uncertain domains, polished appearance, ritualized manner, and professional costume can become shortcuts that flatter observers into trust before any real test of competence has occurred.

That concern fits naturally after Lindy. Chapter 8 used time as a credibility filter. Chapter 9 asks what happens when trust gets assigned through cosmetic signal instead. Taleb is suspicious of signals that look reassuring but are too easy to manufacture. If a marker can be staged for the audience without requiring much exposure to consequences, it tells us less than people want to believe.

So the chapter pushes the reader away from theatrical authority and toward practical signal. Real competence tends to leave traces that matter more than costume: scars, consequence-linked judgment, performance under pressure, and a style that is often less ornamental than outsiders expect. Taleb's deeper claim is that people who truly do the work are not always the ones best optimized for ceremonial credibility.

This extends the book's wider attack on insulated experts. Earlier chapters targeted prestige without downside, theory without exposure, and status detached from correction. Here the same suspicion lands on appearance itself. A polished professional identity may still accompany real skill, but it should not be trusted just because it performs the role convincingly. The signal becomes meaningful only when it remains tied to reality-testing.

So the chapter is less anti-professional than anti-theater. Taleb wants trust to lean toward demonstrated competence rather than toward the costume of expertise. The issue is not whether someone looks impressive. It is whether the look is carrying earned information or just helping spectators outsource judgment.
