# The Facts Are True, the News Is Fake

Taleb's point in this chapter is that a set of true facts can still be arranged into a misleading picture. The problem is not always literal falsehood. It is often the packaging: which events get selected, what gets emphasized, what gets omitted, and how the story is framed for the audience.

That is why the chapter separates facts from news. Facts belong to reality before interpretation. News is a constructed product built from salience, sequencing, narrative shape, and emotional relevance. Even when each included fact is technically true, the assembled story can still distort what matters by exaggerating the vivid, the recent, or the narratively convenient.

This chapter continues the book's wider suspicion of polished mediation. Earlier chapters attacked counterfeit expertise, status-shaped desire, and signals that look stronger than they are. Chapter 12 extends the same skepticism to information packaging. Taleb wants the reader to distrust the leap from event to narrated event, because the leap is where selection bias and narrative pressure begin doing hidden work.

So the chapter is not merely saying that journalists lie. It is making a sharper point about distortion. A mediated account can remain technically factual while still pulling judgment away from underlying reality. Once salience outruns proportion, the audience begins reacting to narrative construction more than to the world itself.

That is why "the facts are true, the news is fake" fits the book's broader realism test. Taleb keeps asking what contact with reality survives after filters, costumes, and packaging intervene. In this chapter, the warning is that true details do not guarantee a truthful picture.
