# Only the Rich Are Poisoned: The Preferences of Others

Taleb's claim in this chapter is that wealth and status do not simply buy freedom. They can also buy distance from one's own direct preferences. When people become insulated from practical need, discomfort, and ordinary trial, they can grow more vulnerable to desires imported from prestige, fashion, or the expectations of other people.

That is why the chapter is not just about luxury. It is about preference formation under asymmetry. A person with enough cushioning can begin to want things not because they are useful, satisfying, or naturally chosen, but because status systems, refined taste markets, or elite cues tell them what should be wanted. Taleb is interested in the corruption of judgment that comes from that distance.

The phrase about being poisoned points to contamination rather than simple abundance. The problem is not merely having more options. It is being surrounded by signals that replace firsthand evaluation with socially manufactured appetite. What looks like sophistication can therefore become a kind of dependence. The richer or more insulated person may be less connected to what is actually good for them and more responsive to prestige-coded suggestion.

This extends the book's broader hostility to mediated reality. Earlier chapters criticized experts who speak without downside and institutions that reward surface signal over lived exposure. Here the same logic enters taste. Preference becomes suspicious when it no longer belongs to the chooser in a direct way but is assembled through status pressure and distance from practical consequence.

So the chapter is not cheap resentment toward wealth. Taleb is asking whether a desire is owned or imported. A preference that survives direct contact, use, and lived consequence has more legitimacy than one borrowed from the preferences of others and maintained by insulation.
