# Why Each One Should Eat His Own Turtles

"You who caught the turtles better eat them." Taleb opens with a meal nobody wants. A group of fishermen catches turtles, cooks them, then realizes too late that the feast is a mistake. When Mercury passes by, they try to solve the problem by handing the bad meal to him. He refuses the arrangement and forces them to eat what they brought. The story is memorable because it turns fairness into something physical. This is not a lecture about virtue in the abstract. It is a plate being pushed across a table, and a refusal to let the person with no part in the decision carry the consequence. The chapter keeps returning to that image because it is the cleanest version of the rule: if you create the risk, catch the animal, or set the terms, you do not get to stand outside the downside once it arrives.

That rule matters most in situations that do not look dramatic at first. Taleb moves from turtles to the kind of helpful advice people give when they benefit from your decision more than you do. He describes a lecture agent who kept telling him that using an agent would be "good" for him, that it would simplify life and free him for better things. The pitch worked well enough for Taleb to use him on a foreign booking. Years later, a tax problem surfaced, and the man who had sold the convenience answered with a cold line: "I am not your tax attorney." The chapter does not treat this as a quirky anecdote about one evasive operator. It treats it as a pattern. Advice starts to smell when the speaker gets the commission, the convenience, or the social benefit at the moment of choice, then vanishes when the hidden cost arrives later. Skin in the game is a way of testing speech by exposure. If someone wants to shape your action while remaining insulated from the downside, trust should fall before the pitch rises.

Taleb sharpens the point by moving into the sales culture of a white-shoe investment bank, where elegance on the surface hides a much rougher moral structure underneath. The firm cultivated an image of ethics and professionalism, but the real job of the sales desk was often to unload inventory the traders no longer wanted on their own books. Clients were softened with dinners, expensive wine, urgency, and the language of opportunity. The insider needed the risk gone. The outsider was told the product was right for him. One salesman reduces the whole arrangement to a grim rule: "Rip them off, don't tick them off." Another line cuts even deeper: "Remember that every day a new customer is born." Those lines matter because they show what asymmetry looks like once it becomes a business model. The seller knows why the trade is being pushed. The buyer hears a story about fit, timing, and upside. One side is managing exposure. The other side is being handed it.

From there the chapter widens into the ancient question of disclosure. Taleb revisits the Rhodian corn problem reported by Cicero: if a merchant brings grain to famine-struck Rhodes while knowing other ships are close behind with more grain, how much does he owe the buyer by way of disclosure? He sides with the stronger transparency tradition, not because trade itself is corrupt, but because the moral fault line is clear once one side knows materially more than the other. The same issue appears in simpler form when a seller knows that wine is spoiling or that merchandise is defective. Caveat emptor may survive as a slogan in some systems, but Taleb's pressure point is harder than that. A transaction turns ugly when one person has certainty, or near certainty, about the defect, the incentive, or the likely harm while the other person is left to carry uncertainty alone. Equality in uncertainty does not mean equal knowledge in every detail. It means one side should not enjoy protected clarity while the other inherits the fog.

This is why the chapter leans on asymmetry rather than on vague appeals to honesty. Taleb uses gharar from Sharia as one example of a rule set trying to limit transactions where one side knows too much about the defect or outcome. He is not pretending every culture or legal tradition draws the line in the same place. In fact, the force of the chapter depends on the opposite truth: these lines are contested, partial, and hard to police perfectly. That is the limit that keeps the chapter from turning into a sermon. Markets cannot remove uncertainty. Sellers will always know some things buyers do not. Persuasion is not automatically fraud. The harder question, and the one the chapter refuses to let go, is where incomplete information turns into protected advantage. At what point does a recommendation stop being ordinary salesmanship and become a way of feeding someone else a turtle you already know tastes bad?

Rav Safra gives Taleb his cleanest answer. A buyer approaches while Rav Safra is praying silently, makes an offer, and then raises the price when he receives no response. Rav Safra still feels bound by the first price because, inwardly, he had already accepted it. Taleb presents the story as a deeper kind of transparency than defect disclosure alone. Not only should the merchandise be honestly represented. The seller's own intention matters. That standard is severe, almost uncomfortable in how far it pushes beyond modern transactional reflexes, which is precisely why it carries weight here. It exposes the difference between squeezing every possible advantage from a counterpart and building a form of trade you could repeat without shame. A deal can be legal, profitable, and still corrode the conditions that make repeated dealing honorable. Once the other side discovers that your gain depended on concealment, your cleverness stops looking like intelligence and starts looking like rot.

So the chapter's real achievement is not that it tells you to be nice. It gives you a diagnostic. Look at the transaction and ask who can walk away clean if the bet goes bad. Ask who is recommending, who is exposed, who knows more, and who has to absorb the damage when the story sold at the front end collapses at the back end. That is the ethical core Taleb wants fixed in your head before the book moves on. A world full of hidden asymmetry does not only produce unfair deals. It produces people, firms, and institutions that learn they can profit while someone else eats the consequences. Once that becomes normal at the level of one transaction, the next question is how it scales. If one person can push downside onto another, what happens when a small group learns how to make an entire system bend around its own refusal?
