# How to Legally Own Another Person

Taleb opens this chapter with a group of wandering monks who are almost impossible to own. The gyrovagues move from place to place, live lightly, and depend on no single institution long enough to be housebroken by it. That is exactly why organized religion disliked them. A monastery needs obedience, schedule, and hierarchy. Truly free people are hard to fit inside a rule-bound machine because they can leave before the machine can train them. Taleb starts there to make a larger claim: institutions do not merely hire labor or gather believers. They try to reduce freedom to the point where dependability becomes predictable.

That is why the chapter pivots quickly from monks to firms. A company does not want abstract autonomy from the people on whom it depends. It wants reliability under pressure. Taleb's pilot example makes the problem vivid. A contractor named Bob has signed a clean legal agreement to fly a profitable Oktoberfest route, but then a richer offer arrives from a Saudi sheikh and Bob walks away. The contract may contain penalties, but the penalties are too small compared with the attraction of the outside option. At that moment the firm discovers the difference between legal obligation and practical control. A contractor fears the law in the abstract. An employee fears losing the paycheck, position, rhythm, and future attached to the job. The firm is not buying virtue. It is buying the kind of downside that makes defection less attractive.

From there Taleb sharpens the distinction between employee and contractor. On paper the contractor looks efficient because you pay only for the task and keep flexibility. In reality the employee may be cheaper at the exact moment when dependability matters. Employees are expensive because you support them even when there is no perfect task ready, yet they are dependable because they have more skin in the game. They fear dismissal more than a contractor fears a lawsuit. The disciplined worker has accepted daily punctuality, someone else's schedule, and a continuing system of evaluation. Employment becomes evidence of domestication. Taleb's tone is intentionally abrasive here because he wants to show that smooth organizational life often depends on people surrendering meaningful portions of their freedom.

The chapter then widens from the single employer to the broader idea of the company man. For much of the twentieth century, a person could become so bound to a corporation that leaving it felt like exile. Dress, humor, language, friendships, and status all carried the firm's stamp. When big companies lasted for decades, that trade could look safe. But as firms became less durable, the old company man did not disappear so much as mutate into what Taleb calls the companies person: someone owned not by one employer alone but by the need to remain employable across an industry. The pressure becomes wider and subtler. You do not merely avoid displeasing your boss. You avoid becoming the kind of person future bosses might distrust. Reputation itself becomes a leash.

That is where Coase enters the chapter. Coase explains why firms exist by pointing to transaction costs. It is costly to negotiate every tiny exchange through explicit contracts, so firms internalize many activities and run them administratively. Taleb does not reject that explanation. He treats it as incomplete. A firm is not only a transaction-cost solution. It is also a risk-management device. The owner prefers arrangements in which crucial people have enough to lose that they behave dependably when stress arrives. That is why Taleb reaches for harsher historical examples, including Roman households using slaves as treasurers. The point is not nostalgia or moral approval. It is that downside could be loaded onto the steward more heavily than onto a free outsider, reducing risk for the owner. The argument is ugly because the mechanism is ugly.

Complexity makes the problem sharper rather than milder. In a long chain of specialized tasks, one missing step can freeze the whole process. That means firms often need even more reliability than simpler enterprises did. A supposedly modern flexible system still depends on people who will not disappear at the critical moment. Taleb's point is that freedom has operational costs. A person with too many outside options, too little dependence, or too little fear of future exclusion may be admirable in one sense and unusable in another. Institutions solve that tension not by celebrating liberty but by engineering attachment. The more fragile the process, the more valuable obedience becomes.

The expat example reveals the softer side of ownership. A company sends someone abroad with a villa, driver, club membership, school access, and visible rank. On paper the employee is privileged, not trapped. But Taleb's point is that privilege itself can become captivity when the person becomes addicted to the status and dreads returning to an ordinary life. Once your social world, comforts, and self-image all depend on institutional favor, you can be owned without chains. You do not need to be threatened every morning. It is enough that leaving would feel like a collapse in identity. The company has learned to bind you with rewards instead of punishments, but the mechanism is still downside.

So the chapter ends with a brutal but clarifying distinction. Formal freedom is not the same thing as practical freedom. A person may sign contracts voluntarily, hold a respectable title, and still be shaped into obedience by fear of unemployment, fear of status loss, fear of reputational exile, or fear of losing the life that institutions have financed. Taleb is not saying every employee is a slave in disguise. He is saying that modern organizations function by creating gradients of ownability. The more you have to lose from disobedience, the easier you are to run. Chapter 3 matters because it takes the language of liberty and asks a colder question beneath it: free to do what, if the cost of doing it is too high to bear?
