# How to Legally Own Another Person

Taleb opens with wandering monks because they show what institutions cannot comfortably absorb. The gyrovagues move lightly, attach themselves to no stable hierarchy, and can leave before anyone can train them into obedience. Organized religion disliked them for that reason. A monastery needs schedule, supervision, and rules that stick. Taleb uses the contrast to make the chapter's real point: institutions do not merely gather labor or loyalty. They reduce freedom until dependability becomes usable.

That is why he shifts from monks to firms. A company does not mainly want noble autonomy from the people it relies on. It wants reliability at the exact moment things can break. The pilot example makes this plain. A contractor signs a legal agreement to fly an Oktoberfest route, then receives a richer outside offer and walks away. The contract exists, but the contract is weaker than the temptation. At that moment the firm discovers the difference between legal obligation and practical control. A contractor fears a penalty in the abstract. An employee fears losing paycheck, routine, position, and future. The firm is not buying goodness. It is buying downside.

From there Taleb sharpens the employee versus contractor distinction. Contractors look efficient because they preserve flexibility and avoid ongoing cost. Employees look expensive because you carry them even when demand is uneven. Yet employees are often what you buy when dependability matters more than local efficiency. They have more skin in the game. They fear dismissal more than contractors fear lawsuits. They have already accepted somebody else's schedule, daily evaluation, and a narrower range of practical disobedience. Taleb deliberately calls this domestication. His point is abrasive but clear: smooth institutional life often depends on people surrendering real portions of their freedom.

The chapter then scales from one employer to the company man. In the older corporate world, a person could become so shaped by a firm that leaving felt like banishment. Dress, humor, language, friendships, and status all carried the company's mark. As large firms became less durable, that old identity widened into something harsher: the need to remain employable. Taleb's companies person is not owned by one boss alone, but by fear of becoming unattractive to the entire industry. Reputation becomes a leash. You stay careful not only because your current employer is watching, but because unknown future employers may be watching too.

Coase enters here as a partial explanation. Firms exist because negotiating every small task through explicit contracts is costly. Taleb accepts that and then adds the missing layer. Firms are also risk-management devices. Owners prefer arrangements in which critical people have enough to lose that they behave dependably under stress. That is why Taleb reaches for harsher historical cases, including Roman households using slaves as treasurers. The point is not moral endorsement. It is that loading more downside onto the steward lowered risk for the owner. The ugliness is the mechanism.

Modern complexity intensifies the problem. In a specialized chain, one broken link can stop everything. That means reliability becomes even more valuable, not less. A highly flexible world still depends on people who will not vanish at the critical moment. Taleb's claim is that freedom carries operational costs. Someone with too many outside options, too little dependence, or too little fear of exclusion may be admirable in one register and dangerously unreliable in another. Institutions respond by engineering attachment. The more fragile the process, the more they value obedience.

The expat example shows the softer form of ownership. A company sends someone abroad with a villa, club access, visible rank, and a life rich in perks. On paper this is privilege. In Taleb's frame it is also a trap if the person becomes addicted to the comforts and status. Once identity depends on institutional favor, chains are unnecessary. Leaving feels like a collapse in self. The company binds through rewards, but the operative force is still downside: the fear of losing what the institution has taught you to treat as normal.

So the chapter lands on a brutal distinction. Formal freedom is not practical freedom. A person may sign contracts voluntarily, hold a professional title, and still be shaped into obedience by fear of unemployment, fear of status loss, or fear of reputational exile. Taleb is not collapsing every job into literal slavery. He is identifying gradients of ownability. The more you have to lose from disobedience, the easier you are to run. That is the cold question under the whole chapter: how free are you if the price of acting freely is too high for you to pay?
