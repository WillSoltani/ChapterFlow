# Why Each One Should Eat His Own Turtles

"You who caught the turtles better eat them." Taleb opens with a meal nobody wants. A group of fishermen catches turtles, cooks them, then realizes too late that the feast is a mistake. When Mercury passes by, they try to solve the problem by handing the bad meal to him. He refuses and forces them to eat what they brought. The scene matters because it turns fairness into something physical. A plate gets pushed across the table, and the person who had no part in the decision refuses the burden. That image becomes the chapter's rule: if you create the risk, set the terms, or make the sale, you do not get to stand outside the downside when it arrives.

Taleb carries that rule into situations that look much cleaner than a ruined meal. He describes a lecture agent who kept insisting that representation would be "good" for him, that it would simplify life and remove administrative friction. The pitch sounded helpful enough for Taleb to use him on a foreign booking. Years later, a tax problem appeared, and the same man answered with a cold line: "I am not your tax attorney." The anecdote matters because it shows how asymmetry hides inside benevolent language. Advice becomes suspicious when the speaker gains at the moment of decision but disappears when the delayed cost shows up. Skin in the game is a way of testing speech by exposure. If someone wants to shape your action while staying protected from the downside, your trust should fall before your agreement rises.

The chapter sharpens once Taleb moves to the sales culture of a white-shoe investment bank. The firm projected ethics and polish, but the actual work of the sales desk often involved unloading inventory traders no longer wanted on their own books. Clients were softened with dinners, expensive wine, urgency, and the language of opportunity. The insider needed risk off the balance sheet. The outsider was told the product suited his interests. One salesman reduced the arrangement to a brutal principle: "Rip them off, don't tick them off." Another said, "Remember that every day a new customer is born." Those lines matter because they strip away the cosmetic layer. The seller knows why the trade is being pushed. The buyer hears a story about fit and upside. One side is managing exposure. The other side is being handed it.

Taleb then widens the frame to disclosure itself through the Rhodian corn problem reported by Cicero. If a merchant brings grain to famine-struck Rhodes while knowing that other ships with grain are close behind, how much must he disclose? Taleb leans toward the stronger transparency tradition because the fault line is not trade versus purity. It is hidden asymmetry. The same pressure appears when a seller knows wine is spoiling or merchandise is defective. Caveat emptor survives in some settings, but the chapter presses on a harder rule. A transaction turns ugly when one side has certainty, or near certainty, about the defect, incentive, or likely harm while the other side carries the uncertainty alone. Equality in uncertainty does not require perfect information. It requires that one side not enjoy protected clarity while the other absorbs the fog.

That is why Taleb leans on asymmetry rather than a soft appeal to honesty. He uses gharar from Sharia as one example of a rule aimed at limiting transactions where one side knows too much about the defect or outcome. He is not pretending every legal or moral system draws the line in the same place. The chapter gains force from that difficulty. Markets cannot remove uncertainty. Sellers will always know some things buyers do not. Persuasion is not automatically fraud. The live question is where incomplete information becomes protected advantage. At what point does a recommendation stop being ordinary selling and become a way of feeding someone else a turtle you already know tastes bad?

Rav Safra gives Taleb his clearest answer. A buyer approaches while Rav Safra is praying silently, makes an offer, then raises the price when silence is mistaken for hesitation. Rav Safra still feels bound by the first price because, inwardly, he had already accepted it. Taleb presents the story as a deeper form of transparency than defect disclosure alone. Not only should the merchandise be honestly represented. The seller's own intention matters. That standard is severe, which is why it carries force here. It marks the difference between squeezing every available advantage from a counterparty and building a form of trade you could repeat without shame. A deal can be legal, profitable, and still corrode the conditions that make repeated dealing honorable.

The chapter's real achievement is that it gives you a diagnostic instead of a slogan. Look at the transaction and ask who can walk away clean if the bet goes bad. Ask who is recommending, who is exposed, who knows more, and who absorbs the damage when the front-end story collapses at the back end. That is the ethical spine Taleb wants planted before the book moves on. Hidden asymmetry does not just produce unfair deals. It trains people and institutions to profit while somebody else eats the consequences. Once that pattern becomes normal inside one transaction, the next question is how it scales. If one person can push downside onto another, what happens when a small group learns how to make an entire system bend around its own refusal?
