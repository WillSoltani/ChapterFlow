# An Expert Called Lindy

Taleb's point here is that time can do a kind of filtering that abstract confidence cannot. In uncertain domains, what survives longer deserves a different kind of trust from what is merely new, elegant, or heavily promoted. The Lindy idea is not that old things are automatically right. It is that endurance under time is evidence that something has already passed through many rounds of hidden selection.

That is why the chapter belongs after the audit of hierarchy and legitimacy. Chapter 7 asked whether reward is tied to downside. Chapter 8 asks what deserves trust when direct proof is difficult, systems are uncertain, and error may be hard to calculate in advance. Taleb's answer is that time itself becomes a credibility filter. What has lived longer under pressure has, in one way or another, already survived many opportunities to fail.

The chapter therefore treats durability as a signal, not as dogma. Something time-tested may still be wrong in a specific case, and something new may still turn out to be excellent. But Taleb wants the burden of proof shifted. In uncertainty, he mistrusts novelty that has not yet faced enough reality, and he gives extra weight to things that have endured because endurance suggests resilience against many unseen stresses.

This keeps the book's broader realism test intact. Earlier chapters asked who bears the downside, who is corrected by exposure, and whether hierarchy is insulated from the harms it creates. Lindy adds another layer. If a practice, idea, tool, or institution has survived across time, that survival is itself a kind of evidence. It does not prove perfection, but it does make cheap enthusiasm for the untested look weaker.

So the chapter is not empty traditionalism. Taleb is not saying old equals sacred. He is saying survival contains information. In uncertain settings, time becomes an expert because it has already done the work of punishing many fragile things before we ever arrive to judge them.
