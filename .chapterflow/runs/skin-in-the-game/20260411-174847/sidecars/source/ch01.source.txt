## Narrow factual notes

- The chapter opens with the turtle adage: people who caught and cooked the turtles try to hand the unpleasant meal to Mercury, who instead forces them to eat what they brought.
- Taleb uses the turtle story as a governing fairness rule: you should bear what you feed, sell, recommend, or impose on others.
- He then moves to unsolicited advice that is presented as "good for you" while also benefiting the advisor, especially when harm lands on the recipient but not on the advisor.
- The lecture-agent anecdote shows a practical asymmetry: the pitch emphasized convenience for Taleb, but when a tax problem appeared later, the agent disowned the downside and gave no useful support.
- Taleb contrasts upright selling with disguised advice. Honest sellers state their own interest; manipulative sellers hide a dump or unload bad inventory under the language of help.
- In the investment-bank example, salesmen unload unwanted securities onto clients, using dinners, expensive wine, urgency, and emotional manipulation to sell inventory that the firm wants off its books.
- The line "every day a new customer is born" is presented as cynical sales logic for exploiting fresh counterparties.
- Taleb then asks how much a seller should disclose when they know more than the buyer, using the ancient Rhodes corn problem reported by Cicero.
- He contrasts Diogenes of Babylon and Antipater of Tarsus on disclosure, siding with stronger transparency rather than caveat-emptor permissiveness.
- The chapter introduces asymmetry as the core concept behind skin in the game: one party should not have certainty while the other bears uncertainty alone.
- Taleb references Sharia's prohibition on gharar as one legal attempt to prevent transactions where one party knows the defect or certainty that the other side does not.
- He then turns to Rav Safra, who refuses to take a higher price that arose while he was silently praying because his original intention had fixed a fairer price.
- Taleb presents Rav Safra as a model of deeper transparency: not only merchandise honesty, but honesty about the seller's own intention.
- The chapter closes on sustainability and shame: overcharging or stuffing clients may be profitable once, but it corrodes honorable trade and repeat trust.
