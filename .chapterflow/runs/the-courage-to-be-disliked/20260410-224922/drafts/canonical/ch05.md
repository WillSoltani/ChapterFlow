Iris is ready to send the handoff note, but she keeps rereading herself until the work goes stiff in her hands. The chapter opens there because the final problem is no longer only comparison, boundaries, or even centrality. It is the misuse of the self. A person can become so self-conscious that they stop acting cleanly and start managing their own image from the outside.

That pressure explains the heading about excessive self-consciousness stifling the self. The chapter does not attack reflection itself. It attacks the kind of self-inspection that turns the self into an object under surveillance. Once that happens, action loses freshness, and the person starts living from how they appear rather than from what they are doing.

From there the chapter turns to trust. Trust is not the same thing as confidence. Confidence says I have reasons to expect a favorable result. Trust accepts that the other person may disappoint, misunderstand, or betray me, and still chooses relationship without demanding certainty first. The distinction matters because a life built only on confidence will keep shrinking back toward control.

Work then receives a careful definition. The essence of work is contribution. Work matters because it connects a person to others through usefulness and participation, not because it grants a superior identity. The chapter keeps this line narrow so it does not become a sermon about productivity. Work is valuable as contribution, but it becomes distorted when a person tries to turn it into total self-worth.

That is why the chapter names workaholism a life-lie. The lie is not simply that someone works hard. It is that busyness can become a respectable disguise for avoiding life, relationship, uncertainty, or inner emptiness. Hard work may look serious from the outside while still functioning as escape.

The chapter then makes its final philosophical move: life is a series of moments. This is not an anti-future slogan. It is a refusal to postpone life until a complete storyline, final recognition, or guaranteed meaning arrives. A person lives only in present moments, and the quality of life depends on how those moments are inhabited.

That prepares the last claim: meaning must be given to seemingly meaningless life. The chapter does not promise a cosmic explanation that removes uncertainty. It says meaning appears through earnest living, contribution, trust, and present-tense seriousness. Meaning is enacted before it is secured.

This closes the whole book. People are not trapped by the past, not required to live by comparison, not obligated to carry other people's tasks, and not meant to build value from centrality. The final demand is to use the self well now: trust without guarantees, work as contribution, and live each moment seriously enough that meaning is created in the act.
