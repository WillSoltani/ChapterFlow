Lena thought she was encouraging the team, but halfway through the meeting she noticed the shape of what she was doing. Every compliment placed her just a little above the people receiving it. Every correction arrived from the same height. The chapter begins there because the problem is no longer just rivalry or stolen tasks. It is the social posture that keeps relationships vertical even when the language sounds warm.

The chapter first asks about the goal of interpersonal relationships. Its answer is not dominance, secure admiration, or being the center around which everyone else rotates. The goal is healthier connection shaped by contribution and shared life. That answer matters because once relationships are treated as a stage for self-importance, even kindness starts bending toward control.

From there the chapter makes a risky move: you are not the center of the world. That sentence can sound humiliating if heard badly. The chapter does not use it to crush the self. It uses it to loosen the fantasy that value requires centrality. Not being the center does not mean being worthless. It means other people are not supporting characters in your private drama.

That is why the chapter turns against rebuke and praise. Both can preserve a vertical relationship posture. Rebuke stands above in open correction, while praise stands above in approved generosity. The chapter is not denying that feedback or gratitude exist. It is exposing how easily both forms keep one person in the higher position and another in the lower one.

The positive turn arrives through contribution. A person feels value not by occupying the center, but by participating in life with others in a meaningful way. Contribution does not mean self-sacrifice without boundaries. It means being useful, present, and connected without demanding superiority or applause as payment.

The chapter then becomes demanding again: someone has to start. Horizontal relationship posture does not appear automatically. Someone has to stop using praise, stop organizing the room around themselves, and stop waiting for perfect reciprocity before acting differently. The first move may not be rewarded immediately, but the chapter says it still matters.

This would become soft if it ended with a community slogan. It does not. Once value is grounded in contribution rather than centrality, the next question becomes how to trust, work, and live seriously in the present without turning life back into a ladder. That is why the final night can speak about work, trust, and the here and now without sounding like a detached sermon.
