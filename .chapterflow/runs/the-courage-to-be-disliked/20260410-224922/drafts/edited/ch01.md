Nia had already apologized by the time the meeting ended, but she kept repeating the same sentence: this is what happens when you grow up around shouting. Everyone in the room let it stand because it sounded humane and plausible. It gave the outburst a history. What it did not answer was the harder question: did that history fully govern what happened at 10:14 on a Tuesday morning?

That is the first night's real pressure. People cling to fatalism not only because it seems accurate, but because it reduces the burden of freedom. If the past fully explains the present, then today's anger, avoidance, or retreat can be grieved without being interrupted. The chapter pushes against that relief. Its opening hinge is severe: if people can change, then the past cannot be the whole story of what they are doing now.

The claim only works if it stays precise. The chapter is not saying pain is imaginary. It is changing the question. Instead of asking only what event caused a pattern, it asks what purpose the pattern serves now. That is why "People Fabricate Anger" is such a jolt. The point is not that anger is fake. The point is that anger can function. It can end a conversation, regain height in a room, postpone embarrassment, or keep another person far enough away that a deeper risk never has to be taken.

Once that lens appears, other behaviors look different too. A graduate student who says panic proves she can never speak in public may also be preserving herself from the humiliation of failing in full view. A manager who blames every tense exchange on childhood criticism may also be protecting himself from the sharper work of changing how he enters conflict. The chapter is not ridiculing either person. It is refusing to let the explanatory story become sacred.

The inward turn is even less comfortable. Why hold so tightly to self-dislike? One answer is that self-dislike can be protective. If you keep announcing that you are the kind of person who will never be chosen, trusted, or loved well, then you do not have to step into the riskier territory where change would require new action. A cramped identity can wound you and still feel safer than possibility.

This is also where the chapter can become cruel if handled lazily. "Deny Trauma" becomes a bad slogan the moment it is used to erase suffering or talk as if constraint does not exist. The stronger reading is narrower and harder: painful history is real, but it does not automatically deserve final authority over the meaning of your present life. The chapter does not ask the reader to stop hurting. It asks the reader to stop treating explanation as destiny.

That shift makes freedom more demanding, not less. If the past is not your final ruler, then your anger, your retreat, and even your self-hatred start looking less like prison walls and more like patterns with a purpose. That is frightening because it makes change imaginable. It also opens the next question the book needs. If people are not simply trapped by what happened before, what are they organizing themselves around when they struggle with other people now?
