Some organizations wait for certainty as if certainty were the entrance fee for action. They hold meetings, ask for more proof, and quietly assume that the right path should be visible before anyone starts moving. Chapter 7 breaks with that instinct. Durable institutions often learn their way forward by trying many things and then keeping what actually proves useful.

That does not mean the chapter celebrates randomness. The title Try a Lot of Stuff and Keep What Works sounds casual, but its logic is disciplined. The company is not supposed to thrash. It is supposed to generate variation, notice results, and retain what strengthens the institution. Experimentation matters here because prediction has limits. Teams rarely know enough in advance to design the perfect path from the center of a conference room.

The chapter follows naturally from what came before. Chapter 6 argued that strong culture gives an institution coherence. Chapter 7 asks how a coherent institution can keep changing without dissolving into drift. The answer is that the core stays stable while the methods stay open to trial. The company does not experiment with its purpose every week. It experiments with the paths that may help the purpose travel farther.

That is why trying many things is not the same as chasing novelty. The chapter is about learning under uncertainty. A serious institution can run multiple tests, compare outcomes, and notice which moves deserve more commitment. The learning comes not from activity by itself but from remembered judgment. Trials matter only if the organization can tell what improved reality and what merely created motion.

This is the hard edge of the chapter. Experimentation can become a flattering excuse for disorder. Leaders can rename confusion as creativity, pile up pilots, and then fail to keep any memory of what worked. At that point the organization is not learning through variation. It is burning energy while calling the burn adaptive. The chapter refuses that drift by insisting that trying and keeping belong together.

So the real lesson is sharper than innovation theater. Durable companies do not need perfect foresight before they move, but they do need a core stable enough to judge the results of movement. They learn by running trials, retaining useful discoveries, and letting successful methods accumulate over time. Once that becomes clear, a new question appears. Who inside the institution can carry that memory and judgment forward as leaders?
