Most organizations eventually fake themselves into a bad choice. They act as if they must either stay true to who they are or change enough to stay alive. One side of the choice sounds noble and becomes rigidity. The other side sounds adaptive and becomes drift. Chapter 4 refuses the split. A durable company does not choose between identity and movement. It preserves a real core and stimulates real progress at the same time.

The title Preserve the Core/Stimulate Progress matters because it separates values from tactics. Chapter 3 established that strong companies need a center deeper than profit alone. Chapter 4 explains how that center survives contact with change. The key is that the core is not everything the company currently does. The core is the values and purpose worth protecting. Methods, routines, and strategies can and should move around that center when the environment demands it.

That distinction is what saves the chapter from becoming balance talk. If leaders treat every old habit as sacred, the company starts protecting custom instead of principle. If they treat every change as proof of progress, the company loses continuity and slowly forgets what it is preserving. The chapter's force comes from refusing both mistakes. Preservation belongs to the core. Progress belongs to everything that helps the institution keep expressing that core under new conditions.

That is why the phrase stimulate progress matters as much as preserve the core. A company that protects its values but stops experimenting with methods is not durable. It is stiff. The world changes, competitors move, and internal habits decay. If progress is absent, the core turns into a museum piece. The institution may still speak proudly about who it is, but it will have fewer and fewer ways to make that identity effective in the world it actually inhabits.

The hard part is diagnosis. Leaders often protect the wrong thing. They call old tactics values. They confuse familiar routines with institutional character. That is the danger built into the chapter. If a team cannot tell the difference between what is truly core and what is merely inherited custom, it will defend stagnation in the language of principle. A company can become deeply self-righteous while quietly becoming less capable.

The chapter therefore asks for a more disciplined kind of loyalty. Be stubborn about purpose and values. Be restless about methods, structures, and practices. The institution should feel continuous at the center and adaptive at the edge. That combination is what lets it survive without becoming hollow or frozen.

So Chapter 4 resolves the tension created by the earlier chapters. A company worth studying is more than a hero and more than a profit engine. It also needs a real core. But a real core is only durable when leaders know how to protect it without wrapping every old method in sacred language. Once that split is clear, a new question appears. What kind of ambition can channel progress without breaking the core it is supposed to serve?
