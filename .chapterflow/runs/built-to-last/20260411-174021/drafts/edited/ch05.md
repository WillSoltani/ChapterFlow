Many organizations announce large goals that do almost nothing. The slogan sounds bold, the slide looks clean, and everyone goes back to work unchanged. Chapter 5 is interested in a harder kind of target, one that actually redirects effort. Collins and Porras give that kind of target a durable label: the Big Hairy Audacious Goal, or BHAG.

The label matters because the chapter is not worshipping size. A BHAG is not merely a goal with bigger numbers attached. It is a stretch objective strong enough to organize movement across the institution. People can see where they are heading, what tradeoffs now matter, and which efforts deserve real commitment. The chapter stays disciplined only if that goal remains institution-serving rather than leader-serving.

That logic follows naturally from the previous chapter. Once a company learns to preserve its core while stimulating progress, it still needs a destination for that progress. Otherwise the institution confuses activity with advancement. A bold shared target gives progress shape. It concentrates scattered initiatives into one visible direction.

That concentration is the chapter's real mechanism. A serious stretch goal aligns decisions that would otherwise remain disconnected. It gives teams a common answer to a simple question: does this move us toward the target? In that sense, the goal is more than motivational language. It becomes a way of coordinating resources, attention, and persistence over time.

The chapter also keeps one hazard in view. Boldness can slide into spectacle if leaders start treating sheer scale as a virtue. Then the target flatters the people announcing it but does not deepen the institution. A large goal becomes vanity theater rather than a useful organizing force. The chapter works only when the audacity stays tethered to core values and purpose.

That tether is what separates a BHAG from reckless grandstanding. The company is not reaching for something big merely to feel impressive. It is choosing a demanding destination because that destination channels progress in service of what the institution is trying to preserve. Ambition, in other words, has to answer to identity rather than replace it.

So Chapter 5 is not generic advice about dreaming bigger. It is an argument for directed audacity under discipline. A worthy bold goal concentrates movement, sharpens commitment, and gives stimulated progress a destination without letting spectacle take over. The next chapter asks what kind of culture can absorb that pressure and keep it productive.
