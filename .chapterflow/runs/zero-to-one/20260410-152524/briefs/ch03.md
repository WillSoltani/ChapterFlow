Core Claim:
This chapter argues that strong monopoly-creating companies become meaningfully different from one another, while failed or fragile companies tend to be trapped in competition, sameness, and thin profits.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
This is the monopoly-definition chapter. Its job is to stop treating competition as the healthy default and to show why real business strength comes from unique position, pricing power, and room to plan.

Author Logic Chain:
First the chapter opens with the happy-families versus unhappy-families contrast.
Then it links competitive markets to profit erosion and strategic sameness.
Then it links monopoly to uniqueness, slack for long-term planning, and stronger economics.
Then it preserves the limit that monopoly here means created distinctiveness, not lazy defense of any powerful incumbent.

Required Anchors:
- the title logic that happy companies differ while failed companies resemble one another
- competition pushes firms toward sameness and weak profits
- monopoly means being so differentiated that direct competition relaxes
- the chapter reframes monopoly from taboo word to business-strength concept
