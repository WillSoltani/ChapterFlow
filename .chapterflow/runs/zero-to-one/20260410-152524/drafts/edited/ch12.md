Many people talk about technology as if its highest purpose were simply to remove people from the picture.

Chapter 12 pushes back on that framing. It argues that the strongest systems often come from complementarity rather than replacement. Computers and people do different kinds of work well, so the real design question is not whether one side disappears. It is how the strengths of each side combine into something stronger.

That is why the chapter separates machine strengths from human strengths. Computers can scan, sort, and process at scales or speeds that people cannot match. People still matter where judgment, interpretation, and context decide what the output means. The point is not to flatter either side. The point is to reject the lazy idea that all work is one kind of work.

Once that distinction is clear, complementarity becomes practical. A system gets stronger when a machine handles pattern-heavy work while a person handles the harder judgment calls the scan cannot settle by itself. Technology matters here not because it replaces the human everywhere, but because it changes the human's job into a sharper one.

The limit matters. This chapter is not defending nostalgia, panic, or airy futurism. It is not saying that people always win, and it is not saying that automation solves everything. It is saying that technology design should improve the work by dividing strengths well instead of fantasizing about replacement in the abstract.

That leads into the next chapter. Once the book shows how technology and human work can complement each other, the next question becomes why some technology bets still fail when timing, scale, and business design are wrong.
