Average-case thinking feels fair because it treats outcomes as if they were spread across the field in a roughly even way.

That is the chapter's first target. Chapter 7 says that instinct becomes misleading once returns are actually concentrated. In a power-law world, a few outcomes matter far more than the rest. Money does not simply reward steady averages. It often follows outliers with disproportionate force, which means equal-looking cases can carry very unequal economic weight.

The chapter then sharpens the mechanism. One unusually strong result can outweigh many modest ones. A portfolio, a business path, or a set of strategic bets can look diversified on the surface and still be governed economically by a very small number of outcomes. The smaller results are real, but they do not contribute equally. One dominant winner can reshape the whole picture.

That is why ordinary averaging can hide what matters most. If someone evaluates each option as if it contributes in similar proportion, the analysis can miss the actual shape of the distribution. The chapter therefore asks readers to follow the money toward where returns concentrate instead of assuming value is spread smoothly across many middling cases.

The limit is essential. This is not a chapter about reckless gambling, random moonshots, or careless selection. Power-law logic does not say every extreme bet is wise. It says the economics are uneven enough that disciplined judgment has to notice concentration rather than defaulting to average-case comfort. The lesson is asymmetry, not impulsiveness.

That sets up the next move in the book. If unusual upside often comes from a small number of dominant outcomes, the next question is where those outliers come from. Chapter 8 answers by turning toward secrets, hidden truths that consensus thinking tends to miss.
