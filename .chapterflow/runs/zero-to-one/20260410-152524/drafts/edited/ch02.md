Nothing makes caution sound smarter than the memory of a public humiliation.

That is the chapter's starting point. The dot-com crash was a real collapse, not a myth founders tell themselves later for drama. Bad companies failed for real reasons. Hype outran substance. But the chapter argues that the aftermath produced its own distortion. Instead of learning how to separate disciplined ambition from fantasy, startup culture learned to distrust ambition itself.

The safer rulebook that followed sounded unimpeachable: make incremental advances, stay lean and flexible, improve on the competition, focus on product not sales. Each lesson contains some truth. That is exactly why the chapter takes them seriously before pushing back. The problem is what happens when a prudent correction gets promoted into a universal operating system.

The first two rules reward timidity when they harden into doctrine. Small steps are easier to defend after a crash because they reduce the chance of visible embarrassment. Flexibility sounds wise because commitment can now be mocked as arrogance. But a startup does not always need less conviction. Sometimes it needs a clearer wager. The chapter's complaint is not against prudence. It is against turning fear into permanent strategy.

The second pair weakens judgment differently. Improving on the competition can keep a company trapped inside someone else's frame instead of asking what new category or capability should exist. Focusing on product, not sales can make commercial weakness sound noble, as if distribution were vulgar rather than necessary. In the chapter's logic, both lessons let founders feel responsible while they quietly shrink the field of what they are willing to attempt.

That is why the chapter calls these lessons overcorrections instead of lies. They are fragments, not foundations. Waste is bad. Recklessness is bad. Good products matter. But startup strategy fails when fragments get mistaken for permanent law. The memory of a crash can become so powerful that it governs what kinds of companies people can still imagine building. Once that happens, the culture starts rewarding safety at the cost of strength.

The chapter still refuses one cheap reading. Criticizing timid orthodoxy is not a defense of bubble-era delusion. The answer is not careless grandiosity. It is better judgment about when boldness is grounded, when caution is cowardice, and when fear is disguising itself as wisdom. That question opens the next chapter: if safety is not the mark of a strong company, what is?
