Competition keeps a strange kind of prestige even when it makes people thinner, more reactive, and less original.

That is the chapter's starting point. In this frame, competition is not just a market fact. It is an ideology, a mindset that makes contest feel honorable and makes constant comparison look like proof of seriousness. Once that mindset takes hold, people start assuming the fiercest arena must also be the most meaningful one. The chapter wants to break that reflex.

The first cost is attention. A team that keeps checking rivals before checking its own plan begins letting the contest define what matters. Roadmaps bend toward competitor launches. Strategy discussions revolve around matching features, countering moves, and staying legible inside someone else's frame. The chapter does not say rivals are unreal. It says fixation on rivals becomes a form of strategic captivity.

The second cost is imitation. Once comparison becomes habitual, reactive copying starts feeling practical. A founder can defend a borrowed move by saying the market already validated it. A manager can call a response prudent because a rival forced the issue. But the chapter's point is that this realism is often just secondhand thinking. It shrinks judgment by outsourcing direction to the contest itself.

Competition also holds its prestige because intensity is easy to admire from the outside. People confuse being locked in a hard fight with doing important work. They treat constant response as evidence of excellence, even when the response is making the company narrower and more derivative. The chapter is not dismissing effort. It is attacking the habit of worshipping the contest itself.

The limit matters. Strategic awareness is still necessary. A company does not get stronger by pretending rivals do not exist. The distinction is between awareness and obsession, between noticing the field and letting the field write the roadmap. Once that difference is clear, the next chapter follows naturally: if rivalry traps companies in the short game, what kind of advantage actually lasts?
