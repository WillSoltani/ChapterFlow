Uncertainty makes luck-talk feel sophisticated because it sounds honest about how little people can predict.

That is the chapter's first target. Once people start describing outcomes as if they were mostly lottery draws, planning begins to look naive. The posture feels realistic because uncertainty is real. But the chapter argues that this realism can quickly become an excuse for drift. If the future is treated as fundamentally random, then weak preparation can hide behind a vocabulary of chance.

The chapter does not deny luck. It denies the surrender that often gets smuggled in under luck-only language. Builders still make choices about where to spend time, what to learn, which risks to absorb, and what kind of position to construct. Those choices do not guarantee results, but they do shape what kinds of results become more likely. Agency survives even when certainty does not.

That is why planning matters in this chapter. Planning is not prophecy, and it is not a claim to total control. It is a way of changing the probability landscape. A plan can improve the odds of reaching some futures and reduce exposure to others. The chapter wants readers to see planning as directional influence rather than as a fantasy of prediction.

The limit is crucial. Once agency is defended, the prose cannot slide into a self-help promise that disciplined people can simply force the world to comply. Contingency remains real. The chapter keeps its balance by saying that uncertainty does not erase the value of choosing well; it only removes guarantees. Planning still matters because surrender matters too, and drift is usually not neutral.

That balance creates the bridge to the next chapter. If outcomes are neither fully controlled nor purely random, then the next question is how money and power-law dynamics concentrate consequences. Chapter 6 restores planning just enough to make those later asymmetries worth taking seriously.
