Being first can look glamorous because beginnings are easy to notice and easy to praise.

That is the chapter's opening challenge. Early arrival may matter, but the book refuses to treat firstness as the real measure of business strength. A company can be first and still fail to keep its lead. Another company can arrive later and still become the one that owns the market's future economics. That is why the chapter shifts attention away from novelty at the start and toward endurance over time.

The chapter's main reframe is value. A business is not worth much because it had an exciting launch or because it moved before everyone else. It is worth more if it can keep generating cash flow far into the future. The argument is not only financial in a narrow sense. It is strategic. A company becomes stronger when its edge lasts long enough to keep turning present effort into future value instead of into a short burst of attention.

That is why a short-lived lead is weaker than it first appears. Early excitement can hide fragility. A company may look impressive when it is new, but if its advantage fades quickly, the long-term economics will be thin. The chapter pushes the reader to ask a harder question than who arrived first. Which company can still dominate after the market matures, after the early noise fades, and after imitation begins?

Last-mover advantage names that harder standard. The point is not that companies should arrive late for its own sake. The point is that durable position matters more than theatrical speed. A serious company wants to be the one that remains strong at the end of the market's development, not merely the one that collected applause at the beginning.

The chapter still needs a limit. Durability is not passive waiting, and long-term value does not come from doing nothing while others move first. Endurance has to be built. A company needs a position strong enough to keep producing value across time, not just a story about patience. Otherwise, long-horizon talk becomes another form of vagueness.

That is the bridge to the next chapter. Once the book says durable advantage matters more than firstness, the next problem appears naturally. If outcomes unfold over time and uncertainty remains real, how much can people still shape the future through planning rather than drift or luck?
