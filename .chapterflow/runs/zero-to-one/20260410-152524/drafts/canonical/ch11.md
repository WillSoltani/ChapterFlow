Many builders talk as if a great product should naturally pull customers toward itself once the product is good enough.

Chapter 11 rejects that fantasy. It argues that quality alone does not guarantee reach. A company still has to get its product to customers, which means distribution cannot be treated as an embarrassing afterthought. The chapter places sales, channels, and market access inside the core logic of business rather than outside it.

That is why the chapter refuses to let product strength stand in for go-to-market strength. A product can be technically impressive and still remain trapped if the company has no deliberate path to the people who might use it. Distribution is therefore not a cosmetic layer placed on top of value. It is part of how value actually becomes real in the market.

This is where the chapter turns distribution into a competence. Sales and channel design matter because they determine whether a company can connect what it has built to actual customers. The chapter is not saying that hype replaces quality. It is saying that businesses fail when they pretend quality alone eliminates the need for reach.

The limit matters. Once the chapter starts defending distribution, it could easily collapse into generic marketing chatter, manipulative sales rhetoric, or cynical product indifference. It resists all three. The point is not to glorify hype. The point is to admit that even strong products still need a path into the world. Distribution matters because customers do not appear automatically.

That leads into the next chapter. If companies need deliberate systems to reach customers, the next question becomes how technology should work with people inside those systems rather than simply replacing them. Chapter 12 answers by moving from distribution to human-machine complementarity.
