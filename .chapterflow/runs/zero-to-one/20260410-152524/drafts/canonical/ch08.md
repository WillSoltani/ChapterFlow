People often talk as if every important truth has already been found, named, and distributed to the whole market.

That is the chapter's first target. Chapter 8 says that assumption is too final. Secrets still exist, not as conspiracies or mystical codes, but as truths that are real and still underbelieved, neglected, or poorly understood. If everyone already saw everything that mattered, unusual opportunity would be much harder to explain.

The chapter then defines what kind of secret matters. A secret is not mere disagreement, eccentricity, or style. It is a hidden truth with enough reality behind it to support action. That is why the chapter is not rewarding performative contrarianism. A belief does not become valuable merely because it is unpopular. It has to be true in a way the consensus has not fully absorbed yet.

This is where discovery connects to value. If builders only copy what everyone already believes, they mostly inherit crowded opportunity. But when they see something real that others are missing, they can act before the truth becomes obvious. The chapter therefore links secrets to unusual upside and to the possibility of building a company around something the market has not fully recognized.

The limit matters. Once the chapter starts defending secrets, it could easily collapse into conspiracy flavor or into generic advice to “think differently.” It resists both. The point is not to disagree for show. The point is to discover something real enough that disagreement becomes secondary to accuracy.

That leads directly into the next chapter. If a hidden truth can create unusual opportunity, the next question is how to organize a company around that truth so it can survive contact with reality. Chapter 9 answers by moving from discovery to foundations.
