One reason competition survives criticism so easily is that people mistake it for seriousness.

That is the chapter's opening pressure. Competition is not only a market condition in this frame. It is an ideology, a way of seeing the world that makes contest feel noble and constant comparison feel disciplined. Once that mindset takes hold, people start assuming that the hardest-fought arena must also be the most important one. The chapter wants to unsettle that assumption.

Rivalry narrows attention first. A company that keeps checking competitors before checking its own plan starts letting the contest define what matters. Product roadmaps bend toward whatever a rival just launched. Strategy meetings revolve around matching, answering, or preempting someone else's move. The chapter is not claiming competitors are imaginary. It is claiming that too much focus on them becomes a kind of captivity.

That captivity then shapes behavior. Once comparison becomes the main habit, imitation becomes easier than independent judgment. A team can justify a reactive move because the rival made it first. A founder can call a copied feature prudent because the market already validated it elsewhere. But the chapter argues that this kind of realism is often just borrowed thinking. It keeps builders inside someone else's frame.

Competition also keeps its prestige because intensity looks admirable from the outside. People confuse being locked in a contest with doing valuable work. They treat constant reaction as proof of commitment, even when the reaction is making strategy thinner. The chapter's point is not that all effort inside competition is fake. It is that the contest itself too easily becomes the thing being worshipped.

That is why the chapter needs a limit. Strategic awareness is still real. Companies do not become wiser by pretending rivals do not exist. The sharper distinction is between awareness and obsession. You can know the field without letting the field write your roadmap. You can notice a rival without organizing your identity around beating them move for move. The chapter is attacking captivity, not perception.

Once that lands, the bridge to the next chapter becomes clear. If competition keeps people reactive, then the deeper question is what kind of position lets a company stop playing that short game. The next chapter answers by turning from rivalry itself to durability: what kind of advantage lasts long enough to matter?
