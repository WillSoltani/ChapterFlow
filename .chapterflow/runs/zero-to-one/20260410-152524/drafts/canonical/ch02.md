The strangest thing about a real disaster is that it can still teach the wrong permanent lesson.

That is the chapter's opening pressure. The dot-com crash was not imaginary. Plenty of companies were inflated, unserious, or built on fantasies they could not support. When that world collapsed, fear took over for a reason. The problem, in this chapter's telling, is what happened next. Instead of treating the crash as a warning against a specific kind of excess, startup culture turned it into a broad argument against boldness itself.

Out of that backlash came a safer rulebook. Make incremental advances. Stay lean and flexible. Improve on the competition. Focus on product, not sales. Each rule sounds disciplined, even mature. That is part of why they spread so easily. They gave builders a way to sound responsible after a humiliating era. But the chapter argues that responsibility hardened into orthodoxy. What began as a correction became a cage.

You can see the problem in the first two rules. Incremental advances are easier to manage, easier to explain, and easier to defend when people are afraid of being wrong in public. Staying lean and flexible sounds intelligent for similar reasons. It treats commitment as danger and optionality as wisdom. The chapter does not say these instincts are always foolish. It says they stop being useful when they become universal law. Some companies really do need a sharper plan and a more decisive wager than a culture of nervous adaptation wants to allow.

The next two rules distort strategy in a different way. Improve on the competition sounds practical, but it can lock a company into someone else's frame instead of asking what new space should exist. Focus on product, not sales sounds pure, but it can turn distribution weakness into a moral pose. In the chapter's logic, these lessons let founders feel serious while quietly shrinking what they are trying to build. They replace stronger differentiation with safer comparison and turn commercial reality into something embarrassing to mention.

That is why the chapter treats the post-crash rulebook as overcorrection rather than as nonsense. Each lesson contains a fragment of truth. Reckless leaps can destroy companies. Waste matters. Competitive awareness matters. Products do matter. But fragments become misleading when they are applied everywhere, all the time, without regard for what kind of company is actually being built. The deeper mistake is letting fear from one era define the imagination of the next.

The chapter still keeps an honest line in place. Rejecting timid orthodoxy is not the same as celebrating delusion. The answer to a bad bubble is not another bad bubble. The answer is a better standard for deciding when boldness is grounded and when it is fantasy. That is the bridge into the next chapter. If universal caution is too weak to guide founders well, what does a genuinely strong company actually look like?
