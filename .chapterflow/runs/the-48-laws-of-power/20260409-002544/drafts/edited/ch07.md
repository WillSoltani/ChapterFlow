People often remember the face on the result more clearly than the hands that built it. Greene places the seventh law inside that unfair gap. Power does not always gather around the person who did the most work. It often gathers around the person who became the visible owner of the outcome. This chapter is sharp because it is not mainly about laziness or efficiency. It is about memory, ownership, and the politics of association.

Labor and credit move differently through social life. Labor can stay hidden inside drafts, prototypes, revisions, and background fixes. Credit sticks more easily to the public claimant, the speaker at the meeting, the byline on the page, or the manager attached to the finished output. Greene treats that difference as leverage. A person who can organize, absorb, or frame other people's effort may appear larger than someone who works alone, because public memory is simpler than actual contribution.

That is the chapter's strongest point: remembered authorship often matters more politically than solitary effort. Delegation expands reach. Borrowed skill can raise the scale of what you seem able to produce. Visible ownership can then convert that scale into status. In practice, people do not always remember who fixed the draft, solved the technical failure, or carried the last revision. They remember who presented the finished object and whose name sat closest to the result.

But the law becomes weak if it is read as simple theft without consequence. Hidden contributors are not passive material. They can resent invisibility, withhold future effort, defect, expose the arrangement, or become enemies who know exactly where your image is fragile. Greene's chapter is politically realistic because it keeps that danger near the surface. Captured credit can build power quickly, but it also produces relational debt and future risk.

That tension reveals the difference between real command and hollow appropriation. Some visible owners truly shape the result through direction, synthesis, timing, or framing. Others merely stand nearest to the spotlight and absorb what more capable people produced. The first kind may still provoke resentment, but it has some real authority over the process. The second kind builds on weaker ground. If the contributors disappear, the public owner may have very little left that can sustain the captured image.

Chapter 6 explained how to become visible. Chapter 7 asks what happens once a visible result exists and the audience starts assigning authorship. Public memory is often compressed and unfair. The same simplification that makes leadership legible can erase the people who made the work possible. Greene wants the reader to see that this is a usable mechanism, but the limit has to stay visible: authority built on absorbed credit becomes brittle if it outruns real command.

Modern settings make the pattern easy to see. A manager presents a team win as singular vision. A student editor receives praise for an issue carried by invisible revisions. A socially skilled friend becomes the public face of a shared project and later gets remembered as the one who made it happen. The cases differ, but the mechanism stays constant: the public prefers one visible owner to a complicated chain of contribution. The next law follows from that discomfort. If people and credit move this way, how do you get others to move toward the field you chose rather than chasing them on theirs?
