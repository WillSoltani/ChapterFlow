Good work can vanish if nobody notices it. In crowded rooms, crowded institutions, and crowded markets, obscurity is not neutral. It is a strategic weakness. Greene's sixth law starts there: attention is not only vanity fuel. It is often the gate through which influence has to pass before anything else can happen. If no one looks up, no one interprets you, remembers you, or adjusts around you.

That is why this chapter treats visibility as leverage. People sort quickly. They look for what breaks pattern, what glints, what seems larger or stranger than the background. A startling quality or memorable surface pulls the eye before it proves anything deeper. Greene is not saying attention automatically deserves respect. He is saying attention decides who even gets the chance to become consequential. Invisible competence may still be real, but obscurity traps it out of circulation.

The chapter sharpens when it adds mystery. Distinctiveness wins a first look. Mystery keeps the look alive because people keep interpreting what they cannot fully place. They speculate, compare notes, and watch for the next clue. Greene uses Mata Hari narrowly for this mechanism. Her layered stories of origin sustained fascination because they kept her unfinished in the public mind. The force of the example is not glamour by itself. It is the way uncertainty keeps the audience returning.

Attention, however, decays. What startled once becomes routine if it never changes shape. Greene therefore builds maintenance into the law. Once visible, vary the act enough that familiarity does not cool into boredom. In practice, that means not repeating one successful signal until it becomes parody. The point is to keep the audience interpreting rather than yawning.

This is why the law is more serious than cheap controversy. Attention is not useful simply because it is loud. The wrong kind of notice can fix the wrong reputation. Someone can become visible as shallow, reckless, or unserious, and that spotlight can be as limiting as invisibility. A workplace can notice theatrics without trusting substance. A school can remember disruption without granting authority. Greene's sharper claim is that attention has to serve a larger image rather than detach from it.

There is also a hierarchy limit. Visibility can backfire if it embarrasses a superior or turns your distinctiveness into a threat above you. The same move that wins attention in one setting can trigger punishment in another. This is one reason mystery can be safer than blunt self-display. It gathers eyes without always forcing a direct status contest.

Chapter 5 showed how reputation lets an image travel ahead of you. Chapter 6 asks what happens when that image cannot rise above the background in the first place. Reputation needs visibility, and visibility needs contrast. The serious lesson is not to become a clown for the crowd. It is to become hard to ignore without becoming hard to respect. Once attention is secured, the next contest is obvious: when many hands help create the visible result, who gets remembered as the author of what everyone can see?
