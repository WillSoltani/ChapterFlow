Greene's eleventh law begins with a hard distinction: appreciation is weaker than necessity. People may like you, praise you, or feel grateful for your help and still replace you as soon as a smoother option appears. The sturdier source of leverage is different. It comes from holding a role, access point, or coordinating function that others cannot easily reproduce under pressure. The chapter is not mainly about being admired. It is about becoming costly to remove.

That is why substitution risk sits at the center of the law. If the same result can be produced by many hands with little friction, then your position stays vulnerable no matter how warm your reputation feels. Greene's logic is colder and more structural than that. Power grows when your usefulness is tied to something others depend on and cannot swap out without losing time, clarity, or control. Replaceability weakens leverage even when appreciation sounds sincere.

The strongest form of this move does not require drama. Dependence can grow because you understand the handoff map nobody else has learned, because you hold a trusted relationship others cannot yet access, or because you keep a complex system working across several moving parts. In each case, the leverage comes from irreplaceable function rather than from emotional debt. The chapter's mechanism is structural necessity, not sentimental attachment.

That distinction matters because the law is easy to vulgarize. Read crudely, it can sound like advice to hoard information, create confusion, or quietly sabotage everyone else's competence. But artificial dependence teaches people to resent you, work around you, or build backups in secret. Greene's law is stronger when it stays on usefulness that is genuinely difficult to substitute. The manipulative version may create dependence for a while, but it also teaches everyone nearby that freedom requires your removal.

The chapter becomes concrete fast. One coordinator is hard to remove because she alone understands how client promises, schedules, and technical constraints fit together in one usable sequence. One student in a robotics lab becomes central because he can translate between design, wiring, and testing whenever the team stalls. A student newspaper editor becomes hard to sideline because she knows the publishing calendar, the contributor chain, and the last-minute fixes that keep the issue from slipping. The question is always the same: what function becomes costly to lose?

The limit is as important as the mechanism. Visible gatekeeping, theatrical indispensability, and obvious monopolizing make leverage brittle. Once people feel trapped, they start documenting your moves, training substitutes, or redesigning the system to break your hold. Dependence can lock in value, but only while it still looks connected to real contribution rather than to coercive scarcity. The backlash threshold appears when your necessity begins looking manufactured instead of earned.

Chapter 10 asked how to protect the field from recurring contagion. Chapter 11 asks what becomes powerful inside a cleaner field: the person whose steadiness, knowledge, or coordination others cannot easily replace. That shift points forward. If necessity creates leverage, the next question is how selective honesty and generosity can lower resistance without dissolving the leverage you built.
