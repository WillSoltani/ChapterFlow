Coriolanus was powerful for as long as people could imagine more depth in him than they could actually hear. Distance helped him. Reserve helped him. His reputation lived partly on the gap between what people saw and what they knew. Once he had to speak at length in politics, that gap closed. Greene uses the collapse to make a sharp point: too many words reduce scale. They reveal irritation, insecurity, excess explanation, and ordinary impulse. Speech can pull someone down from statue height into ordinary human size with surprising speed.

That is why this law is not simply about silence. It is about leakage. The more you say, the more likely you are to reveal motives, emotions, weak spots, or an appetite for approval. Extra words also give other people material to sort, challenge, and reinterpret. They can catch contradictions, detect nerves, or stop projecting significance into you because you filled the empty space yourself. Brevity changes that dynamic. When you stop short, the room often completes the sentence for you, and people usually complete it with more force, intention, or intelligence than your full explanation would have carried.

Louis XIV gives Greene the cleaner political version of the law. Greene presents him as someone who trained himself to speak little, which left courtiers unsure where they stood. That uncertainty mattered. When people cannot easily read your opinion, your anger, or your limit, they become more careful around you. Sparse speech therefore does two things at once: it reduces what you accidentally give away, and it increases the amount other people project into what remains unsaid. The result can look like authority even before formal power enters the room.

Andy Warhol extends the same mechanism into a different style. He did not need royal gravity. Greene uses him to show that even vague, brief replies can create an aura of depth because people dislike empty space and rush to fill it with meaning. That is the chapter's core insight. Silence is not always powerful because it contains profound thought. It is often powerful because other people assume it does. The fewer signals you offer, the more room you give them to imagine purpose, complexity, and control.

The modern uses are obvious once you see the mechanism. A manager who answers a question in one clean sentence often sounds more secure than the manager who keeps talking until the point weakens. A student senator who stops after the sharp line lands usually looks stronger than the senator who keeps explaining the same point three more ways. A person in conflict who sends one controlled message may preserve more dignity than the person who fires off seven texts trying to manage every possible misunderstanding. In each case, too much speech turns strength into exposure.

The limit matters. Silence stops helping when it starts reading as evasion, contempt, or fear. Some people hear this law and become stiff. They answer ordinary questions with theatrical brevity and treat warmth as weakness. That can create suspicion faster than authority. If people start feeling punished, frozen out, or manipulated, the restraint has gone bad. Greene's reversal matters here. In some situations, the safer cover is a relaxed, chatty, court-jester surface that lowers guard better than hard reserve.

So the real discipline is not to speak as little as possible in every setting. It is to speak less than your nerves want, less than your vanity wants, and less than the moment tempts you to spill. Keep enough warmth for trust, enough clarity for coordination, and enough restraint that your words still carry pressure when they arrive. That makes the bridge to the next law natural. If words help decide how much authority, mystery, or weakness people attach to you, then reputation is simply the larger echo those impressions leave behind.
