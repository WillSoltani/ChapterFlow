Greene's thirteenth law begins with an uncomfortable observation: people do not help most reliably because they pity you or remember what they owe you. Mercy can be unstable, gratitude can fade, and loyalty can weaken under pressure. The steadier engine is different. People move more predictably when they can see how helping you serves their own advantage, protects them from loss, or improves their position in some concrete way.

That is why the chapter turns on incentive framing. Greene is not saying that gratitude never matters. He is saying that if you want dependable cooperation, you should not rest the request on sentiment alone. A request becomes stronger when the other person can see a gain, a protection, or a practical benefit that belongs to them too. The ask starts feeling easier to accept because it no longer sounds like pure sacrifice.

The mechanism can be simple without being crude. A manager may support your proposal once it becomes clear that the plan protects budget, timing, or credit for her team. A classmate may help a student senate initiative once he sees that the outcome fixes a problem that keeps costing him time. A friend may respond better when the favor request is tied to a shared benefit rather than to guilt about the past. In each case, self-interest organizes action more reliably than a plea for mercy does.

That distinction matters because the law is easy to cheapen. Incentive framing is not identical to bribery, manipulation, or shameless flattery. The stronger move is to surface a real alignment that already exists or could credibly exist. If the request is too nakedly one-sided, the self-interest appeal starts sounding insulting. If the benefit looks fake, exaggerated, or transparently manipulative, the listener stops hearing alignment and starts hearing extraction.

The chapter therefore has a real limit. People are not machines that respond only to greed. Trust, memory, affection, and obligation still matter. Greene's narrower claim is that action becomes more dependable when your request gives the other side a reason it can accept as its own. The request works best when it makes advantage visible without pretending the other person's motives are nobler than they are.

The failure mode is practical. A tutoring center plea that leans only on warm history may fall flat if volunteers cannot see how the extra work helps them or the system they care about. A workplace request that says only “you owe me” can breed resentment. A personal appeal that uses gratitude as pressure can make the relationship feel transactional in the ugliest way. The law works when it frames help as sensible for the other person, not merely virtuous.

Chapter 12 asked how to lower suspicion with selective honesty and generosity. Chapter 13 asks what should fill the room once suspicion is lower: a request that speaks to interest rather than pity. That points forward. If cooperation has been secured, the next question is how apparent friendship can also become a channel for watching, learning, and gathering information.
