The Marquis de Sevigne was effective for exactly as long as the countess could not be sure what game he was playing. Under Ninon de Lenclos's advice, he approached indirectly, letting friendship, attention, and jealousy keep the situation uncertain. Then he declared love. By naming the goal, he killed the ambiguity that had been working for him. Greene uses that reversal to make a sharp point: the moment people can clearly read what you want from them, they gain room to defend themselves against it.

That is why this law is not about mystery as decoration. It is about initiative. If your intention becomes visible too soon, other people can stall, flatter, refuse, or sabotage before you are ready to move. They can gather allies, prepare objections, or simply calm the emotion you were trying to create. The Marquis fails because uncertainty had been doing real strategic work. As long as the countess had to guess, she remained engaged. Once the pursuit was named, the field became legible and the energy collapsed. Readable motive invites counterplay.

Otto von Bismarck supplies the harder counterexample. Greene presents him as wanting the war that would expand his power while speaking instead about peace and the madness of war. That surface won trust. By sounding aligned with what the king wanted to hear, Bismarck rose into the Prussian premiership before moving toward the conflict he had desired. The lesson is not moral admiration. It is timing. Concealment matters because it prevents the room from reacting before your position is secure enough to survive reaction.

From there the chapter widens into the smokescreen idea. A bland exterior can be powerful because people relax when they think they understand the surface in front of them. They stop looking for the harder line underneath. Greene also allows the opposite route: distraction through humor, color, or harmless chatter. People often mistake appearance for reality and struggle to track more than one thing at once. The shared mechanism is not silence. It is false legibility. You let the room believe it already knows what matters, and that belief buys you time.

The modern versions are everywhere. A manager who announces a negotiation floor before leverage exists invites the other side to harden. A student leader who reveals the full coalition plan before votes are lined up gives opponents a schedule for resistance. A family member who describes a move, a purchase, or a relationship decision too early can trigger interference before the plan is sturdy enough to survive argument. In each case, the loss is not only privacy. It is momentum. Once the intention becomes public, other people begin organizing around it.

The limit matters. Concealment turns foolish when it stops serving timing and starts serving vanity. Some people get addicted to unreadability itself. They hide ordinary plans, cultivate artificial mystery, and make every interaction into a test of who can be least knowable. That does not automatically produce power. It can produce mistrust, isolation, and self-inflicted confusion. If the people who actually need to coordinate with you can no longer tell what commitment you are making, concealment has stopped protecting initiative and started damaging execution.

Greene's reversal keeps the law from hardening into costume. Sometimes cold silence creates suspicion faster than respect. In those moments, the better disguise may be the chatty court-jester posture that seems harmless and readable while the important line stays concealed. The surface changes, but the purpose does not: reveal only as much of the real plan as the situation requires. That bridge leads cleanly into the next law. If speech can either expose or protect intention, then saying less than necessary becomes not just a temperament but a direct instrument of power.
