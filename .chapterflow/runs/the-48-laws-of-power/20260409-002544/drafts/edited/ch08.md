Running after someone else's timing often feels active, but it usually means you have already accepted their field. Greene's eighth law turns that hidden concession into the main problem. Power grows when other people have to move toward you, because movement is not only distance. It is tempo, initiative, and the loss of a little control. The person who chases often accepts the urgency and structure set by the other side before the real contest even begins.

That is why this chapter treats movement itself as leverage. If you can hold position while inducing the other side to cross the distance, you make them spend energy, reveal interest, and adjust first. Greene is not praising stillness for its own sake. Passive waiting can be weakness in disguise. The stronger move is controlled invitation: build a situation in which the other person feels tempted or rewarded for approaching on terms that already favor you.

Bait is the mechanism that makes that invitation work. It does not have to mean crude trickery. It can be an apparent opportunity, a timed opening, a useful offer, or a visible gap that draws someone into motion. Once they move, they begin accepting your tempo. That is the chapter's real logic. The lure matters because it changes who is advancing and who is receiving the advance.

The law becomes practical quickly. A manager who structures access so requests come through a chosen process is controlling movement. A student fair booth arranged to attract approach is doing the same. A tense personal relationship in which one person stops chasing and instead creates calmer, more attractive ground is not mere passivity either. In each case, one side stops spending itself in pursuit and starts shaping where contact happens.

But the law weakens when bait becomes obvious, desperate, or too expensive. Crude inducement can expose anxiety instead of control. Overbuilt lures can make the operator look needy, manipulative, or trapped in their own design. This is the chapter's limit. The point is to control movement, not to become addicted to schemes. If the bait still leaves you reacting on the other person's terms, the tactic has failed.

That limit keeps the law from collapsing into a slogan about never moving first. Some situations require direct action. Some opponents will not bite. Greene's harder claim is narrower: when movement can be induced rather than donated, the person who sets the field usually keeps more initiative. The person who rushes in first often reveals more need than intended.

Chapter 7 asked who gets remembered once the result is visible. Chapter 8 moves earlier and asks who controls the terms of contact before the result exists at all. That is why bait matters here. It is less about deception than about architecture. If others have to approach what you have already arranged, then you are shaping the board they must step onto. Once they are on that board, the next question follows naturally: when conflict becomes direct, what wins more reliably, argument or visible action?
