Most people make up part of their mind about you before you enter the room. A committee sees your name on a folder, a team remembers your last handoff, a friend repeats a small story over coffee, and the interaction begins under a forecast instead of a blank sky. Greene's fifth law treats that forecast as power. Reputation matters because it lets your image start working before you do.

That forecast exists because character is mostly hidden. People cannot look directly into motive, discipline, or judgment, so they rely on appearances, visible actions, and circulating talk. They use shortcuts. Those shortcuts may be crude, but they still decide who receives trust, caution, forgiveness, or suspicion. A good reputation therefore changes the field in advance. It lowers resistance, buys time, and makes your strengths easier to believe. A damaged one does the opposite. Every later explanation starts sounding like repair.

The chapter becomes more useful when it insists on one outstanding quality. If people are going to simplify you, it is better for them to simplify in a way that helps. A person known for reliability enters differently from a person who is vaguely thought of as talented, interesting, busy, and inconsistent all at once. One clear trait gives anticipation somewhere to settle. It also becomes a filter through which later evidence gets read. That is why reputation is not only praise. It is interpretation management.

This is also why reputation acts like a shield. Someone with reputational credit can survive a small mistake because observers are inclined to read it as an exception. Someone without that credit does not get the same generosity. The same mistake becomes proof. The shield is not imaginary, but it is not permanent either. It holds only while behavior keeps feeding it. Once conduct repeatedly contradicts the image, the shield thins and then breaks. Greene's point is about timing: a strong reputation gives you extra room before doubt hardens into consensus.

Repair therefore works better through consistent evidence than through panic. When reputation slips, self-defense often sounds like self-defense. Repeated visible conduct is stronger. Credible association can help too. A manager who misses one handoff does not restore trust with a long Slack monologue. She restores it by delivering the next few handoffs cleanly and by tying her work to teammates or systems the group already trusts. A student newspaper editor who lets one factual error through does not fix the damage by insisting everyone should know it was rare. He fixes it by publishing the correction cleanly, tightening the verification process, and letting the next issues prove that care has returned.

The offensive edge is real, but it is narrower than careless readers admit. Weakening another person's reputation can shift power because the room starts anticipating less from them and watching them differently. But reputational attacks are unstable tools. If the move is obvious, reckless, or weakly supported, it stains the attacker too. Even when it lands, it can turn a controlled conflict into an ugly exchange that lowers trust all around. The chapter is strongest when it keeps that limit visible. Reputation is powerful because social anticipation is powerful. Once you treat every dispute as an image war, you stop protecting force and start corroding the field.

Chapter 4 showed that speech and silence shape the immediate impression you create. Chapter 5 asks what happens after enough of those moments accumulate into a traveling image. Guarding reputation, in the serious sense, means guarding the repeated signals that build that image and resisting the urge to repair it theatrically. You are not trying to seem flawless. You are trying to make sure the first thing that reaches people is a believable strength rather than an organizing doubt. Once that image starts moving ahead of you, the next law follows naturally: what happens when visibility itself becomes the prize?
