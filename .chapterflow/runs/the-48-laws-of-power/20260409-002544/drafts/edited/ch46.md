Greene's forty-sixth law argues that visible perfection can become socially dangerous. Excellence may draw admiration at first, but flawless display can also make other people resentful, defensive, or quietly hostile. The law is not saying that competence is a mistake. It is saying that obvious superiority can trigger envy, and envy often works indirectly. People may not attack what they openly admire, but they may look for ways to diminish it, question it, or bring it back down to their level.

That is why the chapter values visible imperfection and restraint. Greene's point is not to become incompetent or to lie about what you can do. It is to soften the social edge of superiority so others can remain cooperative. A small imperfection, a modest presentation, or a little less display can make success easier to tolerate. The achievement stays real, but the presentation stops pushing other people into humiliation or comparison.

The chapter therefore distinguishes strategic softening from false incompetence. Strategic softening protects cooperation without abandoning authority or results. False incompetence weakens trust, muddies the truth, and can become self-sabotage. Greene's useful claim is narrower than fake humility. Keep excellence from feeling like a taunt, but do not undercut the function you actually need to perform.

Ordinary settings make the mechanism visible. A successful colleague may keep support more easily by sharing credit or showing one rough edge instead of displaying complete polish at every moment. A student leader or exhibition team may hold cooperation better when talent is evident but not flaunted. In personal life, obvious superiority in taste, discipline, or judgment can create resentment if it is displayed without modulation. In each case, social smoothing protects the relationship around the achievement.

The law overreaches when it turns into underperformance, manipulative clumsiness, or fear of visible competence. Some settings require clear authority, clean execution, and open confidence. Greene is strongest when he treats visible imperfection as a strategic adjustment rather than as a moral duty. Chapter 45 showed how reform must be paced so people can absorb change. Chapter 46 shows how success must sometimes be softened so people can absorb superiority without turning envious. Chapter 47 follows by asking how dependence can be created through strategic need.
