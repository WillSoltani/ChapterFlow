Michael III thought gratitude had solved the loyalty problem for him. Basilius had risen from obscurity through the emperor's favor, and that history made the relationship feel safer than it was. Greene uses the betrayal that followed to expose a hard truth: friendship, rescue, and familiarity do not cancel ambition. Sometimes they make you less able to see it. The person you lifted can start treating your help as the natural order of things. Once gratitude hardens into entitlement, the friend becomes dangerous precisely because you stop reading him clearly.

That is the chapter's first reversal. Most people imagine enemies as the obvious danger because hostility is visible. Greene argues that visible hostility at least gives you something clean to work with. You expect friction. You watch incentives. You maintain boundaries. Friendship often does the opposite. It invites you to relax, to infer loyalty from shared history, and to excuse early warning signs because the relationship feels emotionally safe. Michael III trusted the feeling of closeness more than the structure of power around him, and that trust blinded him.

Basilius matters here not just because he betrayed the emperor, but because the betrayal emerged from a pattern Greene wants the reader to recognize. Friends can become ungrateful because they quickly stop experiencing your help as a gift. They become envious because proximity exposes your advantages. They become careless because familiarity weakens caution. In other words, the emotional warmth around a friendship can hide motives that would look obvious if the same person stood one step farther away from you.

Greene turns to Talleyrand and Fouche to show the opposite dynamic. These were not sentimental allies. They were former enemies who understood each other's ambition well enough to work together against a larger threat. Their prior hostility did not make them harmless. It made them legible. Neither man expected innocence from the other. That alone created a cleaner field. A relationship built on caution can sometimes function more professionally than a relationship built on affection, because the cautions stay visible instead of being smothered by flattering stories about trust.

This is the chapter's real mechanism: readable incentives beat comforting labels. Friend and enemy are emotional categories. They tell you how the relationship feels, not how it will behave under pressure. Greene's harder point is that pressure reveals structure faster than sentiment. A former rival may work hard to prove usefulness, stay alert to the bargain, and respect boundaries because the relationship is explicit. A friend may rely on access, assume forgiveness, and resent any reminder that they still owe discipline or performance. The chapter is not romanticizing enemies. It is attacking lazy trust.

That is why the law keeps surfacing in modern work. A founder hires an old friend and assumes old intimacy will absorb missed deadlines, role confusion, and sloppy decisions. It usually does the opposite. The friend feels too comfortable to respect limits, too familiar to hear correction cleanly, and too emotionally entitled to accept hard accountability. Meanwhile, the capable rival from another company, the one everyone thought would be difficult, arrives with clearer incentives: prove value, protect the bargain, and avoid giving anyone a reason to regret the choice.

The same pattern appears in school and personal life. A student council president may trust a close friend with campaign finances because the friendship feels safer than procedure. A former opponent, by contrast, might track every responsibility with care because the alliance still feels earned, not assumed. In family business, the childhood friend given a role out of loyalty may start treating the shop as an extension of personal access. The relative outsider who once competed with you may behave more carefully because they know their place in the arrangement is conditional.

Still, Greene's argument becomes ugly if you read it too broadly. Enemies are not automatically better people, and rivalry does not wash away malice. Some enemies remain destructive because the hatred is stronger than the incentive to cooperate. Some friends remain the most trustworthy people in your life because their loyalty is real and tested. The chapter works only if you preserve its actual claim: do not trust labels more than structures. Trust what the relationship does under pressure, what the incentives reward, and how clearly each side understands the bargain.

That limit matters because suspicion can become its own blindness. A person who treats every friend as a future traitor and every enemy as a useful asset will eventually poison their own judgment. They may miss the stable ally in front of them because cynicism feels sophisticated. Greene is sharper than that when read carefully. The point is not to love enemies and despise friends. The point is to stop letting emotional familiarity do all your thinking for you.

So Chapter 2 expands the first law in an important way. Chapter 1 showed that admiration from above can crack under status threat. Chapter 2 shows that closeness beside you can crack under envy, entitlement, or blurred accountability. Both laws are really about misreading people because you prefer a comforting story to a harsher structure. Once you stop trusting the label friend to tell you how a person will move, the next problem becomes harder still: if names and loyalties are unstable, how much of your intention should you reveal to anyone at all?
