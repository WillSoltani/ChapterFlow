Running after someone else's timing often feels active, but it usually means you have already accepted their field. Greene's eighth law focuses on that hidden concession. Power grows when other people have to move toward you, because movement is not just distance. It is tempo, initiative, and the loss of a little control. The person who chases often accepts the urgency, structure, and emotional frame set by the other side before the real contest even begins.

That is why the chapter treats movement itself as leverage. If you can keep your position while inducing the other person to cross the distance, you are making them spend energy, reveal interest, and adjust to your ground first. Greene's point is not that stillness by itself is magical. Passive waiting is often just weakness in disguise. The stronger move is controlled invitation: create a structure in which the other side feels compelled, tempted, or rewarded for approaching on terms that already favor you.

Bait is the mechanism that makes that invitation work. It does not have to mean crude trickery. It can be an apparent opportunity, a useful opening, a timed offer, a visible gap, or a small inducement that makes the other side start moving. Once they move, they begin revealing priorities and accepting your tempo. This is the chapter's real logic. The lure matters because it changes who is advancing and who is receiving the advance.

That difference can be subtle in practice. A manager who structures access so requests have to come through a chosen process is not merely being difficult. A student fair booth placed and framed to attract approach is not just decoration. A tense personal relationship in which one person stops chasing and instead creates a calmer, more attractive ground is not passivity. In each case, the field changes because one side no longer spends itself pursuing while the other side stays free to choose terms.

But the law weakens if bait becomes obvious, desperate, or too expensive. Crude inducement can expose anxiety instead of control. Overdesigned lures can make the operator look needy, manipulative, or trapped in their own game. This is the chapter's boundary condition. The point is to control movement, not to become obsessed with schemes. If the bait costs too much, signals your dependence too clearly, or still leaves you reacting to the response, then the tactic has collapsed into another form of chasing.

This limit is what keeps the law from becoming a slogan about never moving first. Some situations require direct action. Some opponents will not bite. Some opportunities disappear if you wait for the perfect approach. Greene's harder claim is narrower. When movement can be induced rather than donated, the person who sets the field usually keeps more initiative. The person who rushes in first often shows more need than they intended.

Read after the chapter on credit, this one shifts the focus from who owns the visible result to who controls the preconditions of contact. Chapter 7 asked who gets remembered once the work is already visible. Chapter 8 asks who gets to decide how contact begins in the first place. That is why bait matters so much. It is less about deception than about architecture. If you can make others approach what you have already arranged, then you are not just replying to the game. You are shaping the board they have to step onto. Once contact happens on that board, the next question becomes sharper: when arguments begin, what actually wins, words or visible action?
