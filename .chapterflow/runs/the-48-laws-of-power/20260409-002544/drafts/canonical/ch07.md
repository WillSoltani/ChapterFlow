People often remember the face on the result more clearly than the hands that built it. That gap between labor and remembered authorship is where Greene places the seventh law. Power does not always gather around the person who did the most work. It often gathers around the person who became the visible owner of the outcome. The chapter is sharp because it is not mainly about laziness or efficiency. It is about memory, ownership, and the politics of association.

This matters because labor and credit do not move through social life in the same way. Labor can remain hidden inside drafts, prototypes, revisions, and background problem-solving. Credit tends to stick to the public claimant, the speaker at the meeting, the byline on the page, the manager attached to the finished output, or the leader whose name the result helps enlarge. Greene treats that difference as leverage. A person who can organize, absorb, or frame other people's effort may appear larger than someone who merely works alone, because public memory is usually simpler than actual contribution.

That is the law's strongest point: remembered authorship often matters more politically than solitary effort. Delegation can extend reach. Borrowed skill can raise the scale of what you seem able to produce. Visible ownership can then convert that scale into status. In practice, people do not always ask who soldered the prototype, who corrected the logic, who wrote the cleanest paragraph, or who fixed the draft the night before publication. They remember who presented the finished object, whose name sat closest to it, and who looked like the directing mind behind it.

But the chapter becomes weaker if it is read as simple theft without consequence. Hidden contributors are not inert material. They can resent invisibility, withhold future effort, defect, expose the arrangement, or become enemies who know exactly where your image is vulnerable. This is why Greene's law is politically realistic rather than carefree. Captured credit can build power fast, but it also produces relational debts and latent threats. The more public the ownership gap becomes, the more unstable the arrangement can turn.

That instability shows the difference between real command and hollow appropriation. Some visible owners truly do shape the result through direction, synthesis, timing, or strategic framing. Others simply stand nearest to the spotlight and absorb what more capable people produced. The first type may be resented, but it still has some real authority over the process. The second type builds a weaker structure. If the contributors disappear, rebel, or stop lending ability, the visible owner may have very little left that can sustain the reputation they captured.

This makes the law more morally charged than the previous chapter on attention. Chapter 6 explained how to become visible at all. Chapter 7 asks what happens once the result is visible and the audience starts assigning authorship. The warning is that public memory is often unfairly compressed. The same compression that helps a leader look decisive can erase the people who made decisiveness possible. Greene wants the reader to see that this is a usable mechanism, but the chapter still needs the limit in view: authority built on absorbed credit can become brittle if it outruns real command.

Modern settings make the mechanism easy to recognize. A manager presents a team win as if it reflected a singular strategic vision. A student editor receives praise for an issue carried by invisible line edits and background reporting. A socially skilled friend becomes the public face of a shared project and later gets remembered as the one who "made it happen." None of these cases are identical, but all of them turn on the same fact: the public prefers a visible owner to a complicated chain of contribution. That preference can be exploited, but it can also become dangerous if the visible owner starts believing the simplified story completely.

The serious reading, then, is neither naive teamwork advice nor cartoon villainy. It is a study in how outcomes get socially attached to names. If you want power, you need to understand who gets remembered when many hands produce one result. If you are the visible owner, you need enough real command to keep the machine working and enough caution not to turn contributors into saboteurs. If you are the hidden contributor, you need to decide whether invisible labor is buying access, protection, and future leverage, or whether you are only financing someone else's rise. Once that question lands, the next law follows naturally: if ownership is unstable and people matter this much, how do you get them to move toward your terms instead of chasing them on theirs?
