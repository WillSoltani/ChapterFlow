You are often judged before anyone has actually dealt with you. A hiring panel reads one line of background, a team remembers a previous handoff, a neighbor repeats a story over coffee, and by the time you arrive the room has already decided whether to lean in, hold back, or prepare for trouble. Greene's fifth law treats that advance judgment as a real form of power. Reputation matters because it lets your image walk ahead of your body and start shaping the field before you get a chance to explain yourself.

That works because most people cannot inspect character directly. They infer. They look at visible behavior, recurring signals, and what other people say. They remember whether your name tends to arrive next to steadiness, sharpness, carelessness, or drama. This makes reputation less mystical than it first sounds. It is not a secret essence. It is the shortcut other people use when they do not have time, access, or patience to know more. The shortcut may be unfair, but it is still active. A strong reputation can therefore reduce resistance before the first move. A damaged one can make every later explanation sound like repair work.

The chapter gets sharper when it argues that one outstanding quality often works better than a blur of moderate virtues. If people must simplify, it is better for them to simplify in a way that helps you. A person known for reliability enters the room differently from a person who is vaguely thought of as nice, smart, busy, and hard to read all at once. The first image is usable. The second is fog. One clear trait gives anticipation somewhere to settle. It also acts as a filter. Later actions get interpreted through that anchor, which means a good reputation does more than impress. It organizes how evidence will be read.

That is why reputation behaves like a shield. It covers what is less flattering, or at least slows how quickly people move toward it. Someone with a strong reputation for competence can survive a small wobble because observers are inclined to treat the mistake as an exception. Someone with a weak or unstable image does not get the same mercy. The same wobble becomes confirmation. Greene's point is not that the shield is magical. It only works while enough visible conduct keeps supporting it. But while it holds, it changes timing, and timing matters. A person with reputational credit gets more room to recover before doubt hardens.

The repair side of the chapter is more disciplined than frantic denial. When reputation is damaged, argument alone often fails because people hear it as self-protection. What helps more is repeated visible conduct and, sometimes, association with a steadier or more trusted image. In ordinary life this can look almost boring. A project manager who misses one handoff does not fix the damage by sending six long explanations in Slack. She fixes it by delivering the next few handoffs cleanly and by tying her work to processes or partners the team already trusts. A student editor who lets a factual mistake into the paper does not fully repair credibility by insisting everyone knows it was a rare slip. He repairs it by issuing the correction clearly, tightening the fact-check routine, and letting the paper's next issues show that accuracy is back in command.

The offensive side is where readers often get sloppy. Greene does allow that weakening another person's reputation can shift power, because once the story around them changes, the room starts treating them differently. But the chapter does not support reckless rumor as some effortless superweapon. Reputational fights are noisy, sticky, and hard to contain. If the attack is crude, unsupported, or obviously self-serving, it damages the attacker too. Even a successful hit can create a cycle of mutual dirt that lowers trust all around. This is the boundary the chapter needs. Reputation is powerful precisely because it lives in social anticipation. That also means it is dangerous to handle carelessly. Once you turn every conflict into an image war, you stop controlling the field and start poisoning it.

This is why the law belongs right after the chapter on saying less. Chapter 4 focused on the moment when words and silences shape how people size you up. Chapter 5 widens the frame and asks what happens after those moments accumulate. A room remembers patterns. It forms a story. Then that story starts arriving before you do. Guarding reputation, in the serious sense, means guarding the repeated signals that build that story and resisting the panic that makes repair look theatrical. You are not trying to seem perfect. You are trying to make sure the first thing that reaches people is a strength strong enough to organize expectation rather than a weakness strong enough to organize doubt. Once that image starts moving ahead of you, the next problem is obvious: if being seen matters this much, what happens when attention itself becomes a weapon?
