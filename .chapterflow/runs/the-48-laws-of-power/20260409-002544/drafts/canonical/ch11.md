Greene's eleventh law starts from an uncomfortable point: appreciation is weaker than necessity. People may praise you, thank you, or enjoy working with you and still replace you the moment a smoother option appears. The steadier source of leverage is different. It comes from occupying a role, holding a form of access, or coordinating a function that others cannot easily reproduce on short notice. The chapter is not really about being admired. It is about becoming costly to remove.

That is why substitution risk sits at the center of the law. If the same result can be obtained from many hands with little friction, then your value remains vulnerable no matter how pleasant your reputation is. Greene's logic is colder than that. Power grows when your usefulness is tied to something others depend on and cannot swap out without losing time, clarity, or control. Replaceability weakens your position even when people say they appreciate you.

The strongest version of this move does not require theatrics. Dependence can grow because you understand the handoff map nobody else has learned, because you hold a trusted relationship others cannot yet access, or because you keep a complicated system working across several moving pieces. In each case, the leverage comes from irreplaceable function rather than from sentimental attachment. The chapter's mechanism is structural necessity, not emotional indebtedness.

That distinction matters because the law is easy to vulgarize. If you read it as a license for sabotage, hoarding, or deliberate confusion, the strategy starts rotting from inside. Artificial dependence teaches people to resent you, route around you, or build backups in secret. Greene's law is stronger when it stays on usefulness that is genuinely difficult to substitute. The manipulative version may create dependence for a while, but it also teaches everyone nearby that freedom requires your removal.

You can see the difference in ordinary settings. One employee becomes hard to remove because she knows how client promises, schedules, and technical constraints fit together in one usable sequence. One student in a robotics lab becomes central because he can translate between design, wiring, and testing when the team gets stuck. In a relationship, someone may become relied upon because they bring steadiness and coordination that keep shared life from splintering. The question is always the same: what function becomes costly to lose?

The limit is just as important as the mechanism. Visible gatekeeping, theatrical indispensability, and obvious monopolizing can turn leverage brittle. Once people feel trapped, they start documenting your moves, training substitutes, or redesigning the system to break your hold. Dependence can lock in value, but only while it still feels connected to real contribution rather than to coercive scarcity. The backlash threshold is crossed when your necessity begins looking manufactured.

Chapter 10 asked how to keep recurring disorder from contaminating the field. Chapter 11 asks what becomes powerful once the field is cleaner: the person whose steadiness, knowledge, or coordination others cannot easily replace. That question sets up the next turn. If necessity creates leverage, then how can honesty or generosity lower resistance without giving the leverage away?
