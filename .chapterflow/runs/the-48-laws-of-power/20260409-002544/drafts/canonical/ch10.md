Greene's tenth law begins with a hard warning about proximity. Some people do not keep their disorder contained inside themselves. Their panic, resentment, repeated bad judgment, or chronic crisis-making spills outward into the people and systems around them. That is why the chapter treats unhappiness and instability as possible exposure risks rather than as purely private pain. The issue is not mood alone. It is repeated spillover.

This spillover can work in several directions at once. Emotionally, a chaotic person's fear or bitterness can pull others into defensive thinking. Practically, their lateness, volatility, or constant emergencies can keep bending plans around them. Reputationally, staying too close to obvious disorder can make you look less discerning, less reliable, or more entangled than you realized. Greene's point is that repeated contact changes your field, not just your feelings.

That is why the law favors selective distance. If one person's recurring chaos keeps becoming your judgment problem, your morale problem, or your timing problem, then closeness is no longer neutral. Boundaries protect clarity. Distance can keep one person's spiral from becoming the atmosphere everyone else has to breathe. In that sense, the chapter is about filtering exposure before contamination becomes normal.

But the law becomes ugly if it is read carelessly. Not every unhappy person is contagious. Not every unlucky run reveals character. Some people are simply in pain and do not spread chaos into everyone near them. The chapter is strongest when it distinguishes isolated hardship from repeated spillover. Greene is warning against recurring disorder that keeps becoming your problem too, not against ordinary struggle itself.

This makes the law practical in ordinary settings. A team can notice that one coworker's constant crisis pattern keeps warping deadlines and morale. A theater crew can recognize when one volatile cast member turns every rehearsal into collective instability. A friendship can reach the point where compassion without boundaries becomes a pipeline for repeated mess. In each case, the question is whether exposure keeps multiplying harm.

That limit matters because false compassion and false superiority are both failures here. Unlimited exposure can turn empathy into self-erasure. Blanket avoidance can turn discernment into contempt. Greene's harder claim is narrower: when another person's repeated pattern keeps contaminating judgment, stability, or opportunity, distance may be the cleaner move. The point is not cruelty. It is to stop pretending spillover is harmless once the pattern is already obvious.

Chapter 9 asked what kind of proof ends a contest better than argument. Chapter 10 asks what kind of environment lets clear judgment survive long enough for proof to matter at all. Once the field is protected from contagion, the next question becomes sharper: how do you make your own steadiness so valuable that people depend on it?
