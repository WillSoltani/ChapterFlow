Excellent work can disappear if nobody notices it. In crowded rooms, crowded markets, and crowded institutions, obscurity is not a neutral condition. It is a strategic weakness. Greene's sixth law pushes this point hard: attention is not only vanity fuel. It is often the gate through which influence has to pass before anything else can happen. If no one looks up, no one interprets you, remembers you, or adjusts their behavior around you.

That is why this chapter treats visibility as a kind of leverage. People sort the world quickly. They scan for what breaks pattern, what glints, what seems larger than the background. A startling quality, a memorable surface, a strong contrast with the ordinary crowd: these things pull the eye before they prove anything deeper. Greene is not claiming that attention automatically deserves respect. He is claiming that attention decides who even gets the chance to become consequential. The unseen person may be competent, serious, and useful, but invisibility keeps all of that trapped behind a closed door.

The chapter gets more interesting when it adds mystery. Distinctiveness gets attention once. Mystery keeps attention circulating. When people cannot fully place someone, they keep interpreting. They talk, speculate, and watch for the next clue. That interpretive motion magnifies visibility because the person remains unfinished in the public mind. Greene uses Mata Hari in this narrow sense. Her layered and shifting stories of origin sustained fascination because they kept people working on the problem of who she really was. The power in the example is not glamour alone. It is the way uncertainty keeps the audience returning.

But attention cools if it becomes too familiar. What startled once becomes routine if it never changes shape. Greene therefore builds a maintenance rule into the law: once you have visibility, vary the act enough that boredom does not replace fascination. In practical terms, this means not repeating the exact same signal until it hardens into parody. The person who becomes known for one vivid move still has to keep the move alive by altering timing, surface, or setting. Otherwise the crowd stops leaning in and starts looking past them.

This makes the law more serious than cheap provocation. Attention is not useful just because it is loud. The wrong kind of notice can fix the wrong reputation. A person can become visible as shallow, reckless, annoying, or unserious, and that kind of visibility creates as many problems as invisibility did. Workplaces make this obvious. Someone who gets noticed only for theatrics may never get trusted with substance. Schools make it obvious too. A student who becomes memorable as disruptive cannot easily convert that spotlight into authority. Greene's sharper point is that attention must serve a larger image rather than detach from it.

There is also a hierarchy limit, which keeps the chapter from canceling the first law. Attention can backfire if it humiliates a superior or turns your visibility into a threat above you. The move that works in one field may become dangerous in another. Distinctiveness is safest when it makes you hard to ignore without making the wrong person feel eclipsed. This is part of why mystery can be safer than blunt self-display. It lets attention gather without always forcing a direct public contest over status.

Read in sequence, the law deepens the previous chapter instead of replacing it. Chapter 5 argued that reputation lets an image travel ahead of you. Chapter 6 asks a prior and harsher question: what if the image never gets enough attention to travel very far at all? Reputation needs visibility, and visibility needs some form of contrast. The serious lesson is not to become a clown for the crowd. It is to understand that being noticed is often the first strategic victory, and that it has to be won carefully. You need enough distinctiveness to rise above the background, enough mystery to keep interpretation alive, and enough judgment to avoid becoming famous for the wrong thing. Once attention is secured, a new contest begins: in a world where many hands help create visible outcomes, who gets remembered as the author of what everyone can see?
