Core Claim:
The thirty-fifth law argues that power depends not only on what you do but on when you do it. Greene's point is not that waiting is always wiser than acting or that timing can overcome every structural weakness. It is that ripeness, patience, tempo, and sense of occasion often decide whether force meets readiness or wastes itself against a field that is too early, too late, or emotionally wrong. The chapter is strongest when it stays on timing judgment, strategic patience, and seizing the ripe moment rather than on passive delay or mystical fate talk.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
It shifts from setting a status floor through bearing into choosing the moment when pressure, request, move, or display can actually land. Chapter 34 showed how people price your worth partly through your bearing; Chapter 35 asks when action should appear so that worth is felt as timely rather than premature, needy, or stale.

Author Logic Chain:
First the author argues that events unfold in rhythms, moods, and stages rather than in a flat sequence where any moment works equally well.
Then the author shows that patience, observation, and control of tempo help a person recognize when conditions have ripened.
Then the author concludes that power grows when action meets the right moment and weakens when it is rushed, delayed, or badly timed.

Common Misreadings:
- treating the chapter as praise for passivity
- flattening it into generic patience advice
- assuming good timing means avoiding decisive action
- confusing rhythm-reading with superstition or fate language

Counterarguments or Limits:
Some situations reward immediate action because the opening is brief, the cost of delay is high, or waiting signals weakness. The chapter overreaches if it becomes advice to postpone hard choices forever in the name of perfect timing. The useful limit is to wait when conditions are still forming but to move once the opening is real, rather than mistaking indefinite delay for wisdom.
