Core Claim:
The fifteenth law argues that partial defeat is unstable because unfinished enemies can regroup, seek revenge, or return in a new form. Greene's claim is not that force should be used casually everywhere. It is that half-measures against serious opposition can leave the threat alive. The chapter is strongest when it stays on recurrence risk, unfinished closure, and return danger rather than on spectacle. Its limit matters too. If the output turns the law into generic bloodlust, it will overstate the frozen support and lose the chapter's logic.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
It is the book's clearest argument that unfinished conflict can be more dangerous than decisive closure. The chapter is not general aggression advice. It is about the instability of leaving an enemy able to come back.

Author Logic Chain:
First the author argues that injured or humiliated enemies often retain motive and capacity to return.
Then the author shows that incomplete victory creates revenge, regrouping, or renewed threat.
Then the author concludes that total neutralization is presented as more stable than partial suppression, though the claim must be kept narrow.

Counterarguments or Limits:
The law overreaches when it treats all conflict as total war. Many ordinary disputes should be de-escalated, not maximized. Still, the chapter insists that serious opposition left partly alive can become a recurring threat. The output needs the line between recurrence logic and performative severity.
