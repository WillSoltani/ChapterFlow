Core Claim:
The eighteenth law argues that isolation feels protective but often makes a person weaker by cutting them off from information, circulation, contact, and adjustment. Greene's point is not that boundaries are useless. It is that fortress thinking can narrow your awareness, make you easier to corner, and leave you dependent on stale assumptions. The chapter is strongest when it stays on information loss, reduced adaptability, and the danger of mistaking withdrawal for security.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
It is the book's pivot from making yourself less readable to recognizing that too much distance creates blindness. Chapter 17 values strategic uncertainty; Chapter 18 warns that total retreat can become strategic self-harm.

Author Logic Chain:
First the author argues that isolation feels safe because it reduces exposure.
Then the author shows that withdrawal also cuts off information, flexibility, and living contact with the field.
Then the author concludes that protection becomes dangerous when a person mistakes enclosure for control.

Specific Applications:

- distinguishing healthy privacy from defensive isolation
- keeping contact, feedback, and movement instead of sealing yourself off
- recognizing that retreat can create stale assumptions and blind spots

Common Misreadings:
- treating the chapter as a command for constant social exposure
- assuming all boundaries are weakness
- reducing the law to extroversion advice
- ignoring the role of judgment, selective access, and prudent distance

Counterarguments or Limits:
Some distance is necessary for rest, discernment, and protection. The chapter overreaches if it becomes advice against all privacy or all guardedness. The useful limit is that circulation should preserve awareness without collapsing into indiscriminate openness.
