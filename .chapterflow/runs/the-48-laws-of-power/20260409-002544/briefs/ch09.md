Core Claim:
The ninth law argues that visible results usually change people more effectively than verbal victory does. Greene supports that claim by treating argument as a risky arena where opponents often feel cornered, humiliated, or pushed into resistance even when the logic is sound. The chapter is strongest when it stays on that mechanism of demonstrable proof. Action matters because embodied outcomes can settle the issue without forcing the other side to concede in words. The law's limit matters too. Action can fail when it is vague, symbolic without substance, or detached from the actual disagreement. The point is not to ban explanation. It is to show why proof often outranks verbal triumph.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
It is the book's clearest argument that proof changes the field more deeply than persuasion contests do. The chapter is not generic anti-debate advice. It is about the superior force of outcomes that people have to live with rather than arguments they can keep resisting.

Author Logic Chain:
First the author argues that argument often hardens ego and invites verbal resistance.
Then the author shows that visible action or demonstrated results can bypass that resistance by changing reality instead of merely describing it.
Then the author concludes that proof is stronger than verbal victory, but only when the action is relevant, legible, and tied to the disputed point.

Specific Applications:
- replacing repeated explanation with visible execution that answers the disputed point
- recognizing when verbal correction is only making the other side defend themselves harder
- designing proof that is concrete enough to settle doubt without grandstanding
- knowing when explanation is still needed because the action alone would remain ambiguous

Common Misreadings:
- treating the law as contempt for all discussion
- assuming silence alone counts as action
- confusing theatrical display with relevant proof
- treating humiliation through results as the main goal

Counterarguments or Limits:
The law overreaches when it treats every argument as worthless or every action as self-explanatory. Some conflicts do require explanation, and some proofs are too ambiguous to carry their meaning alone. Action can also become manipulative theater if it is staged mainly to dominate perception rather than to settle the disputed issue. The chapter needs the line between relevant demonstration and empty gesture.
