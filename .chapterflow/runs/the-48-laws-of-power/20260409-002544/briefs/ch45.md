Core Claim:
The forty-fifth law argues that change often fails when reform outruns what people can absorb, even if the reform itself is necessary. Greene's point is not that continuity is always sacred or that change should be avoided. It is that abrupt transformation can provoke fear, resistance, and backlash when people feel their world is being overturned all at once. The chapter is strongest when it stays on pacing, absorption, and backlash rather than generic caution.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
It shifts from unsettling others through reflection into managing collective reaction to change itself. Chapter 44 asked how self-image can be destabilized by the mirror; Chapter 45 asks how reform can proceed without destabilizing the whole field unnecessarily.

Author Logic Chain:
First the author argues that people resist change when it arrives too abruptly.
Then the author shows that pacing reform can preserve legitimacy while still moving the system.
Then the author concludes that successful change often depends as much on speed and sequencing as on the reform's content.

Common Misreadings:
- treating the chapter as anti-change advice
- flattening it into generic caution
- assuming gradualism is always wiser
- confusing prudent pacing with cowardice or delay for its own sake

Counterarguments or Limits:
Some systems need urgent reform, and some gradualism only protects a bad status quo. The chapter overreaches if it becomes a defense of inertia or endless incrementalism. The useful limit is to pace change strategically without letting prudence become an excuse for doing nothing.
