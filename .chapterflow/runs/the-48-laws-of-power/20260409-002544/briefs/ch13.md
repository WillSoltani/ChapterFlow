Core Claim:
The thirteenth law argues that help is more reliable when the request clearly serves the other person's interest. Greene's claim is not that gratitude never matters. It is that mercy, loyalty, and remembered favors are weaker engines of action than visible benefit or avoided loss. The chapter is strongest when it stays on incentive alignment rather than emotional pleading. Its limit matters too. If the self-interest appeal feels too naked, one-sided, or manipulative, the request can become insulting and backfire.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
It is the book's clearest argument that people cooperate more steadily when they can see what they gain. The chapter is not generic relationship advice. It is about framing help requests around incentives.

Author Logic Chain:
First the author argues that mercy and gratitude are unstable motives for action.
Then the author shows that people respond more reliably when a request highlights benefit, protection, or advantage for them.
Then the author concludes that the move works only while the incentive looks credible and not crudely exploitative.

Counterarguments or Limits:
The law overreaches when it treats people as purely transactional. Real relationships involve memory, obligation, and trust. Still, the chapter insists that action is more dependable when the request shows concrete benefit. The output needs the line between realistic incentive framing and transparent opportunism.
