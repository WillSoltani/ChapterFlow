Chapter Title: Disdain Things You Cannot Have: Ignoring Them Is the Best Revenge
Position In Book: thirty-sixth law; turns from timing into refusal, showing how power can be preserved by denying unavailable objects the attention, complaint, and emotional tribute they seek from you

Core Claim:
The thirty-sixth law argues that fixation on what you cannot have can lower status, leak emotional control, and strengthen the thing refusing you. Greene's point is not that desire should never be felt or that every loss can be shrugged off honestly on command. It is that visible frustration often grants more importance to the denied object, while disdain and strategic inattention can preserve leverage, dignity, and revenge through non-response. The chapter is strongest when it stays on refusal of fixation, status preservation, and emotional economy rather than on numb denial or fake indifference.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
It shifts from deciding when to move into deciding when not to keep pursuing. Chapter 35 showed that some openings depend on timing; Chapter 36 asks what happens when the opening is gone, the object is denied, or pursuit itself starts to make you smaller.

Author Logic Chain:
First the author argues that complaint and visible frustration can increase the power of what denies or escapes you.
Then the author shows that strategic inattention can reduce humiliation, deny tribute, and keep status from collapsing into fixation.
Then the author concludes that some forms of revenge or recovery come not from more pursuit but from refusing the denied object further emotional energy.

Specific Applications:
- noticing when complaint is strengthening the object that denied you
- deciding when more pursuit is shrinking your leverage instead of recovering it
- distinguishing genuine release from fake indifference
- refusing to let humiliation become a public performance of dependence

Common Misreadings:
- treating the chapter as praise for emotional numbness
- flattening it into generic stoicism slogans
- assuming every denied object should always be ignored
- confusing strategic inattention with refusal to learn from loss

Counterarguments or Limits:
Some losses require direct repair, open grief, or renewed effort because the object is still recoverable and the situation is not actually closed. The chapter overreaches if it becomes advice to call every wound beneath notice or to treat all disappointment as weakness. The useful limit is to stop feeding what cannot be had with more tribute while still facing reality honestly enough to know when action remains possible.
