Core Claim:
The eleventh law argues that power grows when your usefulness becomes difficult to replace. Greene supports that claim by treating dependence as structural leverage rather than as affection or goodwill. The chapter is strongest when it stays on necessity, substitution, and irreplaceable function. It is not generic advice about being liked. It is about making your role or value costly to remove. The law's limit matters too. Dependence built through hoarding, sabotage, or transparent manipulation can create resentment, brittleness, and eventual backlash.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
It is the book's clearest argument that necessity locks in value more securely than appreciation does. The chapter is not general career self-improvement. It is about leverage created by becoming hard to substitute.

Author Logic Chain:
First the author argues that people control what they can reliably provide and others cannot easily replace.
Then the author shows that dependence can be built through unique function, access, coordination, or protected expertise.
Then the author concludes that indispensability creates leverage, but only while it does not become so obvious or abusive that others start trying to break free.

Common Misreadings:
- treating the law as simple sabotage or hoarding
- confusing usefulness with total control
- assuming dependence never creates backlash
- reducing the chapter to generic “be valuable” slogans

Counterarguments or Limits:
The law overreaches when it treats dependence as infinitely stable. People often resent visible reliance and eventually build workarounds, backups, or revolt against overt control. The chapter needs the line between authentic necessity and brittle manipulation.
