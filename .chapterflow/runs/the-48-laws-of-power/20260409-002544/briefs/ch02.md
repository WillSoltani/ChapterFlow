Core Claim:
The second law argues that friendship often distorts judgment because affection, gratitude, and history hide envy or entitlement. Greene supports that claim by contrasting Michael III's trust in Basilius, which ended in betrayal, with Talleyrand's tactical use of former enemy Fouche, whose incentives were cleaner and more legible. The law's core mechanism is not that enemies are morally better. It is that declared friction can produce clearer boundaries, while friendship can encourage blindness. The chapter is strongest when it treats enemy use as disciplined realism rather than paranoid misanthropy.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
It is the book's sharpest challenge to sentimental trust. The chapter is not about betrayal in general. It is about how familiarity and gratitude can hide dangerous emotional distortions, while rivalry can sometimes produce more professional behavior.

Author Logic Chain:
First the author argues that friends can become ungrateful, envious, and sloppy because they feel entitled to access.
Then the author contrasts that blur with former enemies, who often work under clearer incentives and tighter boundaries.
Then the author concludes that trust should be based on readable incentives rather than comforting labels.

Common Misreadings:
- assuming the law says enemies are automatically loyal
- treating friendship as always naive or useless
- turning the chapter into pure cynicism about all human bonds

Counterarguments or Limits:
The law overreaches if it becomes a universal suspicion ethic. Some friends are trustworthy, and some enemies remain destructive no matter what incentive you offer. The chapter needs to preserve the distinction between readable incentives and fantasy, not between good people and bad labels.
