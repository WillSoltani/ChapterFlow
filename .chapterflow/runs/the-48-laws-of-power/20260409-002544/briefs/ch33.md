Core Claim:
The thirty-third law argues that influence often becomes stronger when it is aimed at the specific fear, craving, vanity, dependency, or vulnerability that actually moves a person. Greene's point is not that everyone has one simple button or that leverage is permanently fixed. It is that generic pressure wastes force, while precise reading lets power work through what matters most to the individual in front of you. The chapter is strongest when it stays on pressure-point diagnosis, hidden motives, and person-specific leverage rather than on cartoonish weakness hunting.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
It shifts from broad desire framing into exact leverage mapping. Chapter 32 showed how fantasy can attract generally; Chapter 33 asks what exact opening, sensitivity, or need turns general attraction into personal control.

Author Logic Chain:
First the author argues that people are moved by specific hidden motives rather than by generic pressure alone.
Then the author shows that observing fears, vanities, cravings, and dependencies reveals leverage points.
Then the author concludes that influence sharpens when it is directed at the exact point where resistance is weakest or desire is strongest.

Common Misreadings:
- treating the chapter as praise for sadism
- flattening it into generic psychology slogans
- assuming one observed weakness defines a whole person permanently
- confusing careful reading with invented motives unsupported by behavior

Counterarguments or Limits:
Some people shift across contexts, hide motives well, or cannot be reduced to a single leverage point. The chapter overreaches if it becomes advice to dehumanize others into one weakness or to press a vulnerability with no regard for context or consequence. The useful limit is to read carefully, test gently, and remain revisable rather than treating the first apparent weakness as final truth.
