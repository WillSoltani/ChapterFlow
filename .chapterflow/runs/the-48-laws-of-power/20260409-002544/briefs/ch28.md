Core Claim:
The twenty-eighth law argues that action often works better when entered with boldness rather than visible timidity or half-commitment. Greene's point is not that every risk should be taken blindly or that preparation is useless. It is that hesitation invites doubt, resistance, and opportunistic counterpressure, while decisive action can create momentum and perception advantages before the outcome is fully known. The chapter is strongest when it stays on bold execution, confidence effects, and the costs of hesitation rather than on generic motivational slogans.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
It shifts from organizing belief and aura into proving that aura through visible action. Chapter 27 showed how loyalty and emotional certainty can be created; Chapter 28 asks how boldness keeps that structure from thinning into theater once action begins.

Author Logic Chain:
First the author argues that hesitation signals weakness and invites resistance.
Then the author shows that bold, decisive action can create psychological and positional advantage.
Then the author concludes that confidence in execution often shapes the field before results fully settle.

Specific Applications:

- distinguishing strategic boldness from reckless stunt behavior
- using confidence to create early momentum without ignoring feedback

Common Misreadings:
- treating the chapter as praise for recklessness in every case
- flattening it into generic "be confident" advice
- assuming boldness means no preparation or revision
- confusing decisive execution with loud theatrical aggression

Counterarguments or Limits:
Some situations reward patience, concealment, or staged preparation more than immediate visible boldness. The chapter overreaches if it becomes advice to act impulsively without calibration. The useful limit is to enter action decisively where hesitation would weaken position, while still keeping enough judgment to avoid preventable overreach.
