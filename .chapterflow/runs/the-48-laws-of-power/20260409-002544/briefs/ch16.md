Core Claim:
The sixteenth law argues that overpresence can reduce value while selective absence can increase respect, honor, and perceived importance. Greene's claim is not that disappearance is always wise. It is that familiarity, constant availability, and saturation can make a person easier to ignore or devalue. The chapter is strongest when it stays on scarcity logic, attention economics, and the loss of distinction through overexposure.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
It is the book's clearest move from force into scarcity. Instead of asking how to defeat or manipulate pressure directly, the chapter asks how less presence can create more value.

Author Logic Chain:
First the author argues that constant visibility can produce overfamiliarity and reduce respect.
Then the author shows that scarcity and selective absence can increase attention and perceived importance.
Then the author concludes that absence works best when it interrupts saturation without collapsing trust or disappearing irresponsibly.

Common Misreadings:
- treating the law as permission to ghost people indiscriminately
- confusing strategic scarcity with unreliability
- assuming all visibility lowers value
- using absence so heavily that trust collapses

Counterarguments or Limits:
Absence can increase value only when some foundation of regard already exists and when withdrawal does not read as neglect or irresponsibility. The chapter overreaches if it becomes generic nonresponse advice.
