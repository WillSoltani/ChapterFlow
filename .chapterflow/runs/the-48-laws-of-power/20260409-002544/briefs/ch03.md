Core Claim:
The third law argues that declared intention often gives other people too much time and clarity. Once they can read your aim, they can defend, delay, flatter, or sabotage it. Greene supports that claim with the Marquis de Sevigne's failed seduction, which collapses the moment he reveals the game, and Otto von Bismarck's rise, which depends on sounding peaceful while pursuing a different objective. The law is strongest when it treats concealment as control over timing and legibility rather than as empty lying for its own sake.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
It is the book's clearest statement that opacity can be strategically useful because predictability invites resistance. The chapter is not just about dishonesty. It is about protecting initiative by keeping your real line of movement hard to map.

Author Logic Chain:
First the author argues that revealed motives let other people defend themselves against you.
Then the author shows that decoys, smokescreens, and bland surfaces keep your target misreading the situation.
Then the author concludes that concealment is useful when it protects timing and position, but dangerous when it turns into manipulative habit or needless paranoia.

Common Misreadings:

- assuming concealment means permanent silence in every setting

Counterarguments or Limits:
Concealment breaks down when it becomes identity rather than tactic. Some trust requires legibility, and overuse of ambiguity can poison alliances or turn you into someone who cannot act directly even when directness would work better. The chapter needs the line between protecting initiative and becoming addicted to manipulation.
