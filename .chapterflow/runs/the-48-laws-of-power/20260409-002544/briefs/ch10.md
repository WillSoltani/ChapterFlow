Core Claim:
The tenth law argues that some people spread disorder into the environments around them, and that repeated exposure to their chaos can damage your judgment, morale, reputation, or opportunities. Greene supports that claim by treating unhappiness and chronic bad patterning as strategically contagious rather than purely private. The chapter is strongest when it stays on that exposure logic. It is not a demand for cruelty. It is a warning that proximity matters, and that some forms of repeated contact carry reputational, emotional, or practical spillover. The law's limit matters too. Not every struggling person is contagious, and the chapter weakens when it becomes blanket contempt instead of selective boundary-setting.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
It is the book's clearest argument that environment quality and human proximity are strategic variables. The chapter is not generic positivity advice. It is about how another person's recurring disorder can become your exposure problem if you keep standing in its path.

Author Logic Chain:
First the author argues that repeated contact with unstable, chronically miserable, or self-sabotaging people can reshape your own judgment and conditions.
Then the author shows that contagion can be emotional, practical, and reputational rather than merely dramatic.
Then the author concludes that selective distance is often protective, but only when it is guided by pattern recognition rather than by callous superstition.

Common Misreadings:
- treating all struggling people as dangerous
- confusing empathy with total exposure
- using the law to justify cruelty or class contempt
- assuming contagion is mystical rather than practical

Counterarguments or Limits:
The law overreaches when it treats every unhappy person as infectious or every run of bad luck as proof of character. Some people are simply in pain and do not spread chaos outward. The chapter needs the line between repeated spillover and ordinary hardship. It also needs the reminder that selective distance is different from humiliation or moral superiority.
