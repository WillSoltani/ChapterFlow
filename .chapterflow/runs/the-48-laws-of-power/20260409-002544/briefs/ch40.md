Core Claim:
The fortieth law argues that what appears free often carries hidden cost, dependence, or loss of autonomy. Greene's point is not that generosity is always false or that every bargain hides manipulation. It is that gifts, shortcuts, and cheap access can create obligations, lower standards, or make the receiver easier to influence. The chapter is strongest when it stays on hidden cost, dependency, and self-valuing rather than on blanket suspicion or ingratitude.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
It shifts from exposing weakness in others into refusing the kind of dependence that makes weakness easier to exploit. Chapter 39 showed how disturbance can reveal instability; Chapter 40 asks how hidden cost and cheapness can quietly create it.

Author Logic Chain:
First the author argues that free or cheap offers often conceal a price not stated openly.
Then the author shows that dependence on gifts, bargains, or shortcuts can weaken judgment and freedom.
Then the author concludes that paying full cost, or at least seeing the real cost clearly, often preserves autonomy better than accepting the free lure.

Specific Applications:

- distinguishing fair exchange from dependency disguised as help
- refusing bargains that save money or effort now but reduce autonomy later

Common Misreadings:
- treating the chapter as praise for cynicism
- flattening it into generic anti-generosity advice
- assuming every gift is manipulative
- confusing prudent independence with performative self-sufficiency

Counterarguments or Limits:
Some generosity is real, some exchanges are fair, and some help is worth receiving without paranoia. The chapter overreaches if it becomes advice to reject all gifts, cooperation, or support as weakness. The useful limit is to see the true cost clearly and refuse the offers that quietly purchase your freedom, not to distrust every act of goodwill by default.
