Core Claim:
The thirty-seventh law argues that striking visible form can command attention and shape interpretation more forcefully than abstract explanation alone. Greene's point is not that spectacle should replace substance in every setting or that manipulation through flash is always justified. It is that human attention is often captured by image, symbol, scene, and dramatic form before it is organized by argument. The chapter is strongest when it stays on visible impact, attention capture, and symbolic staging rather than on empty showmanship.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
It shifts from refusing to feed unwanted attention into designing attention on purpose. Chapter 36 showed how denying tribute can shrink what refuses you; Chapter 37 asks how attention can be seized again through spectacle, image, and memorable visible arrangement.

Author Logic Chain:
First the author argues that people often respond more quickly to images and striking form than to abstraction alone.
Then the author shows that spectacle, symbol, and visible drama can simplify meaning and make power feel undeniable.
Then the author concludes that compelling display can direct attention and memory, provided the spectacle serves a real strategic point.

Counterarguments or Limits:
Some settings punish display, require sobriety, or demand argument that can stand without visual drama. The chapter overreaches if it becomes advice to replace meaning with flash or to trust sensation where credibility is thin. The useful limit is to use spectacle where visible form clarifies and concentrates power, not where it merely decorates weakness.
