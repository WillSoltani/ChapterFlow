Core Claim:
The thirtieth law argues that power often increases when heavy labor, design, and rehearsal are hidden behind a smooth visible result. Greene's point is not that effort is shameful or that outright lying is always acceptable. It is that exposed strain, cluttered process, and visible overengineering can weaken mystique, reduce persuasive force, and make accomplishment look smaller than it is. The chapter is strongest when it stays on concealment of labor, polished finish, and authority effects rather than generic "work hard quietly" slogans.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
It shifts from designing the full route into controlling the appearance of the finished result. Chapter 29 showed how deep planning protects the ending; Chapter 30 asks how that planned accomplishment should look once others finally see it.

Author Logic Chain:
First the author argues that visible strain and exposed machinery can shrink an accomplishment.
Then the author shows that polish, rehearsal, and concealed process can increase mystique and persuasive force.
Then the author concludes that power often lies not only in doing difficult things, but in making the result appear natural and easy.

Common Misreadings:
- treating the chapter as praise for outright fraud
- flattening it into generic "grind in silence" advice
- assuming visible effort is always weak in every context
- confusing polished presentation with denial that help, rehearsal, or labor existed

Counterarguments or Limits:
Some settings require transparency about process, collaboration, or difficulty, especially where safety, ethics, or fair credit matter. The chapter overreaches if it becomes advice to fake competence, erase contributors, or lie about the work. The useful limit is to hide clutter and strain that weaken the effect while staying honest about authorship, support, and material facts.
