Core Claim:
The seventh law argues that visible ownership often matters more politically than solitary labor. Greene supports that claim by treating power as a struggle over remembered authorship: people who can direct, absorb, or frame other people's effort may gain disproportionate credit because the public remembers the visible face attached to the result rather than the hidden contributors behind it. The chapter is strongest when it stays on that mechanism of appropriation, delegation, and memory. It is not just about efficiency. It is about who gets associated with the outcome. The law's limit is also essential. Credit capture can create resentment, dependence on exploited talent, and hollow authority if the visible owner cannot keep producing or cannot manage the contributors whose labor made the image possible.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
It is the book's clearest argument that authorship in public memory often diverges from actual labor. The chapter is not generic advice about teamwork or delegation. It is about how power accumulates around the person who becomes the remembered owner of a result.

Author Logic Chain:
First the author argues that directing or absorbing others' work can expand reach beyond what solitary effort allows.
Then the author shows that public memory usually attaches results to the most visible claimant rather than to the full chain of contributors.
Then the author concludes that taking credit can build power, but only under conditions where the visible owner can preserve authority without provoking damaging backlash.

Common Misreadings:
- reducing the law to simple theft with no consequences
- treating all delegation as exploitation
- assuming the visible owner never needs real competence
- confusing remembered authorship with moral legitimacy

Counterarguments or Limits:
The law overreaches when it implies that visible ownership can float forever without underlying competence or contributor management. Credit captured too aggressively can create sabotage, defection, exposure, or dependence on talent you do not control. The chapter needs the line between strategic coordination and corrosive appropriation.
