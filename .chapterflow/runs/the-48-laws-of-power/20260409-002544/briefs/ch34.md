Core Claim:
The thirty-fourth law argues that treatment often follows the level of worth, dignity, and expectation you project. Greene's point is not that costume alone creates status or that vanity can replace substance forever. It is that self-presentation sets a floor for how others read, test, and value you. The chapter is strongest when it stays on bearing, self-pricing, and dignified expectation rather than on cartoon aristocratic swagger.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
It shifts from diagnosing hidden motives in others into shaping the reading others form of you. Chapter 33 showed how people search for seams; Chapter 34 asks how royal bearing can reduce cheap treatment by teaching others where your floor sits.

Author Logic Chain:
First the author argues that people often take their cues for value from how you present your own worth.
Then the author shows that dignity, composure, and elevated self-pricing can influence treatment before overt struggle begins.
Then the author concludes that acting below your own worth invites cheap pricing, while grounded royal bearing can set a higher standard of response.

Common Misreadings:
- treating the chapter as praise for empty arrogance
- flattening it into generic confidence advice
- assuming presentation alone can permanently replace conduct or value
- confusing grounded dignity with theatrical superiority

Counterarguments or Limits:
Some settings punish overt status display or require quieter dignity than visible grandeur. The chapter overreaches if it becomes advice to demand esteem unsupported by conduct, capability, or context. The useful limit is to present yourself above cheap treatment while staying grounded enough that the bearing does not collapse into ridicule.
