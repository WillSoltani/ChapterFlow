Core Claim:
The forty-sixth law argues that appearing too perfect can make other people resentful, defensive, or eager to cut you down. Greene's useful point is not that excellence is bad or that one should become incompetent. It is that flawless display can provoke envy and resistance, while small visible imperfections or restraint can keep cooperation intact.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
It shifts from pacing change to managing the social cost of superiority. Chapter 45 asked how reform can move without provoking backlash through excess speed. Chapter 46 asks how success, polish, or visible completeness can trigger envy if displayed without modulation.

Author Logic Chain:
First the author argues that conspicuous perfection can attract envy and quiet hostility.
Then the author shows that visible imperfection, modesty, or restraint can reduce that hostility.
Then the author concludes that strategic self-presentation sometimes protects achievement better than pure display does.

- the chapter's distinction between protecting cooperation and faking incompetence

Specific Applications:
- reducing resentment when success, competence, or polish is already obvious
- deciding how much visible imperfection can keep collaboration stable
- separating strategic softening from fake incompetence
- protecting authority without inviting needless envy

Common Misreadings:
- treating the chapter as anti-excellence advice
- flattening it into fake-humility performance
- assuming visible imperfection always helps
- confusing social smoothing with lying about competence

Counterarguments or Limits:
Sometimes visible imperfection weakens trust, authority, or execution. In some settings people need clear confidence and clear competence. The chapter overreaches if it becomes self-sabotage, manipulative false modesty, or deliberate underperformance. The useful limit is to reduce envy without damaging the real function you must perform.
