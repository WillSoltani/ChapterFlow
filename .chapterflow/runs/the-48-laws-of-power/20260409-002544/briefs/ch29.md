Core Claim:
The twenty-ninth law argues that power strengthens when actions are planned through their downstream consequences instead of treated as isolated openings. Greene's point is not that every variable can be controlled or that improvisation is worthless. It is that many failures come from short-range thinking: winning the beginning, missing the chain of effects, and stumbling later into preventable traps. The chapter is strongest when it stays on end-state thinking, consequence mapping, and sequence control rather than on generic productivity planning talk.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
It shifts from bold execution into full-route foresight. Chapter 28 showed how decisive entry creates momentum; Chapter 29 asks how that momentum survives once the rest of the sequence, reaction chain, and end-state have to be managed.

Author Logic Chain:
First the author argues that many people focus too narrowly on the opening move.
Then the author shows that downstream consequences, reactions, and end states must be anticipated in advance.
Then the author concludes that planning through the whole chain prevents short-term wins from becoming long-term losses.

Common Misreadings:
- treating the chapter as praise for impossible total control
- flattening it into generic project-management advice
- assuming planning can replace adaptation once reality shifts
- confusing long-range thinking with paralysis

Counterarguments or Limits:
Some situations require iteration, adaptation, and rapid adjustment more than a perfectly fixed blueprint. The chapter overreaches if it becomes advice to pretend uncertainty can be eliminated. The useful limit is to think through the likely chain far enough to avoid obvious self-created reversals, while staying responsive when the field changes.
