Core Claim:
The twenty-second law argues that resisting force directly can sometimes waste strength, while tactical surrender can absorb pressure, reduce damage, and create a later opening. Greene's point is not that passivity is always wise. It is that apparent yielding can preserve energy, expose an aggressor's overreach, and reverse the situation when direct confrontation would fail. The chapter is strongest when it stays on timing, redirection, and survival-through-yielding rather than on defeatism.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
It shifts from being underestimated to using visible weakness itself as material. Chapter 21 preserved room by pacing recognition; Chapter 22 asks what happens when you accept pressure temporarily so that force spends itself on worse terms.

Author Logic Chain:
First the author argues that direct resistance can strengthen the stronger side when the balance is bad.
Then the author shows that surrender or yielding can absorb force, buy time, and encourage overconfidence in the attacker.
Then the author concludes that apparent weakness can become strategic advantage if it preserves initiative for the next move.

Common Misreadings:
- treating the chapter as advice for cowardice
- flattening it into generic passivity or avoidance
- assuming surrender always produces advantage
- ignoring the need for later agency, timing, and recovery

Counterarguments or Limits:
Some situations punish surrender more than resistance, especially when yielding destroys future options or legitimizes abuse without recourse. The chapter overreaches if it becomes advice to submit whenever pressure rises. The useful limit is to yield only when doing so preserves more agency than direct struggle would.
