Core Claim:
The forty-first law argues that succession becomes dangerous when a person inherits a great predecessor's role, aura, or expectations without creating a distinct identity. Greene's point is not that tradition is worthless or that every successor must rebel loudly. It is that comparison can trap the heir inside someone else's scale, making imitation look safe while actually shrinking force, room, and authority. The chapter is strongest when it stays on comparison pressure, inherited shadow, and the need to define an independent line rather than generic advice about standing out.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
It shifts from refusing dependency bargains into refusing identity dependence. Chapter 40 warned that cheap help can quietly own your freedom; Chapter 41 warns that borrowed stature can quietly own your image, scale, and measure of success.

Author Logic Chain:
First the author argues that inheriting a great predecessor's place traps the successor inside comparison.
Then the author shows that imitation, continuity theater, or borrowed prestige often makes the successor seem smaller instead of stronger.
Then the author concludes that creating a distinct scale, rhythm, or identity is often necessary to escape the predecessor's shadow.

Common Misreadings:
- treating the chapter as a demand for rebellion against every predecessor
- flattening it into generic self-branding advice
- assuming continuity is always weakness
- confusing distinct identity with loud contrarian theater

Counterarguments or Limits:
Some inheritance is valuable, some continuity preserves trust, and some predecessors leave systems that should not be broken just to appear original. The chapter overreaches if it becomes vanity about novelty or contempt for useful foundations. The useful limit is to escape shrinking comparison while still carrying forward what remains structurally sound.
