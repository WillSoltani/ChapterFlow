Core Claim:
The sixth law argues that obscurity weakens power because influence depends on remaining visible in a crowded field. Greene supports that claim by treating attention as a scarce social resource: those who become magnets for the eye and the imagination gain leverage over those who fade into the background. The chapter is strongest when it stays on supported mechanisms rather than theatrical excess for its own sake. Distinctiveness makes you harder to ignore, mystery keeps attention active because people keep interpreting what they do not fully understand, and variation prevents familiarity from cooling into boredom. The law's limit is equally important. Attention can become dangerous if it embarrasses a superior, fixes the wrong reputation, or turns visibility into noisy self-damage.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
It is the book's clearest statement that being noticed is itself a strategic condition. The chapter is not generic advice about charisma. It is about how visibility, mystery, and controlled spectacle convert public attention into leverage.

Author Logic Chain:
First the author argues that disappearing into the crowd makes power difficult because obscurity removes social leverage.
Then the author shows that startling distinctiveness and cultivated mystery pull attention toward you and keep it there.
Then the author concludes that attention must be maintained through variation and controlled boundaries so visibility does not sour into embarrassment or the wrong image.

Specific Applications:
- avoiding being ignored in crowded work or school environments where good work alone stays invisible
- choosing one distinctive surface that makes people look up without locking yourself into a damaging caricature
- varying a visible role or act so the audience does not go numb to it
- knowing when attention helps and when it risks outshining the wrong person

Common Misreadings:
- reducing the law to cheap controversy or constant noise
- assuming attention is valuable even when it fixes the wrong image
- confusing mystery with random vagueness
- treating visibility as automatically wiser than restraint in every hierarchy

Counterarguments or Limits:
The law overreaches when it treats attention as an unconditional good. Some visibility attracts punishment, trivialization, or the wrong audience. Mystery also fails when it becomes incoherence rather than intrigue. The chapter needs the line between strategic distinctiveness and self-defeating spectacle.
