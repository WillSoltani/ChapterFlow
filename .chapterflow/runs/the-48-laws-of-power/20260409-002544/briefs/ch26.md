Core Claim:
The twenty-sixth law argues that power often depends on protecting your visible reputation from blame, mess, and harsh execution even when outcomes still require unpleasant action. Greene's point is not that responsibility disappears or that scapegoating is automatically wise. It is that public standing can survive longer when visible dirt and fallout do not land directly on your own hands. The chapter is strongest when it stays on blame management, reputation shielding, and controlled distance rather than on generic hypocrisy advice.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
It shifts from shaping the self into protecting that shaped self from contamination by visible labor, punishment, or failure. Chapter 25 showed how a person can redesign identity; Chapter 26 asks how that identity remains clean while others carry the more damaging surface burdens.

Author Logic Chain:
First the author argues that visible blame and dirt can damage authority even when action was necessary.
Then the author shows that distance, intermediaries, or delegated execution can protect reputation from direct contamination.
Then the author concludes that clean public hands can preserve status if the burden transfer remains controlled rather than reckless.

Required Anchors:
- the grouped summary claim that visible cleanliness can be a form of power
- the supported mechanism that blame and harsh execution damage public standing when attached directly to the actor
- the chapter's distinction between strategic distance and reckless scapegoating
- the supported claim that intermediaries or buffers can preserve reputation and authority
- the limit that the tactic fails if delegation destroys control, creates obvious injustice, or lets the agent become stronger than the principal

Specific Applications:

- distinguishing clean-handed reputation management from reckless scapegoating
- deciding when visible distance preserves standing and when it creates moral or practical blowback

Common Misreadings:
- treating the chapter as praise for cowardly blame-dumping in every context
- flattening it into generic "never take responsibility" advice
- assuming clean public hands remove all actual accountability
- confusing controlled distance with total detachment from consequences

Counterarguments or Limits:
Some settings require visible ownership, shared sacrifice, or direct accountability more than distance. The chapter overreaches if it becomes advice to abandon responsibility entirely or to burn intermediaries casually. The useful limit is to protect reputation from unnecessary visible dirt while preserving control, fairness, and enough responsibility that the structure does not collapse into obvious abuse.
