Core Claim:
The fourteenth law argues that information is easier to gather under a friendly surface than through visible opposition. Greene's claim is not that all friendship is fake. It is that people reveal motives, weaknesses, plans, and habits more freely when they feel unthreatened. The chapter is strongest when it stays on information advantage rather than on melodramatic betrayal. Its limit matters too. Obvious snooping, overperformed friendliness, or constant hidden extraction can expose the tactic and destroy trust.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
It is the book's clearest argument that access often matters more than confrontation when knowledge is the goal. The chapter is not general advice to be charming. It is about gathering information under lowered vigilance.

Author Logic Chain:
First the author argues that people guard themselves more carefully against open enemies than against friendly surfaces.
Then the author shows that trust, ordinary conversation, and proximity can reveal more than direct pressure can.
Then the author concludes that the move works only while the friendly surface remains plausible and not visibly extractive.

Common Misreadings:
- treating the law as proof that all friendship is fake
- confusing observation with constant deception in every relationship
- assuming information gathering always requires lying
- reducing the chapter to generic social engineering slogans

Counterarguments or Limits:
The law overreaches when it treats every relationship as a cover story. Real trust exists and can be damaged by excessive suspicion or extraction. Still, the chapter insists that people reveal more under ease than under attack. The output needs the line between strategic observation and self-defeating overperformance.
