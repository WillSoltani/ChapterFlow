Core Claim:
The twenty-fourth law argues that power in status-heavy environments often depends on tact, timing, and controlled self-presentation rather than blunt sincerity alone. Greene's point is not that authenticity has no value. It is that hierarchical settings reward indirection, social intelligence, and the ability to move gracefully without triggering avoidable envy or offense. The chapter is strongest when it stays on status navigation, impression-management, and strategic tact rather than generic manners advice.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
It shifts from where force should gather into how a person survives and advances inside a social court. Chapter 23 concentrated power; Chapter 24 asks how power must often be presented and carried in status theaters where direct force alone is costly.

Author Logic Chain:
First the author argues that hierarchical settings punish clumsy self-display and blunt offense.
Then the author shows that tact, indirection, and graceful presentation can reduce friction and preserve access.
Then the author concludes that courtly skill is a form of power because it helps a person move through status systems without needless backlash.

Counterarguments or Limits:
Some settings reward directness more than indirection, and some hierarchies are so corrosive that graceful adaptation only deepens compromise. The chapter overreaches if it becomes advice to perform endlessly at the cost of principle or self-respect. The useful limit is to use tact where it preserves access and reduces avoidable friction without dissolving the core self.
