Core Claim:
The twentieth law argues that commitment can make you easier to use when you tie yourself too quickly to one side, faction, or agenda. Greene's point is not that loyalty is always foolish. It is that premature alignment narrows your options, invites manipulation, and lets other people spend your leverage for their purposes. The chapter is strongest when it stays on independence, optionality, and the danger of being captured by someone else's conflict.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
It shifts from reading different people accurately to refusing to let those differently built people lock you in. Chapter 19 asked who you are dealing with; Chapter 20 asks why you should be careful about joining their side on their terms.

Author Logic Chain:
First the author argues that visible commitment makes you easier to predict and use.
Then the author shows that remaining uncommitted preserves leverage because multiple sides still want access to you.
Then the author concludes that selective distance can keep options open while avoiding being consumed by other people's agendas.

Frameworks or Terms Introduced:

Specific Applications:
- noticing when a side wants your commitment more than your good
- delaying allegiance until terms and incentives are clearer
- preserving bargaining power by not being too easy to count
- distinguishing strategic independence from passive indecision

Common Misreadings:
- treating the chapter as a command for cynicism in every relationship
- confusing noncommitment with cowardly drift
- assuming no commitments are ever worth making
- flattening the law into generic aloofness

Counterarguments or Limits:
Some commitments are necessary and honorable. The chapter overreaches if it becomes advice against all loyalty or all cooperation. The useful limit is to preserve independence until commitment is actually worth its cost.
