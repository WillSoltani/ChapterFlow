Core Claim:
The twelfth law argues that selective honesty or generosity can lower suspicion more effectively than direct pressure. Greene's claim is not that total sincerity wins. It is that limited openness or a well-timed gift can relax defenses, create trust, and make later influence easier. The chapter is strongest when it stays on disarming resistance rather than on moral virtue. Its limit matters too. Overdisclosure, indiscriminate generosity, or transparent bait can expose the strategy and weaken the position that the tactic was supposed to protect.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
It is the book's clearest argument that strategic openness can work as camouflage. The chapter is not simple advice to be nice. It is about using measured candor or giving to reduce vigilance.

Author Logic Chain:
First the author argues that obvious aggression keeps defenses awake while selective openness lowers them.
Then the author shows that a gift, concession, or limited truth can create trust disproportionate to its cost.
Then the author concludes that the move works only while it remains selective; constant openness or obvious bait destroys the effect.

Common Misreadings:
- treating the law as endorsement of total honesty
- confusing generosity with constant self-sacrifice
- assuming any gift automatically creates trust
- reducing the chapter to generic kindness advice

Counterarguments or Limits:
The law overreaches when it treats trust as easy to stage indefinitely. People can read manipulation, and repeated strategic gifts or confessions can become suspicious, exhausting, or self-exposing. The chapter needs the line between measured disarmament and self-defeating overexposure.
