Core Claim:
The eighth law argues that power increases when other people have to move toward your chosen ground instead of forcing you to chase them on theirs. Greene supports that claim by treating movement itself as a strategic concession: the person who advances first often gives up tempo, predictability, and positional strength. The chapter is strongest when it stays on that mechanism of initiative control. Bait matters because apparent opportunity, attraction, or urgency can pull others into motion without open force. The law's limit is just as important. Crude bait can expose the tactic, create counter-manipulation, or leave the operator reacting anyway if the approach is misread or overcommitted.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
It is the book's clearest argument that forcing movement onto others can be more important than winning the immediate exchange. The chapter is not generic persuasion advice. It is about positional control through invitation, lure, and tempo.

Author Logic Chain:
First the author argues that chasing others usually means accepting their timing and terrain.
Then the author shows that bait or apparent opportunity can make others move into a field you prefer.
Then the author concludes that initiative must be preserved carefully because obvious bait or overengineering can collapse into a reactive trap.

Common Misreadings:
- reducing the law to childish manipulation
- assuming never moving first is always strongest
- confusing bait with lying in every case
- treating passivity as strategic control

Counterarguments or Limits:
The law overreaches when it treats all forward movement as weakness. Some situations require direct initiative. Bait also fails when it is transparent, too costly, or when the other side has better patience. The chapter needs the line between controlled invitation and self-defeating game-playing.
