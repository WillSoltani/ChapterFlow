Scott Young turns to directness because learning can stay busy for a long time without getting close enough to the real target to transfer well. A learner may study, rehearse, and prepare, yet still discover that the actual performance context feels foreign. The chapter's answer is simple and demanding: practice should move closer to the real situation the skill is meant to support.

Young defines directness as practicing in the real context of the target skill. That matters because transfer is not automatic. Work done in safer, cleaner, or more controlled settings may help a little, but it can also leave out the pressure, messiness, and demands of the real arena. When that gap stays too large, preparation starts feeling stronger than it really is.

This is why the chapter is suspicious of comfortable proxies. Indirect practice often feels productive because it is easier to control and easier to repeat. A learner can stay in exercises, tutorials, or simulations that never force the same performance the real target requires. Young does not deny that scaffolds can help. He denies that they should become a permanent shelter from the thing the learner actually wants to do.

The principle works by pushing practice closer to the target context. If the goal is public speaking, the practice should begin to look more like speaking in a real room. If the goal is professional performance, the work should start resembling the actual decisions, conditions, and outputs the job demands. Directness makes practice more revealing because the task starts answering back in the same language the real situation will use.

That sharper feedback is also what makes the chapter uncomfortable. The real arena is often messier than preparation. It exposes weakness faster and gives the learner less room to confuse background familiarity with usable skill. Young treats that discomfort as informative. It shows where transfer is still thin and where practice is still too protected.

But the chapter also needs a limit. Directness does not mean throwing away every scaffold at once or pretending the most realistic form is always available immediately. A learner can increase directness in steps. What matters is that the steps actually point toward the target context instead of circling around it forever. Scaffolding is useful when it leads forward. It becomes a problem when it becomes home.

That makes the principle more precise than a slogan about realism. Directness is about spending attention in the arena that will matter later, or at least moving steadily toward it. Chapter 5 protected attention. Chapter 6 asks whether that attention is being spent in the right place. Once the practice is closer to the real target, the next question is which weak point inside that target deserves to be isolated and drilled.
