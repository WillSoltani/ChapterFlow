Chapter Promise:
By the end, the reader should understand why auxiliaries are worse than mercenaries, why mixed arms are only a partial improvement, and why Machiavelli treats one's own forces as the only base of real victory and durable reputation.

Opening Move:
Open on the sharp comparative claim that Chapter 13 does not relax the military warning from Chapter 12 but intensifies it. The foreign force may be brave and disciplined, yet that is exactly what makes it dangerous to the prince who borrows it.

- anchor 1 used in paragraph(s): P1 for defining auxiliaries and stating the captive-or-undone paradox
- anchor 2 used in paragraph(s): P2 for Pope Julius, Pisa, and the Greeks as proof cases
- anchor 3 used in paragraph(s): P3 for the comparison between mercenaries and auxiliaries and the claim about real victory
- anchor 4 used in paragraph(s): P4 for Cesare Borgia's sequence and rising reputation
- anchor 5 used in paragraph(s): P5 for Hiero, David, and the borrowed-arms metaphor
- anchor 6 used in paragraph(s): P6 for France, mixed forces, hidden poison, the Goths, and the final own-force rule

Paragraph Job Map:
- P1: define auxiliaries and state why defeat and victory both threaten the borrowing prince
- P2: prove the point through Julius, Florence, and Constantinople
- P3: compare auxiliaries with mercenaries and insist on preferring loss with one's own arms to victory with others
- P4: explain Borgia's sequence from French auxiliaries to Orsini and Vitelli to his own soldiers
- P5: reinforce the principle through Hiero and David, ending in the borrowed-arms image
- P6: explain mixed forces through France, then close with the Roman Empire and the final definition of one's own forces
- P7 if needed: not needed; the close should turn toward the prince's obligation to practice war directly in Chapter 14

Required Distinction or Mechanism:
The key distinction is that mercenaries are hired by you, but auxiliaries already belong to another command. That is why their very coherence becomes your danger.

Hard-Depth Tension:
Hard depth must keep three tiers distinct: mercenaries are bad, auxiliaries are worse, and mixed forces are better than foreign dependence alone but still below one's own arms.

Threshold Question:
If foreign soldiers are brave and win for you, what in that success actually makes you less secure than before?

Hard Takeaway Topics:
- Takeaway 1: auxiliaries are another prince's forces and therefore obey another center
- Takeaway 2: they are worse than mercenaries because their ruin is ready made
- Takeaway 3: a wise prince prefers losing with his own arms to winning with others
- Takeaway 4: Borgia's reputation rises as he becomes master of his own forces
- Takeaway 5: borrowed arms do not fit and either fall away, weigh down, or bind
- Takeaway 6: mixed forces are an improvement over pure foreign dependence but still inferior to one's own
- Takeaway 7: power not founded on one's own strength is unstable and easily drained outward
