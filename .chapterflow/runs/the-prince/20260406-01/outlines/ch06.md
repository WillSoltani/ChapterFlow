Chapter Promise:
By the end, the reader should understand why self-made principalities are hardest to acquire, why they can become easier to keep once their foundations are truly the founder's, and why opportunity, persuasion, and force must be distinguished rather than blended together.

Opening Move:
Open on the paradox that the ruler who has to climb the hardest may end up standing more securely than the one who was lifted quickly, because self-made founding hurts at the start but can create real roots.

Required Distinction or Mechanism:
The load-bearing distinction is not merely ability versus fortune. It is opportunity versus durable founding power. Fortune can provide the opening, but only self-owned force, reorganized loyalties, and the ability to hold a new order against resistance turn an opening into a stable principality.

Hard-Depth Tension:
The chapter admires founders and yet insists that founding is the most dangerous political labor in the book so far. Hard depth must preserve both sides: imitation is useful, opportunity matters, and admiration is real, but none of that removes the structural hostility faced by the innovator.

Threshold Question:
When a founder has spotted the opening but has not yet broken the old order's organized resistance, what must be secured first so that the new order can survive disbelief: force, presence, or reorganized loyalties?

Hard Takeaway Topics:
- Takeaway 1: high aim and imitation matter because founders need a standard above ordinary practice
- Takeaway 2: fortune gives opportunity, but ability gives it political form
- Takeaway 3: founders acquire with difficulty because they must introduce new rules and methods
- Takeaway 4: old beneficiaries resist actively while future beneficiaries defend only weakly

- Takeaway 6: Hiero shows that replacing old soldiery and alliances with one's own creates durable holding
