If fortune governs human affairs, why bother giving advice at all? Chapter 25 opens by taking that problem seriously. Machiavelli admits that recent upheavals make fatalism tempting. Still, he refuses to surrender to it. Fortune may govern about one-half of our actions, but not all. The chapter's force comes from that narrow middle ground: contingency is real, yet passivity is still blameworthy.

The river metaphor makes that point concrete. Fortune is like a flood that sweeps away everything before it. But fair weather is exactly when men should raise defenses and barriers so the next flood does less damage. That is why Machiavelli says fortune shows her power where valour has not prepared resistance. The turn to Italy sharpens the accusation. Italy is described as an open country without barriers and without defense. Fortune is not being denied. It is being met too late.

The chapter then shifts from public catastrophe to the ruler's character. A prince may be happy today and ruined tomorrow without changing his disposition at all. Why? Because the times changed while he did not. The prince who relies entirely on fortune is lost when fortune changes, but the deeper rule is that success belongs to the man whose actions accord with the spirit of the times. Machiavelli insists there is no single winning method. Men arrive at glory and riches by opposite paths: caution, haste, force, skill, patience, or their contraries. What matters is fit.

That is also why adaptation is so rare. Men are pulled by nature toward one way of acting, and previous success hardens that tendency further. The cautious man who prospered through patience does not know how to become adventurous when circumstances demand it. So Chapter 25 is not only about external fortune. It is also about internal rigidity. Fixed character becomes dangerous in a changing world.

Julius II gives the live example. He acted impetuously, and because the times fit that method, he succeeded. Against Bologna, he moved before Spain, the Venetians, and France had fully aligned with him. That very boldness forced others into passivity or cooperation. But Machiavelli immediately adds the limit. If circumstances had later required caution, Julius would have been ruined, because he would not have changed the style to which nature inclined him. So Julius is not proof that boldness always wins. He proves that boldness won when boldness fit the moment.

That is why the chapter's final preference for adventurousness must be read carefully. Machiavelli does prefer it in his own judgment, but only after showing that success depends on agreement between method and times. Even the chapter's last, violent gendered metaphor about fortune should be treated as rhetoric of force and timing, not as literal ethical guidance. The durable lesson is stricter and more useful: build barriers before the flood, and do not let one successful style harden into destiny. From there the book can move into Chapter 26, where opportunity in Italy is no longer discussed abstractly but demanded directly.
