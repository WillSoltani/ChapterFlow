Chapter 11 feels strange on purpose. After measuring ordinary strength by resources, walls, and civic steadfastness, Machiavelli turns to ecclesiastical principalities and says their difficulties lie before possession, not after it. They may be acquired by capacity or good fortune, but they are held without either because they are sustained by the ancient ordinances of religion. That makes them the book's clearest exception.

Machiavelli presses the point with a series of paradoxes. These princes have states and do not defend them. They have subjects and do not rule them. The states are not taken away, and the subjects neither wish nor are able to alienate themselves. Such principalities alone, he says, are secure and happy. He almost stops there, saying they are upheld by powers beyond ordinary human reach. But he does not actually stop, because he still wants to explain how the Church became so great in temporal power.

That explanation begins in the older Italian balance before Charles of France entered Italy. The Pope, the Venetians, Naples, Milan, and Florence all watched one another. To keep the papacy weak, Roman barons and their factions, especially Orsini and Colonnesi, were allowed to remain a standing source of disorder near the Pontiff. Short papal lives made the weakness persistent, because a pope rarely had time to crush one faction before the cycle renewed itself.

Alexander VI changes that condition. Machiavelli says he showed what a pope with money and arms could do, especially through the Duke Valentino and the opportunity created by the French entry. Alexander meant to aggrandize the duke more than the Church, but the Church inherited the result after his death and the duke's fall. That is the chapter's first historical correction to its own exception: secure holding does not cancel worldly accumulation.

Julius II receives a stronger Church and strengthens it further. He inherits Romagna, reduced barons, weakened factions, and greater means of accumulation. Then he expands the Church's position, moves against the Venetians and the French, and, crucially, acts to strengthen the Church and not any private person. He also keeps the Roman factions within bounds and denies them factional cardinals, because those cardinals would recreate disorder through the barons.

Leo X therefore inherits the most powerful pontificate yet. Chapter 11 matters because it holds two truths together: ecclesiastical principalities are sustained in an exceptional way after acquisition, yet their temporal greatness still grows through concrete political history. The next chapter can therefore return to general rules with clearer force, because this religious exception has been marked off rather than quietly mixed into the ordinary logic of power.
