What if the easiest rise is the most dangerous one? Machiavelli begins by stripping glamour from sudden elevation. The man raised by good fortune or by the arms of others flies upward, but he reaches the summit before he has roots of his own. He has title before loyal force, territory before fixed relations, visibility before the habits of command. That is why Machiavelli compares such states to things that grow fast and therefore fall at the first storm. What self-made rulers build before accession, these rulers must build afterward, while already exposed.

He makes the contrast sharp through Francesco Sforza and Cesare Borgia. Sforza rises by his own means, suffers in the gaining, and keeps with little trouble. Borgia rises through his father's ascendancy and loses when that ascendancy declines. Yet Machiavelli does not use Borgia as a flat warning. He uses him because Borgia almost solves the problem. The chapter's real question is whether a ruler lifted by borrowed power can build roots quickly enough after accession to stand on his own.

Borgia's first lesson is that the forces that raise you can later rule you. Alexander VI can only enlarge him by rearranging the Italian powers, inviting French help, and using papal influence to open Romagna. That wins him territory, but not independence. The Orsini forces he uses may refuse him or seize what he has taken. France may stop him when its interests change. So Borgia makes the chapter's decisive turn. He resolves to depend no more on the arms and the luck of others. Everything that follows is an attempt to make that sentence true.

He weakens the Orsini and Colonna factions by drawing their adherents into his own service with pay, office, and honor. He survives the dangers that rise around the Magione meeting, then turns reconciliation itself into a trap at Sinigalia. Machiavelli lingers on this sequence because it shows what cutting dependence actually requires. The ruler elevated by others cannot stay safe while the real levers of force and faction still answer elsewhere. He has to detach supporters, isolate rival leaders, and turn necessary partners into contained dangers.

The Romagna adds a second problem: order among the ruled. Borgia finds disorder, installs Ramiro d'Orco with full power, and gets peace restored quickly through harsh means. Then he changes the form of rule. He creates a court so justice no longer appears as one minister's naked severity, and then he stages Ramiro's public execution at Cesena. Machiavelli does not soften this. He treats it as a political sequence. The duke wants the benefits of stern restoration without letting the minister's full odium remain attached to him. The people are left, in Machiavelli's phrase, satisfied and dismayed. The example is disturbing because it shows order, institution, and public theater working together.

After that Borgia thinks ahead to the blow he knows is coming. Alexander will die, and the next pope may try to strip everything away. So he works on four fronts: destroy rival families, win Roman gentlemen, shape the college, and gather enough strength to withstand the first shock. Machiavelli's judgment is that he nearly succeeds. He has already built a power that might soon have stood by itself. Then fortune interrupts the sequence at the worst moment. Alexander dies, Borgia is deathly ill, hostile armies remain nearby, and one fatal choice follows. He lets Julius II rise, wrongly believing new benefits can erase old injuries. They cannot. That is why the chapter remains so hard. Borgia shows how much a fortune-made ruler can still build afterward, yet he also shows that borrowed ascent leaves a man racing against contingencies that the self-made founder has already paid through on the way up.
