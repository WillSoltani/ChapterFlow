Alexander dies before Asia has settled, and the expected result never arrives. A conqueror has moved fast, the empire is new in his hands, and the man who bound it together is gone. If acquisitions are usually unstable, why does this one not break apart at once? Machiavelli answers by looking past the spectacle of conquest and into the conquered state's internal design. The real puzzle is not why Alexander won. It is why his successors inherited so little resistance after his death.

He begins by separating principalities into two kinds. Some are ruled by a prince through servants who govern only by his favor and permission. Others are ruled by a prince alongside barons whose rank comes from antiquity of blood, whose lands are their own, and whose subjects are attached to them. That is not a minor detail about titles. It decides where political credit lives. In the first kind, no one inside the country stands above the ruler in an independent way. In the second, power is already spread through people who can carry loyalty with them. Before asking whether a kingdom can be taken, Machiavelli asks who inside it can help an invader enter and who can organize resistance after victory.

The Turk is his model of the first kind. The realm is unified under one lord, and the officials beneath him do not stand on inherited local authority. Even if one were corrupted, he could not bring a whole people with him. That makes the kingdom hard to seize. An outsider cannot count on some powerful insider to open the door. He must meet a united front and rely on his own strength in the field. But the same structure reverses the problem after victory. Once the prince is overthrown, his armies broken, and his family removed, there is no second rank of beloved lords ready to reclaim the country. What made entry difficult now makes possession easier, because the one line around which obedience gathered has been cut away.

France shows the contrary arrangement. The ruler stands among ancient lords whose own subjects know them and are attached to them. That makes entry easier for an outsider, not because the kingdom is simply weak, but because a dissatisfied baron can help crack it open. Somebody inside the regime can invite change. Yet the advantage does not last. The helpers expect reward, the defeated do not disappear, and killing the old princely family is still not enough. The remaining lords become heads of fresh movements against the new ruler. The same plurality that makes conquest possible preserves the materials for later revolt. Easy entrance is already a warning that keeping the state will be harder than taking it.

From there the opening puzzle answers itself. Darius's empire resembled the first kind, not the second. Alexander did not inherit a country stitched together by hereditary rivals who could survive the fall of the crown and reassemble resistance under new banners. He defeated Darius in the field, took the country, and once Darius was killed the empire did not generate a broad rebellion on its own. Machiavelli adds one condition that keeps the claim exact: if Alexander's successors had remained united, they could have held Asia securely and at ease. Their real difficulties came from their own ambitions, not from an internal nobility reviving the old order. That corrects hero worship without denying Alexander's force. Asia stayed quiet partly because of the kind of empire it was.

The Roman example turns the point into a general rule. Rome faced repeated rebellions in Spain, France, and Greece because those lands contained many principalities whose memory endured. As long as that memory remained alive, Roman possession stayed insecure. Only with time and the long continuance of empire did the memory pass far enough for rule to settle. So the closing correction lands hard: the stability of a conquest does not come simply from the conqueror's abundance of ability or collapse from his lack of it. Much depends on whether the subject state was uniform under one ruling line or divided among multiple living centers of authority. The next chapter sharpens the problem again by asking what happens when the dangerous memory is not many lords, but liberty itself.
