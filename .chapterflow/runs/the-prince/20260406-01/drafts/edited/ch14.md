Chapter 14 answers the question left open by the military chapters. If borrowed force is unsafe, what must the ruler himself do? Machiavelli's answer is severe: war, its rules, and its discipline are the proper study of one who rules. Princes who think more of ease than arms lose their states. Francesco Sforza rose by being martial; his sons fell by avoiding the hardships of arms. The point is not love of battle for its own sake. It is that rule rests on a competence the prince cannot neglect.

That competence affects authority before any campaign begins. Machiavelli says there is nothing proportionate between the armed and the unarmed, so an unarmed prince among armed servants breeds disdain above and suspicion below. Soldiers do not fully respect a commander who does not understand their craft. That is why war must never leave the prince's mind, and why peace matters so much. In peace he should exercise himself more than in war, because quiet is when judgment can be built without the noise of immediate danger.

The chapter then divides preparation into action and study. Action means keeping men organized and drilled, hardening the body, and learning the shape of the ground. Hunting matters because it teaches mountains, valleys, plains, rivers, and marshes, which in turn teach defence, surprise, quarters, battle order, and siege. Machiavelli wants the ruler to know actual terrain, not to govern from abstraction. Knowledge of one country even helps with others, because practiced attention learns to see resemblance across places.

Philopoemen gives the model of peace-time rehearsal. While traveling with friends, he would stop and ask what would happen if the enemy were on that hill and his army were here. How would one advance? How would one retreat? How should pursuit work? The point is that ordinary movement becomes stored judgment. Surprise weakens when a leader has already learned to reason through possibilities before they become real.

Study completes what action begins. The prince reads histories, studies victories and defeats, and imitates exemplary captains. Alexander looks to Achilles, Caesar to Alexander, Scipio to Cyrus through Xenophon. History is not ornament here. It is preparation. The wise prince never stands idle in peace, but increases his resources so fortune finds him ready to resist her. Chapter 14 therefore turns military self-reliance into a discipline of body, place, imagination, and memory. That prepared seriousness is what Chapter 15 will test against the harder realities of conduct and blame.
