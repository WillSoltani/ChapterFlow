Why does the ruler who climbs the hardest sometimes stand the firmest once he arrives? Machiavelli answers by starting higher than the ordinary case. If the subject is an entirely new principality, the founder has to look at the greatest founders and think like the archer who aims above the mark in order to hit it. The lesson is not that imitation makes one equal to Moses, Cyrus, Romulus, or Theseus. It is that founding cannot begin from a small standard. A new prince is not inheriting a settled arrangement. He is trying to make a new one hold. That is why Machiavelli narrows quickly from ability and fortune together to a sterner line: the man who has relied least on fortune stands strongest. If he has no other state and must live in the new one himself, his footing is stronger still.

The famous names do real work in the argument. Fortune gives them opportunity, but not the principality itself. Moses finds an oppressed people, Cyrus a dissatisfied one, Romulus a life forced out of its earlier track, Theseus a people scattered and unformed. None of those conditions is yet stable power. They become politically meaningful only because someone sees the opening and shapes it. That is the chapter's first hard distinction. Opportunity without ability lies unused, and ability without opportunity has nothing to seize. Fortune supplies the material. Ability turns material into form.

That is why Machiavelli can say that self-made rulers acquire with difficulty but keep with ease. The pain is in the ascent, because they must introduce new rules and methods instead of stepping into a structure that already favors them. In doing so, they create active enemies out of everyone who prospered under the old conditions and win only lukewarm defenders among those who may prosper under the new. The losers know exactly what is being taken from them. The possible winners have not yet felt their gain. The founder therefore climbs while the old order is organized and the new order is still half-believed.

From here the chapter becomes harsher. Founding does not fail mainly because the idea is weak. It fails because persuasion is weaker than the resistance gathered against it. Machiavelli asks whether the innovator must depend on others or can rely on himself, whether he must use prayers or can use force. His answer is blunt because the problem is blunt. People are easy to persuade and hard to keep persuaded. When belief loosens, the founder needs some means of keeping the new order from dissolving with it. That is what sits behind the line about armed prophets and unarmed ones. The point is not theatrical toughness. It is that no new order survives on applause alone.

Savonarola keeps that lesson close to earth. He had followers, momentum, and a new order to announce, yet once the multitude no longer believed, he had no means of holding the believers steady or compelling the unbelievers. Machiavelli uses him to show that the founder's danger is concentrated in the climb. If he survives that stage, if the main enemies are broken and the new arrangement stops looking temporary, then difficulty in acquiring can turn into ease in keeping. The hard road upward is precisely what can make the rule more secure later.

Hiero gives the chapter its practical miniature. He rises from private station through opportunity, but he holds because he does not leave the old supports standing. He abolishes the old soldiery, organizes the new, abandons old alliances, and makes new ones. That sequence is the real meaning of one's own arms and ability here. It is not a mood of self-belief. It is the work of replacing borrowed supports with loyal structures that belong to the founder. Hiero suffers much in acquiring and little in keeping because, by the end, the foundations are his. That leaves the next question ready: what happens when a man reaches the summit first and only afterwards has to build what still is not his?
