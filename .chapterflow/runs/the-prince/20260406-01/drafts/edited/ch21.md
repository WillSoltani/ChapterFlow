Chapter 21 treats esteem as something built, not inherited from stillness. Machiavelli says nothing makes a prince so much esteemed as great enterprises and setting a fine example. That is why Ferdinand of Aragon leads the chapter. The point is not one bold deed. It is a sequence of visible actions large enough to keep subjects and rivals occupied. Granada becomes the foundation of his dominion, while the barons of Castile stay fixed on war and fail to notice how much power he is gathering over them. One enterprise leads into another, and the minds of the people remain in suspense and admiration rather than settling into steady opposition.

The chapter then brings the same logic inside the state. Bernabo da Milano becomes the example of a ruler who made unusual rewards and punishments memorable enough to be widely talked about. A prince, Machiavelli says, should always work to gain the reputation of being a great and remarkable man. Esteem depends on public shape. The ruler must give people visible reasons to think of him as larger than routine administration.

From there Machiavelli turns to the chapter's hardest practical rule: a prince is respected when he is a true friend or a downright enemy. Neutrality looks cautious, but it usually leaves him unsupported. If he refuses to declare himself, he becomes prey to the conqueror and earns no shelter from the loser. The Achaean example sharpens this. The Romans answer Antiochus by saying that noninterference simply leaves a state as the guerdon of the conqueror. Neutrality is therefore not safety above politics. It is often the position most useful to those who want your passivity for free.

Machiavelli still keeps the lesson from collapsing into reckless alliance. Sometimes the likely victor will remain dangerous to you, and sometimes he will not. In both cases he generally prefers declared support to neutrality, but he warns princes not to ally with someone stronger than themselves in order to attack others unless necessity compels it. The Venetians show the ruinous version of that choice. The Florentines show the compelled version. From there comes the chapter's broader prudence rule: no course is perfectly safe. One avoids one trouble only by entering another, so prudence means choosing the lesser evil.

The end of the chapter widens renown beyond war. The prince should be a patron of ability, reward those who honor the city, and let commerce, agriculture, and every other calling proceed without fear of seizure or punitive taxation. He should also appear among the people through festivals, spectacles, and attention to guilds and societies. But Machiavelli adds the limit that keeps all this from becoming softness. Courtesy and liberality must never erase the majesty of rank.

So Chapter 21 defines renown as visible direction. Great enterprises, memorable internal examples, declared alignment, civic rewards, and ceremonial presence all make the prince legible as a great man. Neutrality, by contrast, usually leaves him shapeless and unsupported. That sets up Chapter 22 well. Once the prince has learned how to gain renown in public, the next question is what his closest appointments reveal about his judgment in private.
