Chapter 22 makes an unusually intimate claim about rule. Before a prince speaks at length, people begin judging him by the men around him. That is why Machiavelli says the first opinion formed of a prince and of his understanding comes from observing his servants. If they are capable and faithful, he appears wise because he recognized the capable and kept them faithful. If they are not, the fault does not stop with them. The prince has already exposed a prime error in choosing them.

Pandolfo Petrucci's use of Antonio da Venafro lets Machiavelli sharpen the point. The example introduces the three classes of intellect: one comprehends by itself, one understands what others have comprehended, and one does neither. The prince does not have to be the first kind in order to rule well. He can still be good in the second way if he can recognize quality in another. That is why Pandolfo still appears clever. Even if he did not originate the best thinking himself, he showed judgment by selecting Venafro.

That second-rank wisdom is active, not passive. The prince who can tell good from bad in a servant, praise the one, and correct the other keeps the servant from hoping to deceive him. A brilliant secretary proves wisdom only when the prince can still evaluate him. Otherwise the secretary stops reflecting the ruler's judgment and starts replacing it. Chapter 22 is therefore not dazzled by talent alone. It cares whether talent remains governable.

The chapter then gives its never-failing test. A servant who thinks more of his own interests than of the prince's, and seeks inwardly his own profit in everything, will never be trustworthy. The problem is not ambition in the abstract. It is misalignment inside an office that carries another person's charge. The man who has the state of another in his hands cannot safely treat it as a ladder for himself.

Machiavelli finally turns back to the prince's own work. To keep a servant honest, the prince must honour him, enrich him, do him kindnesses, and share honours and cares with him. Reward and burden belong together. A reliable servant must feel that service brings value, visibility, and real responsibility. But the prince must also let him see that he cannot stand alone. That is the chapter's hardest line. Trust is built not only through esteem, but through a structure in which separation looks dangerous. When prince and servant are disposed that way, they can trust each other. When they are not, the end becomes disastrous for one or both. From there the book can move naturally into Chapter 23, where the question is no longer how to choose secretaries, but how to keep counsel from collapsing into flattery.
