Mercy gets tested here the same way liberality did in the last chapter: by consequence rather than by appearance. Machiavelli says every prince should want to be thought clement. Then he turns the question. Cesare Borgia's reputed cruelty reconciled Romagna and restored order, while the Florentines, trying not to look cruel, let Pistoia collapse into destruction. The point is not that cruelty sounds better. The point is that soft indulgence can spread injury across a whole people while a few severe examples strike only a few.

That is why Machiavelli says a prince who keeps his subjects united and loyal should not fear the reproach of cruelty too much. Too much mercy can let disorders grow into murders and robberies. The new prince faces this pressure most sharply because new states are full of danger. Still, Chapter 17 does not praise blind harshness. Machiavelli insists on prudence, temperance, and humanity even while he says some severity is unavoidable.

From there the chapter asks whether it is better to be loved or feared. One would like both, but if one must choose, it is safer to be feared. Love rests on obligation, and Machiavelli thinks men break obligations when advantage changes. Fear rests on punishment, and punishment is more dependable than affection. So the ruler who leans only on being loved is relying on something outside his control.

Yet fear is safe only if it stops short of hatred. Machiavelli gives that limit concrete form. If a prince must punish, he should act on proper justification and manifest cause. Above all, he should keep his hands off the property of others and their women. Arbitrary seizure and unexplained violence make rulers hated in a way disciplined punishment need not. So the real contrast is not kindness against terror. It is bounded severity against caprice.

The army examples sharpen the rule. Hannibal held a mixed army together because his cruelty reinforced discipline, while Scipio's excessive forbearance loosened it. The chapter closes on control: men love according to their own will and fear according to that of the prince, so a wise prince should build himself on what he can enforce while still avoiding hatred. Chapter 17 therefore argues that a few hard acts can be more merciful than indulgence, and that feared rule is safer than loved rule only when it remains justified, restrained, and disciplined. Chapter 18 will turn from punishment to faith and promises.
