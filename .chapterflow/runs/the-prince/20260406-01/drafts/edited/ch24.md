Chapter 24 is harsher than its title first suggests. Machiavelli does not begin by pitying princes who lost their states. He begins by saying a new prince can become more secure than a hereditary one if his actions are seen to be able. Because a new ruler is watched more narrowly, present performance matters more sharply. Men are attracted more by the present than by the past. If the present rule is good, they bind themselves to it more tightly than ancient blood might suggest. That is why the chapter opens by redefining legitimacy. Inheritance is not safer by itself.

The contrast that follows is severe. A new prince who establishes a principality and strengthens it with good laws, good arms, good allies, and good example wins double glory. A prince born to rule who loses his state suffers double disgrace. Machiavelli is judging outcomes through effort. The harder success earns more honor. The easier inheritance, if wasted, earns more shame.

The diagnosis of failure is concrete. The Italian rulers who lost their states shared one common defect in regard to arms. Then they had a political defect as well: either the people were hostile, or, if the people were friendly, they had not secured the nobles. That compression matters because it gathers much of the earlier book into one short verdict. Arms, popular support, and elite management belong together. In the absence of those defects, Machiavelli says, a state with enough power to keep an army in the field cannot be lost.

Philip of Macedon serves as the counterexample to excuse-making. He did not command vast territory compared with the Romans and Greeks facing him, yet he was warlike, knew how to attract the people, and secured the nobles. Because of that, he sustained the war for years and, though he lost some cities, kept the kingdom. The point is not simple toughness. It is that pressure does not automatically become ruin when the ruler has kept the right supports in place.

So Machiavelli tells princes not to accuse fortune first, but their own sloth. In quiet times they never imagined change and made no provision in the calm against the tempest. When bad times came, they thought of flight and not of defending themselves. Then they hoped the people, disgusted with their conquerors, would recall them. Machiavelli grants that this may sometimes happen after other remedies fail. What he condemns is treating that hope as the plan after neglecting all other expedients.

The chapter ends on its hardest line. Deliverance that does not depend on yourself is not secure. Only what depends on yourself and your valour is reliable, certain, and durable. That is why Chapter 24 reads less like consolation than like exposure. The fall is presented as the visible result of earlier neglect. From there the book can move into Chapter 25, where fortune itself finally comes under direct examination.
