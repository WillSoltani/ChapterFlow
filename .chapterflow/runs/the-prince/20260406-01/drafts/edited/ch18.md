Faith is the virtue Machiavelli destabilizes next. He begins by admitting that it is praiseworthy for a prince to keep faith and live with integrity. Then he cuts against the praise. Princes who achieve great things, he says, often cannot treat good faith as sufficient. They overcome rulers who rely too simply on their word. So the chapter is not asking whether fidelity sounds noble. It is asking whether fidelity alone can survive the actual contest of rule.

That is why Machiavelli says a prince must know two ways of contesting: by law and by force. Law belongs to man, force to beast, and political life often requires both. The fox and lion make the rule memorable. The lion cannot detect snares. The fox cannot terrify wolves. A ruler who brings only force is easy to trap. A ruler who brings only craft is too weak when conflict turns direct. Chapter 18 therefore defends a pairing, not a single trick.

Only after that does Machiavelli say the thing that gives the chapter its reputation: a wise lord cannot, nor ought he to, keep faith when observance would turn against him and the reasons for the pledge no longer exist. The conditions matter. This is not a blank check for convenience. It is a rule for a world where men are bad and do not keep faith themselves. Fidelity becomes conditional when the political ground underneath it has changed.

But the prince must also know how to disguise this flexibility. Alexander VI stands here as the example of someone who deceived constantly because he understood how ready people are to believe what suits present necessity. So Machiavelli draws the chapter's second severe distinction: it is not necessary for a prince always to have the named virtues, but it is necessary to appear to have them. To seem merciful, faithful, humane, upright, and religious is politically useful even when complete observance would be injurious.

The chapter becomes sharper still when Machiavelli says a prince, especially a new one, is often forced to act contrary to fidelity, friendship, humanity, and religion in order to maintain the state. Yet he still adds a limit people often erase: the ruler should not depart from the good if he can avoid doing so. If compelled, then he must know how. That keeps the chapter from collapsing into casual deceit. It is about political necessity, not about celebrating fraud for its own sake.

The close explains why appearance matters so much. Men judge more by the eye than by the hand. Everyone sees what a prince appears to be; few really know what he is; and in princes one judges by the result. That is why conquering and holding the state covers so much. Chapter 18 therefore joins craft, force, conditional fidelity, and staged virtue into one rule: a prince must know when to keep faith, when to break it, and how not to advertise the difference. Chapter 19 will widen that logic from promises to hatred, contempt, and conspiracy.
