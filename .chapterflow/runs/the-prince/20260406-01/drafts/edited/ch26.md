Chapter 26 is where Machiavelli stops analyzing and starts calling. After a whole book spent explaining how states are acquired, held, and lost, he says the present time is especially fit for a new prince who could introduce a new order in Italy. That shift matters. The chapter is not patriotic ornament added at the end. It is the point where the rest of the book is tested against one live historical opening.

He frames that opening through founders from the past. Israel had to be captive for Moses to appear, the Persians had to be oppressed for Cyrus, and the Athenians had to be dispersed for Theseus. Italy, he says, has now been reduced to an equivalent extremity: more enslaved than the Hebrews, more oppressed than the Persians, more scattered than the Athenians, without head and without order. The misery is not described only to provoke pity. It identifies the scale of the political problem and the size of the chance before a founder.

Machiavelli then says Italy has already shown that it is ready to follow a banner if someone will raise it. A recent spark promised redemption but was rejected by fortune, so the country still waits. He directs that hope toward the Medici house and adds the chapter's hardest moral language: the war is just because it is necessary, and arms are hallowed when there is no other hope. Even that rhetoric does not erase action. God may have prepared the hour, but God does not wish to do everything and take away free will or the share of glory that belongs to human beings.

That is why the chapter immediately returns to institutions. Italy's old order was not good, and true honor belongs to the newly risen man who establishes new laws and ordinances. The emotional appeal only works because it is tied to founding. Chapter 26 is still operating by the same standard as the rest of the book: durable power requires structure, not feeling alone.

The sharpest diagnosis comes in the line that Italy has great valour in the limbs while it fails in the head. Italians are not condemned as lacking courage. They are condemned as lacking unified leadership. Armies fail because of the insufficiency of leaders, because no one stands high enough above the rest to command obedience. So Machiavelli returns to the book's central military rule: before all things, the prince must have his own forces. Italian soldiers can be better than foreign ones if they are commanded, honored, and maintained by their own prince.

The chapter closes by insisting that the opportunity must not be allowed to pass. A liberator would be received with love, revenge, devotion, tears, and obedience. But the final appeal, including the Petrarch lines, is not meant to float above the book's mechanism. The whole argument ends in one demand: if willingness and courage already exist, the founder must supply what Italy lacks most, namely head, order, own arms, and a new political form. Only then does exhortation become liberation.
