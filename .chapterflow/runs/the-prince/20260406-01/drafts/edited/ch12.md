Chapter 12 returns from the map of principalities to the force that keeps any state standing. Machiavelli begins with foundations: good laws and good arms belong together. He leaves laws aside and classifies the arms of a prince as his own, mercenary, auxiliary, or mixed. The argument becomes severe at once. A ruler who rests on hired force is not using a weaker version of the same instrument. He is trusting men whose stake lies elsewhere.

The first problem is the mercenary soldier. Machiavelli says stipend is too weak a bond to make men share real danger. Mercenaries may look serviceable in peace and even bold among friends, but when the attack comes they melt away. That is why a state built on them stands neither firm nor safe, and why he says that in peace one is robbed by them and in war by the enemy. Italy's humiliation proves the point for him. Mercenary troops looked adequate until foreigners arrived. Then Charles of France crossed the peninsula "with chalk in hand," as if the country were already half open.

The next problem is the mercenary captain. Machiavelli refuses to let the prince imagine that better hiring solves the difficulty. If the captain is unskilful, he ruins you. If he is skilful, he threatens you. A capable hired commander has ambitions that can turn against the master or outgrow the master's purposes. So the prince is trapped between weakness below and overmighty service above. Machiavelli's answer is that princes and republics must command their own arms. The prince should go in person. A republic should send citizens and bind commanders by laws. Rome, Sparta, and the Swiss matter here because they show that arms rooted inside a political body are harder to turn against it.

The historical cases drive the point home. Carthage was oppressed by its mercenaries. The Thebans raised Philip and lost their liberty. Francesco Sforza was hired by the Milanese and then turned on them. Florence escaped some similar dangers mostly by chance, not by principle. Venice shows the same lesson at larger scale. It was safest when it fought with its own men. Once it turned to land war and trusted captains, it entered a world in which commanders could not be safely dismissed, could not be fully trusted, and could leave the state fearing loss more than expecting gain. Hence Machiavelli's sharp summary: from such arms conquests come slowly and losses suddenly.

The final movement widens from individual captains to the whole Italian system. Church territories and republics, unused to arms, hired foreigners. Condottieri then reshaped warfare to suit themselves. They lowered the credit of infantry, leaned on cavalry, and stripped war of effort and danger wherever possible. Night attacks disappeared. Winter campaigning disappeared. Camps went unfortified. Machiavelli's point is larger than military technique. When the military order is designed for hired captains to preserve themselves, the state is trained into weakness.

Chapter 12 therefore condemns more than cowardice. It condemns dependence. A prince who rents danger rents his future, because both the soldiers and the captains are aligned elsewhere. That is why the next chapter becomes even harsher. Auxiliaries will prove worse than mercenaries, because the foreign force arrives not only hired but already coherent and already obedient to another command.
