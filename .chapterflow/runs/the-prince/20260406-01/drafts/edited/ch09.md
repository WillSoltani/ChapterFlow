Chapter 9 begins with a quieter route to power than conquest or massacre. A leading citizen becomes prince by the favor of fellow citizens. Machiavelli calls this a civil principality, and he immediately explains why the source of support matters. Every city contains two opposed desires: the people do not want to be ruled and oppressed by the nobles, while the nobles want to rule and oppress the people. So the prince's position is shaped from the start by which side's desire raised him.

That is why a prince raised by nobles stands on harder ground than one raised by the people. Noble backers think of themselves as his equals, so he cannot manage them freely. A prince raised by the people stands more alone and finds fewer around him unwilling to obey. Machiavelli is not praising popularity for its own sake. He is making a security argument. The people want only not to be oppressed. The nobles want more power for themselves. One demand is limited. The other expands.

The contrast becomes sharper in adversity. A hostile people may abandon the prince, and that is dangerous enough. Hostile nobles do more. They intrigue, defect early, and attach themselves to whoever looks likely to win. Because they are few, a prince can replace one set of nobles with another. He cannot replace the people the same way. So popular friendliness is not a decorative asset. It is the condition of safety when pressure arrives.

Machiavelli then sorts the nobles. Some bind themselves entirely to the prince's fortune and should be honored if they are not rapacious. Others do not. Some of these are merely weak or cautious and can still be used, especially when they give good counsel. The dangerous ones are the ambitious nobles who think first of themselves. They should be feared like open enemies, because in adversity they help ruin the prince. The chapter's point is practical: elite allies are not one class of friend. They must be distinguished.

That is why a prince raised by the nobles must seek above all to win the people by taking them under his protection. Machiavelli thinks this conversion can happen quickly, because people who expected harm often bind themselves strongly to a ruler who instead protects them. Nabis helps him answer the proverb that building on the people is like building on mud. That proverb applies to a private citizen who expects rescue without command. It does not apply to a true prince who can command, keeps courage in adversity, and sustains confidence.

The last warning is easy to miss and should not be. Civil principalities become weak when rule rests too heavily on magistrates. In calm times everyone promises loyalty. In troubled times few can be trusted, and citizens accustomed to intermediaries do not suddenly obey the prince directly. So a wise ruler must build things so citizens always, in every sort of circumstance, have need of the state and of him. That is Chapter 9's final measure of security, and it leads directly to Chapter 10's next question: how do you tell whether a principality is actually strong enough to stand when pressure comes?
