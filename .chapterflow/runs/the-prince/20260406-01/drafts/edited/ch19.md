Many faults can be endured in a ruler. Hatred and contempt cannot. That is Chapter 19's narrowing move. Machiavelli is no longer asking which virtues sound admirable. He is ranking dangers. A prince who avoids hatred and contempt has handled the most dangerous part of rule, because those states turn ordinary opposition into something more organized and more daring. Other reproaches may hurt without breaking power. Hatred and contempt change the structure around the prince.

The chapter separates their sources carefully. Hatred comes above all from rapacity and from violating what subjects count as theirs, especially property and women. If those are left untouched, most people remain content and only a few ambitious men have to be managed. Contempt comes from another failure: seeming fickle, frivolous, weak, vain, or irresolute. So the prince must not only avoid plunder. He must also avoid looking easy to deceive, easy to bend, or too small for his station. His judgments should seem firm. His conduct should convey gravity and fortitude. One danger grows from injury. The other grows from visible smallness.

That distinction matters because esteem is one of the strongest defenses against conspiracy. Machiavelli says a prince has two fears, one from without and one from within. External danger is checked by arms and allies. Internal danger is the secret plot. But conspiracies are weak when the people are not hostile, because the conspirator expects success only if the prince's removal will please others. If public feeling runs the other way, accomplices can betray him for advantage, and escape becomes doubtful even after the deed. The Bentivogli case in Bologna gives the claim real weight. After Annibale Bentivogli was murdered, the people rose and killed the conspirators themselves. Goodwill did not prevent the first strike, but it ruined the conspirators' hopes of profiting from it.

France adds another practical lesson. Parliament checks nobles and protects the people without forcing the king to wear every reproach directly. Machiavelli draws a rule from this arrangement: leave affairs of reproach to others and keep grace in your own hands. The point is not cosmetic spin. It is political routing. Necessary punishments and refusals should not all attach directly to the prince's face, while visible benefits should. At the same time, he must not please the nobles in a way that makes himself hated by the people.

The Roman emperors seem at first to break the whole argument, because some good emperors died badly. Machiavelli answers that emperors faced a third pressure besides nobles and people: soldiers. The people loved peace. The soldiers loved a warlike and rapacious ruler who indulged them. In that setting, goodness alone could fail. Marcus succeeds because hereditary authority and personal respect let him keep both sides in place. Pertinax fails because disciplined reform angers corrupt soldiers. Alexander fails because goodness is mixed with contempt; he appears effeminate and ruled by his mother. Here the chapter makes one of its hardest claims: hatred can be acquired by good works as well as bad when the body you depend on is already corrupt.

The harsher emperors confirm the same rule from another direction. Only Severus succeeds, because he combines force, cunning, and renown strongly enough to found and hold power. Caracalla becomes hated and then gravely injures those around him. Commodus loses dignity and becomes both hated and despised. Maximinus becomes intolerable through ferocity and public indignity. So the chapter ends with a selective lesson. A new prince should not try to imitate Marcus wholesale, because Marcus inherited stability. Nor should he copy Severus wholesale, because founding and preserving are not the same task. He should take from Severus what establishes a state and from Marcus what keeps an established state honorable and firm. That prepares the next chapter's question about fortresses and devices. Before asking which instruments help, Machiavelli first shows which public feelings make instruments too weak to save a ruler.
