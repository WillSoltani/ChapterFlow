The first trap in a mixed principality is that the new ruler often enters on a wave of welcome. People change rulers hoping life will improve, and that hope makes the acquisition look easier than it is. Then experience catches up. The new prince brings soldiers, burdens, and disruptions, so the same people who opened the gates may feel they have gone from bad to worse. Chapter 2 showed what happens when continuity already supports the ruler. Here the acquired territory may be attached to his old state, but its memory and expectations do not become his just because he has entered.

That is why mixed acquisition begins in a double bind. The ruler injures those he displaces, yet he cannot fully satisfy the allies who helped him arrive. They expected more than rule can actually deliver, and he still needs their goodwill. Armed force does not cancel this problem. Machiavelli insists that even a strong prince entering a province needs the goodwill of the natives. Louis XII in Milan is the proof. He took the city quickly and lost it quickly because those who had welcomed him discovered that the new rule felt harsher than the promise that invited it.

Machiavelli then adds a detail that keeps the argument precise. A rebellious province taken a second time can become harder to lose, because rebellion exposes delinquents, suspects, and weak places. France lost Milan first through a limited counterstroke and later only through a much wider effort. But that point leads into the chapter's real distinction. Mixed dominions do not all behave alike. Some resemble the old state in language and custom. Others differ in language, customs, or laws. The maintenance rule changes with that split.

If the annexed territory resembles the older principality, the path is narrower than it first sounds. Extinguish the family of the former prince and leave laws and taxes alone. Those steps work together. Remove the line that can rally memory against you, but do not keep reminding everyone that conquest has altered daily life. When the territory has not been accustomed to self-government and its customs are close to those of the old state, minimal disturbance lets it settle into one body with the existing dominion.

Unlike territories are harder because difference multiplies distance. Machiavelli's first remedy is residence. The ruler should live there, see disorders as they begin, and stop officials from turning distance into pillage. If he will not reside, he should plant colonies in key places rather than rely on heavy garrisons. Colonies are cheaper and injure fewer people. Garrisons spread hardship across the whole territory and teach nearly everyone to hate the acquisition. That is why the chapter's harsh maxim appears here: people are either to be treated well or crushed, because lighter injuries leave room for revenge. The sentence is brutal, but it belongs to the chapter's larger warning against half-measures that distribute resentment without removing it.

The problem is not only inside the annexed territory. The ruler also has to manage the surrounding map. In a differing country he should support the lesser neighbors, weaken the greater, and stop any strong foreign power from getting a foothold. Discontented parties will invite one in if they can. Rome is Machiavelli's model because the Romans treated future trouble as the real object of prudence. They planted colonies, held down stronger powers, and acted early. His fever analogy gives the rule its shape: political evils are easiest to cure when they are still hard to see. By the time everyone can see them, the remedy is late. Deferred war is therefore not peace. It is a transfer of advantage.

Louis XII becomes the anti-model that gathers all of this into one case. Machiavelli grants that Louis had an opening in Italy. The Venetians brought him in, Lombardy gave him a foothold, and a ring of weaker powers moved toward him. The failure comes after entry. He strengthened the Church by helping Alexander into the Romagna. He brought Spain into Naples. He did not settle in the country. He did not send colonies. He weakened minor powers that might have depended on him, then helped ruin the Venetians after already changing the balance against himself. Each move made the next one harder to correct because each one strengthened a rival or widened the shelter for malcontents. The closing rule therefore lands with force: the ruler who makes another power stronger is preparing his own decline. Chapter 4 turns that lesson into a sharper question by asking why Alexander's successors did not face the same backlash in Darius's kingdom.
