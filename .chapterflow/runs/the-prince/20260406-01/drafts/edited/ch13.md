Chapter 13 sharpens the military warning of Chapter 12 instead of repeating it. Mercenaries are dangerous because they are hired. Auxiliaries are worse because they already belong to another prince. They arrive united, disciplined, and obedient elsewhere. That is why Machiavelli frames the trap so cleanly: if they lose, you are undone; if they win, you are their captive. The danger is not only failure. It is successful dependence.

Pope Julius gives the recent example. Distrusting mercenaries, he turned to Ferdinand of Spain for armed help against Ferrara. Fortune saved him from the full consequence only because events broke unexpectedly and he ended up escaping both enemy defeat and allied captivity. The Florentines, who sent French forces against Pisa, and the Greeks, who admitted Turks who would not leave, make the point even plainer. A foreign force may solve the immediate problem while shifting control away from the power that summoned it.

That is why Machiavelli says auxiliaries are more hazardous than mercenaries. Mercenaries are slower to injure because they are found and paid by you. Auxiliaries are ruin ready made because they already move as one under another command. So the wise prince prefers his own arms and would rather lose with them than conquer with the others. The claim is severe, but exact: victory under another's force is not real victory because command has already leaked away.

Cesare Borgia shows the logic in sequence. He begins with French auxiliaries, turns to the Orsini and Vitelli as mercenaries, then destroys that dependence and relies on his own men. Machiavelli says the duke was never more highly esteemed than when people saw he was complete master of his own forces. Chapter 13 therefore ties security to reputation. A prince may look active while leaning on others, but he does not look sovereign.

Two compressed images follow. Hiero cut away useless mercenaries and fought with his own. David rejected Saul's armor. From that, Machiavelli derives one of his hardest lines: the arms of others either fall from your back, weigh you down, or bind you fast. The image matters because it shows three different failures at once. Borrowed force may fail, burden, or dominate.

France then supplies the middle case. Charles VII strengthened the kingdom with its own ordinance. Louis weakened that basis by relying on the Swiss. The result was a mixed force, better than pure foreign dependence but still worse than one's own. Machiavelli ends by widening the lesson to the Roman Empire, whose vigour declined when it enlisted the Goths. Power grows unstable when the strength that supports it passes outward.

Chapter 13 finishes the classification that the previous chapter opened. Mercenaries are dangerous because they are rented. Auxiliaries are worse because they are coherent and commanded elsewhere. Mixed arms are an improvement, but still compromised. Only forces composed of subjects, citizens, or dependents make a principality secure. That clears the ground for Chapter 14, where Machiavelli can finally ask what a prince must do to keep such force ready.
