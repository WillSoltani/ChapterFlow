Chapter 20 looks at first like a bag of princely tricks. Arm subjects. Disarm them. Foster factions. Build fortresses. Destroy them. Machiavelli's point is sharper than that. He is testing whether a device actually makes the prince safer in the state he has or only imitates safety. The right measure is not whether the device sounds forceful. It is whether it turns men and institutions into the prince's own strength or instead reveals distrust and breeds hatred.

That is why he says a new prince in a new principality arms subjects rather than disarming them. Armed by him, they become more his. Disarm them and he advertises distrust, invites hatred, and often ends by leaning on mercenaries. But the rule changes once a new state is added to an older one. Then disarming the annexed province may be necessary, because the prince wants the armed force in the state to be his own longstanding men. The chapter never lets the device float free from the political condition.

The same conditional logic destroys the old idea of ruling by factions. Machiavelli rejects the maxim about holding Pistoia by factions and Pisa by fortresses because divided cities fail under outside pressure. What looks manageable in peace proves ruinous in war. Venice becomes the warning case. Once the larger shock came, the divided city did not remain easier to manage. It became easier to lose.

The middle of the chapter explains why some apparent liabilities can become assets. Fortune may raise enemies so a new prince can defeat them and rise higher by the obstacle. Formerly distrusted men may also serve more faithfully than those trusted from the start, because they need to erase the old suspicion by deeds. But Machiavelli adds a hard qualification. Men who supported the prince only because they hated the previous government are often harder to satisfy than former enemies who now need the prince's favor. Grievance is not the same thing as loyalty.

Fortresses gather the whole chapter into one visible test. They are sometimes useful, and Machiavelli does not deny it. Yet they help or hurt according to where the real danger lies. If the prince fears the people more than foreigners, a fortress may help. If he fears foreigners more, it may matter less or even injure him. Francesco Sforza's castle at Milan is the warning that a structure built for security can become a source of trouble. The strongest line follows from that: the best possible fortress is not to be hated by the people.

The Countess of Forli proves both sides of the rule. Her fortress helped once against a popular attack, but failed later when the people were joined with foreigners. So Machiavelli ends by praising both builders and destroyers of fortresses when the choice fits the case, and blaming any prince who trusts walls while neglecting public hatred. That is the chapter's real hierarchy. Devices may help. Popular hostility overrules them. From there the book can move naturally into Chapter 21, where the question becomes how a prince gains esteem rather than merely hiding behind expedients.
