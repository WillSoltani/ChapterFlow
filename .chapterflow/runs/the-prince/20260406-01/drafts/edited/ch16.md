The first applied virtue test goes straight at a quality everyone praises. Machiavelli says it would be good to be reputed liberal. The problem begins when a ruler tries to keep that reputation visible. Liberality that stays private wins no name, while liberality that wins a name becomes expensive enough to govern the ruler instead of the ruler governing it.

That expense drives the chapter. A prince who keeps buying the reputation of liberality consumes his property and then has to burden his people, tax them, and search for money. The result is not stable love. It is odium, poverty, and low esteem. Then, when he tries to pull back, he immediately acquires the reproach of being mean anyway. Chapter 16 is not attacking generosity as such. It is attacking the political trap of a generous image that must be financed.

That is why Machiavelli says a wise prince should not fear a reputation for being mean. In time, such a ruler may be more considered than if liberal because his revenues are enough, he can defend himself, and he can undertake enterprises without burdening his people. The sharp formula follows: he becomes liberal toward the many from whom he does not take, and mean toward the few to whom he does not give. The argument is distributive. Better the reproach of a few than the hatred of many who were forced to pay for your magnificence.

Julius II and the King of Spain confirm the point for established rulers. Julius used a reputation for liberality in reaching the papacy, but once in power he did not keep feeding that image through extraordinary taxes. The King of Spain, Machiavelli says, could not have done so much if he had been reputed liberal. Then the chapter makes its two crucial distinctions. A prince already in power is in a different case from one still trying to become one, so Caesar's liberality belongs to ambition more than preservation. And spending your own goods is different from spending others' goods. Cyrus, Caesar, and Alexander can be liberal with what is not theirs in the relevant sense, while an established ruler who squanders his own base ruins himself. The closing rule is severe: liberality quickly makes a ruler poor or rapacious, despised or hated. So a reputation for meanness is often wiser than a search for liberality that ends in hatred.
