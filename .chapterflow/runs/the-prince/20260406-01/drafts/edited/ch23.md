Chapter 23 refuses an easy answer to the problem of flattery. Princes need truth, because courts fill with flatterers. But if everyone may speak the truth whenever they like, respect for the prince falls away. Machiavelli therefore rejects both simple openness and simple secrecy. Truth has to be governed.

His answer is a third course. The prince chooses wise men, gives only them liberty to speak the truth, and only on matters he asks about. Then he questions them widely, listens patiently, and afterwards forms his own conclusions. Inside that gate, freer speech is rewarded. Outside it, the prince listens to no one and remains steadfast in what he resolves. The point is not to hide from truth. It is to prevent truth from dissolving into a crowd of competing voices.

That discipline protects the prince from two kinds of contempt. One comes from flatterers, when the ruler cannot hear unwelcome truth at all. The other comes from instability, when he keeps changing under varying opinions. Chapter 23 says the prince who does not keep counsel in order is either overthrown by flatterers or made contemptible by visible reversals. Listening is necessary, but so is remaining the judge after listening.

Maximilian shows the opposite error. Fra Luca says he consulted no one, yet never got his own way in anything. Machiavelli explains that Maximilian kept his plans secret and did not receive counsel beforehand, but once his actions became visible he was pliant under the obstruction of those around him. So he became both closed and weak at once. What he did one day he undid the next, and no one could rely on his resolutions.

The chapter then sharpens its rule. A prince should take counsel only when he wishes, discourage unsolicited advice, be a constant inquirer, and be a patient listener about what he has asked. If anyone withholds truth, he should let his anger be felt. Finally Machiavelli reverses the common vanity about good advisers. A prince does not become wise because he has them. Good counsels are born of the prince's own wisdom, because only a wise prince can choose advisers, question them, see through their interests, and prevent either one prudent man or many competing men from taking hold of the state. That is why Chapter 23 turns naturally toward Chapter 24. Lost states are what remain when rulers cannot govern advice, truth, and steadiness together.
