A city can look calm and still be waiting for its next chance. It may pay tribute, keep some of its old laws, and even accept a friendly inner circle backed by the new ruler, yet none of that proves the deeper problem is gone. Machiavelli opens with three ways to hold a place used to living under its own laws and in freedom: ruin it, reside there in person, or let it keep its laws while drawing tribute through an oligarchy that depends on your favor. At first that sounds like a menu. By the end, it no longer does.

The third course has an obvious appeal. A local oligarchy created by the prince knows it cannot stand without his friendship and interest, so it has reason to support him. Rule through the city's own citizens looks cleaner than permanent occupation. It seems cheaper, less violent, and more intelligent than breaking everything apart. But a city accustomed to freedom does not simply retain administrative habits that can be reused under new management. It remembers ruling itself. Its laws and privileges carry a prior identity, and leaving them in place can preserve the language in which resistance will gather again. The mild arrangement is therefore not automatically the prudent one. It may leave the conqueror leaning on local authorities while the city's deepest loyalty remains elsewhere.

Sparta and Rome test that appearance. The Spartans held Athens and Thebes by establishing oligarchies there, yet they lost them. The Romans dismantled Capua, Carthage, and Numantia and did not lose them. Rome also tried the Spartan route in Greece, keeping cities free and permitting their laws, and failed there as well. The comparison narrows the lesson. Where a city has lived in freedom, distance plus indirect control may preserve exactly what the conqueror most needs to dissolve. So Machiavelli presses to the brutal line: there is no safe way to retain such cities other than by ruining them. The point is not which policy sounds milder. It is which one actually removes the political basis for renewed resistance.

That basis has a name. In rebellion, a free city has the watchword of liberty and its ancient privileges as a rallying point, and neither time nor benefits will cause it to forget. Outward calm can therefore be a false reading. Benefits do not cancel remembered independence. Time does not automatically turn a self-governing people into subjects who have forgotten their former standing. Machiavelli even adds a limit: unless they are disunited or dispersed, they do not forget that name or those privileges. Pisa makes the point severe. Even after a hundred years under Florentine bondage, the city still rallied to the memory of what it had been. A conqueror who points to present quiet or improved conditions may only be describing the interval before memory finds its next opening.

The chapter closes by contrasting this case with places used to living under a prince. Once the old princely family has been exterminated, such places are slower to take up arms. They are accustomed to obedience, cannot agree in making one from among themselves, and do not know how to govern themselves. Republics differ because the memory they carry is not attachment to one line but practice in self-rule, joined to vitality, hatred, and desire for vengeance. That is why the conclusion narrows so sharply: the safest way is to destroy them or to reside there. From here Machiavelli turns to a different problem, not how to hold a city that remembers liberty, but how new rule is founded by one's own arms and ability.
