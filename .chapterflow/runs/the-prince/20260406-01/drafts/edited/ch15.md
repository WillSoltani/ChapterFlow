Chapter 15 changes the method of the book before it changes any single moral verdict. Machiavelli says he wants to write something useful, which means following the real truth of the matter rather than the imagination of it. Many writers, he says, have pictured republics and principalities never known or seen. He refuses that comfort. The problem is not that virtue sounds foolish. The problem is that imagined perfection can become politically useless when rulers have to govern in the world as it is.

That is why the chapter's central contrast is between how people live and how they ought to live. A prince who neglects what is done for what ought to be done works his ruin rather than his preservation. Machiavelli is not praising corruption for its own sake. He is warning that rule happens among people, pressures, and dangers that do not wait for ideal conditions. If a ruler behaves as though the world were already morally orderly, he gives up the judgment needed to survive it.

The famous consequence follows: a prince wishing to hold his own must know how to do wrong, and to use that knowledge or not according to necessity. The phrase is narrower than it first sounds. Machiavelli is not endorsing unlimited wickedness. He is saying the prince must not be helpless when preservation requires conduct that will not look pure. Necessity is the key word. It keeps the chapter tied to prudence rather than appetite.

Machiavelli then lists the qualities for which princes are praised or blamed: liberal and miserly, generous and rapacious, cruel and compassionate, faithful and faithless, bold and cowardly, and so on. Everyone would prefer a prince to possess all the qualities counted good. But human conditions do not permit it. That is why prudence must rank faults instead of dreaming of flawless character. The prince should avoid the vices that lose the state and, when needed, accept reproach for faults without which the state can hardly be saved.

The closing reversal is the chapter's hardest rule. Some things that look like virtues bring ruin if followed. Some things that look like vices bring security and prosperity. Chapter 15 does not yet settle which cases those will be. It sets the measure by which they must be judged. From here on, Machiavelli asks not only what sounds admirable, but what actually preserves rule under real conditions.
