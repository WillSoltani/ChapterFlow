Chapter 10 stops asking how a prince rose and asks what the principality can survive. Machiavelli measures strength by a hard distinction: can the ruler support himself with his own resources, or does he always need the assistance of others? In practice that means one of two conditions. Some rulers can raise enough men or money to meet an attacker in the field. Others cannot show themselves there and must defend themselves by sheltering behind walls. The chapter matters because Machiavelli refuses to blur those cases into one vague idea of toughness.

If the prince belongs to the second kind, the advice is exact. He should provision and fortify the town and not try to defend the whole country. That sounds narrower than bravery talk, but it is more realistic. A ruler who cannot win openly should not waste strength by acting as if every exposed edge can be saved. Yet the city is not strong just because it has walls. Machiavelli adds a political condition at once: it must be well fortified and the prince must not be hated by his people. Fortification and civic temper belong to the same problem.

The German cities show what that means. They have ditches, walls, artillery, and public stores for a year. They also keep people at work in the labours that sustain the city, honor military exercises, and maintain ordinances. The example is not about hardware alone. Machiavelli is showing a city that looks costly to attack and credible to endure. Visible difficulty discourages enemies, while stores, work, and routine help the citizens believe the defence is real.

The hardest objection comes next. What if citizens see their property outside the walls burnt? Will not a long siege and self-interest make them forget their prince? Machiavelli answers that a powerful and courageous prince can manage this by giving hope that the evil will not last, fear of the enemy's cruelty, and adroit treatment of subjects who are too bold. He also notes the timing: the enemy burns the country early, while spirits are still hot. The loss is real, but it arrives before discouragement finishes its work.

Then comes the chapter's sharp paradox. Once the damage is already done, citizens may unite more closely with the prince because their houses and possessions were ruined in his defence. Men are bound, Machiavelli says, by the benefits they confer as much as by those they receive. But this only holds if the prince does not fail to support and defend them. Shared loss produces steadfastness only when the ruler still carries his side of the defence.

Chapter 10 therefore measures strength by fit, not by swagger. If the prince can fight in the field, that is one condition. If he cannot, strength means fortified concentration, provisions, visible difficulty, anti-odium, and the ability to keep citizens steady after the outer country burns. That is why the next chapter feels so exceptional: ecclesiastical principalities will seem secure by means that ordinary walls and resources cannot fully explain.
