How do you read a chapter that admits wicked seizure can work and still refuses to honor it? Machiavelli's answer is severe from the start. A private man may rise by criminal means, and that fact has to be faced politically. But the chapter's first rule is just as important as its second: such methods may gain empire, not glory.

Agathocles is the first proof. He rises from low birth through the military profession, seizes Syracuse by slaughtering the senate and leading citizens, then proves formidable enough to defend the city and force terms from the Carthaginians. Machiavelli does not credit this to fortune. He also does not let endurance in danger turn massacre into greatness. Agathocles matters because he joins real competence to acts that cannot be celebrated. The chapter insists that political success and honor are not the same thing.

Oliverotto da Fermo sharpens the point through deceit. He returns home under the appearance of filial respect, is received honorably by the uncle who raised him, stages a banquet, lures the city's chiefs into a private chamber, and has them killed. For a year he secures the city, removes malcontents, and becomes dangerous to his neighbors. Machiavelli does not deny that such a seizure can hold for a time. He asks instead why some cruel rulers stay secure while others never stop producing fresh enemies.

His answer is the chapter's real mechanism. The difference is not between evil and innocence. It is between harms that stop and harms that keep multiplying. Severities are, if one can speak that way at all, properly used when they are delivered at one blow for immediate security and not continued afterward except insofar as later measures turn toward the subjects' advantage. They are badly used when they begin smaller and keep growing. That distinction does not redeem wickedness. It explains why one vicious regime is less self-defeating than another.

From there Machiavelli gives the rule readers remember. Injuries should be done all at once, so they are tasted less. Benefits should be given little by little, so their flavor lasts. The point is not moral compensation. It is political memory. A ruler who keeps reopening punishment teaches everyone to wait for the next blow. That is why Machiavelli says such a man must keep the knife in his hand. He has built a regime that depends on continued injury rather than on any settled attachment.

The final paragraph adds the limit that prevents the chapter from sounding like admiration. A prince must live among his people so sudden events do not force late harshness or late mildness, both of which fail when crisis arrives. Rule cannot be stabilized by last-minute correction once expectation has hardened. Chapter 8 therefore ends in a deliberately uneasy place: wicked seizure may sometimes secure power, but it never becomes glorious, and it remains politically brittle whenever injury keeps renewing itself. That sets up Chapter 9, where Machiavelli turns from criminal acquisition to civil principality and asks what rule looks like when it grows from the city's conflict between nobles and people instead.
