Before Machiavelli tells a prince what to do, he forces him to answer a colder question: what exactly has he taken hold of? That is a strange way to open a book now famous for strategy. You might expect an immediate lesson in daring, cruelty, persuasion, or war. Instead, he starts with categories. All states, he says, are either republics or principalities. Then he narrows the field and begins cutting the second category into smaller ones. The move can look dry on first contact. It is not dry at all. It is the first serious warning in the book. Anyone who asks how to keep power before naming what kind of power this is has already blurred together cases that will not behave the same way.

That is why the first split matters so much. Principalities are either hereditary or new. Those labels do more than sort regimes into neat drawers. A hereditary state comes wrapped in continuity. The ruling family has been there long enough that rule itself feels familiar. A new principality does not arrive with that advantage. It arrives with rupture, replacement, and comparison. Even at this early stage, Machiavelli is teaching a hard political habit: stop treating possession as a single fact. Start asking what history came with it. The same word, "rule," can cover situations that differ at the root.

He sharpens the point by refusing to let "new" stay simple. Some new principalities are entirely new, as Milan was to Francesco Sforza. Others are annexed members attached to an existing hereditary state, as Naples was to the King of Spain. That is not a minor refinement. It means novelty itself has kinds. A prince who founds something wholly new faces one sort of problem. A prince who adds new territory to an older body faces another. In the first case the whole structure must be established. In the second, the new possession arrives already measured against the old state it has been joined to. "New" turns out to be too blunt a word once Machiavelli opens it.

Then he introduces a distinction that cuts beneath the ruler and reaches the people being ruled. A dominion may be accustomed to living under a prince, or accustomed to living in freedom. That line changes everything because it changes what kind of memory the territory carries. People used to one ruler have one set of habits. People used to ordering themselves have another. Machiavelli does not pause here to tell you the full answer. He only makes it impossible to pretend that prior civic life is background detail. The place you acquire is not raw material. It comes to you with memory already inside it.

He adds one more axis and makes the map harder still. States are acquired by the arms of the prince himself, or by the arms of others, or else by fortune, or by ability. Now the classification becomes almost uncomfortable because the labels can stack. A prince may annex territory long used to freedom and do it with borrowed force. Another may establish a wholly new state through his own ability. Another may inherit a quiet dominion without having earned any of it. The route into power is not separate from the thing acquired. It helps define the thing. Borrowed force leaves one kind of dependency behind. Personal ability leaves another. Fortune may open a door that ability did not build. Ability may seize a chance fortune did not guarantee.

That is the hard edge hidden inside this short opening. Machiavelli gives a grid before he gives advice, but the grid immediately creates a live problem of its own: when several labels apply at once, which distinction governs first? He does not answer that question here. He does something more disciplined. He makes it impossible to believe that all principalities can be ruled by one clean rule. Politics begins with classification before it begins with technique. Misclassification is the first unforced error in rule. Once that is clear, the book can finally move to a more precise question: if a principality is hereditary rather than new, what exactly makes it easier to keep?
