Why does the ruler who climbs the hardest sometimes stand the firmest once he arrives? Machiavelli opens this chapter by refusing the comfort of ordinary examples. If the subject is an entirely new principality, he wants the reader looking at founders on the highest scale, and he gives the image of the archer who aims above the mark in order to hit it. The point is not that imitation makes one equal to Moses, Cyrus, Romulus, or Theseus. It is that founding cannot begin from a low standard. A new prince has to think above the immediate target because he is not entering a settled inheritance. He is trying to create a rule that did not exist in his hands before. That is why Machiavelli quickly narrows from the broad pair of ability and fortune to a harder line: the man who has relied least on fortune stands strongest. If he has no other state and must live in the new one himself, so much the better, because distance gives him fewer places to hide from the work of founding.

The great names matter here for a specific reason. Machiavelli does not say fortune did nothing for them. He says fortune gave them the opportunity, the raw material, the opening in the world that could be shaped. Moses finds a people oppressed and ready to follow release. Cyrus finds the Persians dissatisfied and the Medes softened. Romulus is forced out of the place he might have remained. Theseus finds the Athenians scattered. None of those circumstances is yet a principality. They become politically meaningful only because someone sees the opening and gives it form. That is the chapter's first hard distinction. Opportunity without ability stays unused, and ability without opportunity has nothing to seize. Fortune can place the stone in the field, but it does not cut it, move it, or build the wall. Machiavelli is already teaching the reader not to confuse the occasion with the power that turns the occasion into a durable order.

From there the chapter moves into its central paradox. Those who rise by their own arms and ability acquire with difficulty, but keep with ease. The ascent hurts because they cannot step into arrangements that already favor them. They have to introduce new rules and methods, and that means they are not merely taking possession of a place. They are unsettling a whole distribution of advantage. Machiavelli calls the introduction of a new order the most difficult, perilous, and uncertain labor in politics, and the reason is plain enough once he states it. Everyone who did well under the old conditions becomes an active enemy. Everyone who may do well under the new conditions defends only weakly. The losers know exactly what they are losing, while the possible winners have not yet felt the gain in their hands. The founder therefore climbs under pressure from both sides at once. He is opposed by people with habits, laws, and interests already on their side, and he is only half-supported by those who are not yet sure the new order will hold.

That is why Machiavelli does not let the chapter settle for a simple celebration of bold founders. The hardest part of founding is not vision. It is surviving the period in which the old order is organized and the new one is not. He presses the question sharply: can the innovator rely on himself, or must he depend on others? Can he complete the enterprise by prayers, or can he use force? The distinction is brutal, but it is exact. People are easy to persuade and difficult to keep persuaded. The moment disbelief returns, applause evaporates. If the founder has no means of making the new order stand when enthusiasm cools, then persuasion has only rented him a moment. This is what sits behind the harsh line about armed prophets and unarmed ones. Machiavelli is not admiring force as a mood. He is saying that a new order needs some instrument by which the founder can keep it from dissolving the first time belief weakens.

Savonarola is the warning that makes the argument immediate. Machiavelli does not leave the point at the level of Moses or Romulus. He names a recent figure who tried to found a new order and fell as soon as the multitude no longer believed. That example keeps the chapter from drifting into legend. A founder can persuade, excite, and gather followers, yet still fail because he has no way to keep the persuaded firm or to compel the unbelieving. The danger is greatest in the ascent, when envy, disbelief, and habit all press against the new arrangement. But the chapter does not stay there. Once the founder survives that climb, once the main enemies are broken and the order stops looking experimental, the same prince who suffered most in acquiring begins to look secure, respected, and durable. The pain of self-made acquisition is front-loaded. The reward is that the structure gradually belongs to the founder instead of to the fortune that first lifted him.

Hiero gives Machiavelli the smaller and more practical version of the whole argument. Chosen by oppressed Syracusans and then made prince, he is not important because he proves a private citizen can rise. He is important because he shows what rising well looks like once opportunity appears. He abolishes the old soldiery, organizes the new, abandons old alliances, and makes new ones. That sequence explains what it means to found by one's own arms. It is not a mystical quality of toughness. It is the work of replacing inherited supports with supports that answer to you. Hiero had much trouble in acquiring and little in keeping because, by the end, the foundations were his. That is the closing pressure of the chapter. If self-made founding is so hard because the ruler has to build everything while climbing, what happens to the man who is raised quickly by someone else's fortune and only afterwards discovers that nothing underneath him is yet his own?
