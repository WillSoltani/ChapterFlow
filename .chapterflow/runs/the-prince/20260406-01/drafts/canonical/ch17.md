Chapter 17 begins with a reversal that is easy to miss if one only listens to the moral labels. Every prince, Machiavelli says, should want to be thought clement rather than cruel. He grants the attraction of mercy at once. But then he asks what mercy does when it is used badly. Cesare Borgia had a reputation for cruelty, yet Machiavelli says that cruelty reconciled Romagna, unified it, and restored peace and loyalty. The Florentines, by contrast, tried to avoid the reproach of cruelty and allowed Pistoia to be destroyed. The comparison is the chapter's first hard edge. The question is not which word sounds nobler. It is whether the ruler's softness lets a wider disorder rip through the people while he preserves a cleaner image for himself.

That is why Machiavelli says a prince who keeps his subjects united and loyal should not mind the reproach of cruelty too much. A few examples, he argues, may be more merciful than a looseness that lets murders and robberies arise. The severity offends particular individuals. The resulting disorder injures the whole people. Chapter 17 therefore judges mercy by its public consequences, not by its surface tone. It is less interested in whether punishment feels hard than in whether indulgence multiplies victims. This is also why the new prince occupies such a sharp place in the argument. New states are full of dangers, so the ruler cannot avoid some imputation of cruelty. Yet Machiavelli does not license blind harshness. He immediately adds that such a prince should be slow to believe and to act, should not show fear himself, and should proceed with prudence, temperance, and humanity. The chapter is not praising rage. It is ranking risks in an unstable field.

Only after that setup does Machiavelli raise the question everyone remembers: is it better to be loved than feared, or feared than loved? Ideally, he says, a ruler would have both. But if one of the two must be surrendered, it is much safer to be feared than loved. The reason is not mystical. It is a claim about human reliability. People are grateful while success is distant, promises are cheap, and sacrifice remains hypothetical. They offer blood, property, life, and children when the need is far away. When need arrives, they turn. Love rests on a bond of obligation, and Machiavelli thinks such bonds are broken whenever advantage encourages it. Fear rests on the dread of punishment, and that dread does not fail in the same way. So the ruler who relies on affection alone is building on a material he does not control.

Still, the chapter refuses the lazy conclusion that feared rule can be careless rule. Fear must not become hatred. That is the real limit. Machiavelli says a prince can endure being feared very well so long as he is not hated, and he gives that condition practical content. The ruler should abstain from the property of his citizens and subjects and from their women. If he must proceed against someone's life, he should do it on proper justification and manifest cause. The line about property is especially sharp. Men, he says, forget the death of a father more quickly than the loss of patrimony. Whether or not one accepts the claim in full, the political point is clear. Arbitrary seizure, private violation, and unexplained violence produce a hatred that disciplined punishment need not. Chapter 17 is therefore not contrasting kindness with terror. It is contrasting ordered severity with hated caprice.

The military discussion shows how far this logic runs. A prince commanding an army cannot worry too much about a reputation for cruelty, because discipline among a multitude of soldiers requires a fear strong enough to hold them together. Hannibal becomes Machiavelli's proof. He led a vast mixed army in foreign lands without internal dissension, and Machiavelli credits that achievement not only to valor but to a cruelty that made Hannibal revered and terrible to his troops. Scipio becomes the counterexample. He was excellent and celebrated, but his excessive forbearance gave soldiers too much license. Disorder followed, and he was rebuked for it. The pair matters because it prevents a sentimental reading. Some forms of softness flatter moral vanity while dissolving obedience in exactly the places where obedience is needed most.

The close returns from army command to the general rule. Men love according to their own will and fear according to that of the prince. So a wise prince should found himself on what lies in his own control and not on what lies in the control of others. That does not mean he should seek hatred as a badge of strength. On the contrary, he must strive to avoid it. Chapter 17 therefore refines the method of Chapter 15 and the reversal of Chapter 16. Admired clemency can become public injury. Feared rule can be safer than loved rule. But only a fear shaped by justification, restraint, and discipline is politically usable. The chapter does not ask whether mercy sounds lovely. It asks whether mercy keeps order, whether punishment remains bounded, and whether the ruler has built himself on something firmer than affection. Chapter 18 will now shift from punishment to promises and ask how far princely appearances can be trusted when necessity presses.
