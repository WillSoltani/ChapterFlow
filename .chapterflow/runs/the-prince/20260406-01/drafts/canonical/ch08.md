What do you do with a chapter that says a man can seize power through atrocity and still not deserve admiration? Machiavelli begins Chapter 8 by refusing a simple answer. He will not pretend wicked acquisition does not happen. Nor will he flatter it into greatness. A private man may rise by criminal means, and if that happens the political question becomes brutally practical: what kind of rule follows from such a beginning? The chapter's first hard line is therefore the one readers most need to keep in view. Some deeds may secure empire. They still do not earn glory.

Agathocles is the first proof. Machiavelli chooses a man of low origin who rises through the military profession, shows real endurance in danger, and then stains everything with massacre. The episode at Syracuse matters because it joins competence to horror. Agathocles calls together the senate and leading citizens as if public business were to be discussed, then has them killed and takes the city without civil commotion. Afterward he does not simply collapse. He defends Syracuse, survives military reverses, and pressures the Carthaginians hard enough to make terms. Machiavelli refuses the easy explanation that fortune carried him. Very little, he says, can be credited to fortune here. Yet that refusal does not become praise. To kill fellow citizens, betray friends, and live without faith or mercy may gain dominion, but it does not make a man admirable. Agathocles becomes the chapter's first lesson in the difference between effectiveness and honor.

Oliverotto da Fermo sharpens the lesson by making the seizure more theatrical. He returns home under the appearance of filial honor, is received generously by the uncle who raised him, stages a banquet, shifts the conversation into a private chamber, and turns hospitality into slaughter. The pattern is important because Machiavelli is not just cataloguing evil men. He is comparing forms of acquisition. Oliverotto's rule is born in deception, fear, and a carefully timed strike against exactly those people who might resist him. For a year he does hold. He kills malcontents, issues new civil and military ordinances, and becomes formidable to neighbors. The chapter does not deny the holding power of such a start. It insists instead that readers look at the price and at the mechanism. The seizure works for a time because the worst dangers are not merely threatened. They are removed at once. But the very structure of the act keeps Oliverotto outside anything like glory, and his end shows how thin such security remains.

That leads Machiavelli to the chapter's real analytical question. If so many rulers use cruelty, why do some of them stay secure for a while while others never stop trembling? His answer is grim and precise. The issue is not whether the regime contains evil. The issue is whether the harms keep multiplying. Machiavelli says that severities are, if one can even use the phrase, properly used when they are applied at one blow for immediate security and not persisted in afterward except insofar as they are turned toward the subjects' advantage. They are badly used when they begin small and then grow. That distinction does not redeem the evil. It explains why one form of wicked rule is politically less self-defeating than the other. A seizure that closes its own punitive phase may still be odious. A seizure that keeps reopening injury teaches everyone to live in expectation of the next blow.

The chapter's most memorable rule follows from that distinction. Injuries should be done all at once, so they are tasted less and do not have to be repeated daily. Benefits should be given little by little, so their flavor lasts. Machiavelli is not offering moral balance here, as if later favors could wash away original wrongs. He is making a claim about memory, fear, and attachment. Repeated injury keeps the ruler permanently armed against his own subjects. If every week brings another confiscation, expulsion, humiliation, or punishment, then no one can settle into obedience because everyone is still waiting to learn whether he is next. That is why Machiavelli says the ruler who acts from timidity or bad advice in this matter must keep the knife in his hand. He has created a regime that depends on continual reopening of fear.

The last paragraph adds a quieter but crucial limit. A prince must live among his people in such a way that unexpected good or evil does not force him into sudden changes. If the crisis comes and he then reaches for harsh measures, he is too late. If he suddenly turns mild, that fails too, because it looks forced and wins no gratitude. The point is not just proximity in space. It is political presence before the emergency arrives. Rule cannot be stabilized by last-minute correction once trust, fear, and expectation have already hardened. That final thought keeps Chapter 8 from sounding like a manual of successful brutality. Even on its own brutal terms, wicked acquisition remains fragile if it has to improvise its severity or its favors under pressure.

So the chapter leaves the reader in a deliberately uncomfortable place. It has explained how a wicked seizure may hold longer than a softer but recurring cruelty, and it has still denied the wicked ruler glory. That tension matters because the next chapter turns away from atrocity and toward civil principality, where a private citizen rises not by open crime but by the favor of fellow citizens. After this dark accounting of seizure through evil, Machiavelli is ready to ask what changes when rule begins inside the city's ordinary conflict between nobles and people.
