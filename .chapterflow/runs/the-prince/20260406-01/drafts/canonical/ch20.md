Chapter 20 can look at first like Machiavelli emptying out a drawer of princely tricks. Arm subjects. Disarm them. Foster factions. Gain over the distrusted. Build fortresses. Tear them down. That surface is misleading. The chapter is really asking one hard question: which devices actually make a prince safer in the state he has, and which only imitate safety? Machiavelli refuses a single abstract answer because each expedient changes meaning once the particulars change. What matters is not whether the device sounds forceful or clever. What matters is whether it turns men and institutions into the prince's own strength, or whether it reveals distrust, breeds hatred, and rests on peacetime illusions.

That is why the first distinction in the chapter is so important. A new prince in a new principality arms subjects rather than disarming them. The reason is not generosity. It is conversion. Once armed by him, those subjects become more his. The distrusted can become faithful, the faithful remain so, and the newly armed understand that danger and service justify differentiated reward. Disarming does the opposite. It tells subjects they are suspected, either as cowards or as disloyal, and Machiavelli says that opinion breeds hatred. Worse, once a prince disarms his people he cannot remain unarmed himself, so he is pushed toward mercenaries, whose political weakness the book has already established. Yet Machiavelli immediately refuses to universalize even this rule. If the prince acquires a new state and adds it as a province to an older one, then disarming that province may be necessary, because the point is to concentrate arms in the prince's own longstanding men. The device is not good or bad by itself. Its value depends on whether the prince is founding new attachment or folding a suspicious annex into an older body.

The same conditional reasoning destroys the old wisdom about factions. Earlier rulers had said it was useful to hold Pistoia by factions and Pisa by fortresses. Machiavelli thinks that may once have looked plausible in a relatively balanced Italy, but he denies it as a present rule. Divided cities are weak when pressure arrives from outside, because the weaker party will call in external force and the stronger party will be unable to resist. Venice becomes the example of this error. The Venetians encouraged old rivalries in tributary cities so the inhabitants would remain too distracted to unite against them. Yet after the rout at Vaila, one party immediately took courage and seized the state. A method that looks manageable in peace proves ruinous in war. So the chapter starts to expose a recurring illusion: some devices work only so long as the real test has not yet come.

Machiavelli then makes the chapter stranger by admitting that new princes can sometimes profit from difficulty itself. Fortune may allow enemies to arise so the prince can overcome them and mount higher by the very ladder they raise against him. That is why some think a wise ruler may even foster a measure of animosity against himself when he has the opportunity, so that crushing it increases renown. The chapter does not celebrate hostility for its own sake. It says obstacles can become politically useful when they end in visible mastery. The same logic appears in the chapter's treatment of distrusted men. New princes, Machiavelli says, have often found more fidelity in those first distrusted than in those first trusted. Pandolfo Petrucci ruled more by the former than the latter. The reason is not mysterious. Men who know they began under suspicion may work harder to erase that impression by deeds. Those who begin in easy security may neglect the prince's affairs.

But even here Machiavelli splits appearances. Not every early supporter is a durable friend, and not every early enemy is equally recoverable. Men who were initially hostile but who need the prince in order to support themselves can often be gained over with relative ease, precisely because their position requires service strong enough to reverse the old judgment against them. By contrast, men who favored the prince only because they were discontented with the former government are much harder to satisfy. They helped not out of natural affection but out of grievance, and grievance does not end once power changes hands. This is one of the chapter's most useful corrections. The easiest allies to mistake for loyal are often the ones most impossible to content, while former skeptics with something to prove may serve more faithfully than flatterers who arrived under no pressure at all.

Fortresses gather all of these lessons into one visible symbol. Machiavelli does not simply mock them. Princes have long built them as bridles for internal enemies and refuges from a first attack, and he acknowledges that they can help. Yet he also names recent rulers who demolished fortresses because they judged them more dangerous than useful. Nicolo Vitelli did so at Citta di Castello. Guido Ubaldo did so at Urbino after returning from Borgia's expulsion. The Bentivogli made a similar choice in Bologna. So the chapter refuses to turn masonry into doctrine. If a prince fears the people more than foreigners, fortresses may help. If he fears foreigners more than the people, he may be better without them. Francesco Sforza's castle at Milan becomes the warning case: the structure built for security brought the house of Sforza more trouble than other disorders in the state.

That is why the chapter ends with its strongest simplification. The best possible fortress is not to be hated by the people. Physical defenses are still secondary to political feeling. If the people hate the prince, foreigners will not be lacking to assist them, and walls will not save him. The Countess of Forli seems, at first, to offer a counterexample, because her fortress let her survive a popular attack and wait for help from Milan after her husband was killed. Yet even that advantage proved temporary. When Cesare Borgia attacked and the people were allied with foreigners, the fortress counted for little. Machiavelli's closing judgment is exact: praise the prince who builds and the prince who does not, if the choice fits his condition; blame the prince who trusts fortresses and cares little about being hated by the people. That final priority prepares Chapter 21 perfectly. Once the devices of security have been cut down to size, the next question is no longer what protects the prince mechanically, but what makes him esteemed and renowned in the first place.
