If fortune really governs human affairs, why has Machiavelli spent an entire book giving princes advice? Chapter 25 opens exactly on that problem. Many people, he says, believe that fortune and God govern the world so completely that human wisdom cannot direct events and no one can even help them. In a period of violent political changes, he admits he partly understands the attraction of that opinion. But he refuses to let it extinguish free will. His first move is a division rather than a denial. Fortune may be the arbiter of one-half of our actions, perhaps even a little more, yet not of all of them. That opening matters because it rejects two lazy comforts at once. It rejects the comfort of thinking everything is controllable if only one is clever enough. It also rejects the comfort of surrender, the claim that since fortune is powerful, labor is pointless. Machiavelli wants a prince to live inside the harder space between those fantasies, where contingency is real but does not excuse passivity.

The chapter then gives that middle position its most memorable image. Fortune is like a raging river. When it floods, it overflows plains, sweeps away trees and buildings, and forces everything before it. But the destructive force of the flood does not mean men should do nothing while the weather is fair. In calm conditions they can build defenses and barriers so that the next surge is channeled, limited, and less dangerous. That metaphor does more than say that preparation is useful. It says fortune's violence becomes decisive where resistance has not already been raised. The point is sharpened by the turn to Italy. Italy is described as an open country, without barriers and without defense, and therefore especially exposed to great changes. So the river image does not cancel human agency. It relocates it. Agency works most powerfully before the crisis, when barriers can still be built, not after the waters have already taken the field. Chapter 25 therefore continues the lesson of Chapter 24 in another key. The rulers who blamed fortune after collapse were often the same rulers who had failed to build the defenses that fortune would later expose as absent.

Machiavelli then narrows from fortune in general to princes in particular, and here the chapter becomes even sharper. A prince may be happy today and ruined tomorrow without showing any change in disposition or character. That means the problem is not always visible instability in the ruler. Sometimes the ruler stays the same while the times stop rewarding what he is. The prince who relies entirely on fortune is lost when it changes, but the failure is deeper than luck alone. Success belongs to the ruler whose actions accord with the spirit of the times. Machiavelli insists that human beings reach glory and riches through many opposite methods: caution and haste, force and skill, patience and its contrary. One can even see two men acting differently and both succeeding, or two men acting the same way and one failing. That is the chapter's hardest analytical turn. The question is not which single method is always correct. The question is whether the method suits the moment. What looks like virtue in one season becomes blindness in another if the times have turned against it.

That claim becomes harsher when Machiavelli explains why adaptation is so rare. Men do not often know how to accommodate themselves to change, partly because nature inclines each man toward one way of acting, and partly because success hardens habit. The ruler who has prospered through caution cannot easily be persuaded to leave it. The patient man who rose by waiting feels confirmed in his method precisely by the fact that it worked. So when the moment requires adventure, he does not know how to turn. The problem is not ignorance alone. It is character educated by prior success. That is why Chapter 25 is not just a lesson about planning. It is also a lesson about self-limitation. The same traits that made a ruler successful can become the causes of ruin once conditions shift. Machiavelli is severe here because he treats constancy, usually praised as a virtue, as politically dangerous when it hardens into one fixed style. Fortune changes, but mankind stays steadfast in its ways. The collision between moving circumstances and fixed temperament is where estates change hands.

Pope Julius II is introduced to show how the rule works in practice. Julius acted impetuously in all his affairs, and because the times conformed to that disposition, he succeeded. The Bologna enterprise is the key example. The Venetians were not agreeable, the King of Spain was not agreeable, and the enterprise was still under discussion with the King of France. A more cautious pontiff would have waited in Rome until plans were arranged and everything fixed. Julius did the opposite. He personally entered upon the expedition with his usual boldness. That abrupt movement made Spain and the Venetians hesitate and dragged France along, because France found it harder to refuse a Pope already in motion than one still hesitating at the planning stage. The example matters because it is not simply praise of courage. Julius succeeded because his audacity fit the political temperature of the moment. Machiavelli immediately adds the limit: if times had later required caution, Julius would have been ruined, because he would never have departed from the methods to which nature inclined him. So Julius is not proof that boldness always wins. He is proof that a fixed style looks like genius only when the times happen to reward it.

From there the conclusion follows with unsettling force. Fortune is changeful, mankind steadfast in its ways; success comes when they agree and failure when they fall out. Machiavelli then says, for his part, that it is better to be adventurous than cautious. But even here the chapter should not be flattened into a slogan. That preference comes after the analysis of fit, not before it. It reflects his sense that in the Italy before him, boldness answered the hour more effectively than caution. The final metaphor about fortune as a woman who yields to violent audacity is coercive, gendered, and deliberately provocative. Modern readers should not import it as literal advice. What matters analytically is the shape of the argument underneath it: fortune favors those who meet changing conditions with forceful initiative rather than cold delay when the moment demands movement. Chapter 25 therefore leaves the reader with two tasks, not one. Build barriers before the flood, and learn that no single method remains safe forever. The ruler who prepares but cannot adapt is still exposed. The ruler who improvises boldly but never prepares is exposed in another way. What fortune governs is large, but what human beings still control is large enough to matter. That is why the next chapter can move from theory to exhortation. Once contingency has been measured and free will preserved, the final question is whether anyone in Italy will seize the moment fortune has made possible.
