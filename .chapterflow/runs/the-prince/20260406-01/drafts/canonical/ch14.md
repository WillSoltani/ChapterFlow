After condemning mercenaries and auxiliaries, Machiavelli turns the question inward. If the prince cannot safely borrow the force that keeps him standing, what must he himself do? Chapter 14 answers with unusual severity: the ruler should have no study more central than war, its rules, and its discipline. This is not a decorative preference. Machiavelli says it is the sole art belonging to one who rules. It upholds those born princes and can raise private men into princely rank. The reverse is equally sharp. Princes who think more of ease than arms lose their states. Francesco Sforza becomes Duke of Milan by being martial; his sons, by avoiding the hardships of arms, fall from dukes into private condition. The point is not that the ruler should be theatrically bellicose. It is that security and rank rest on a competence he cannot afford to outsource or postpone.

That claim reaches beyond the battlefield into authority itself. An unarmed prince among armed servants does not merely lack technique. He invites contempt. Machiavelli says there is nothing proportionate between the armed and the unarmed, and therefore no stable trust between them. The armed servant looks upward with disdain, and the unarmed ruler looks outward with suspicion. Soldiers cannot fully respect a commander who does not understand the craft that governs their danger. So war study is bound up with command, not just emergency response. The prince who neglects it cannot rely on his own people. That is why Machiavelli says war must never leave the ruler's mind, and in peace he should exercise himself in it more than in war. Peace is not the excuse for forgetting. It is the best condition for storing the judgment that conflict will later test.

Machiavelli then divides this preparation into action and study. On the action side, the prince keeps men organized and drilled and hardens himself against effort. Hunting matters here not as sport but as a way of accustoming the body to hardship while teaching the shape of the land. He must learn how mountains rise, valleys open, plains lie, and rivers and marshes alter movement. This knowledge helps in two linked ways. First, it teaches defence of one's own country in real detail instead of from abstract maps. Second, it trains the eye to read other places by analogy, because localities resemble one another enough for practiced judgment to transfer. Machiavelli makes the practical use explicit: terrain knowledge helps surprise an enemy, choose quarters, lead armies, array battle, and besiege towns to advantage. Chapter 14 therefore rejects leadership from upholstered distance. The ruler must know the ground on which danger will actually unfold.

Philopoemen shows what this looks like in habit. In peace, while moving through the countryside with friends, he treated the landscape as a standing school of war. If the enemy were on that hill and we were here, who would hold the advantage? If we advanced, how would we keep order? If we retreated, how should pursuit unfold? The value of this example is not military romance. It is the elimination of surprise by repeated rehearsal. Ordinary travel becomes stored judgment because each path, rise, and bottleneck is tested in imagination before it must be tested in danger. Machiavelli praises this because war rarely defeats the prepared only by force. It defeats them by presenting a situation they have never previously learned to read. Philopoemen's gift is that he keeps turning peace into a workshop where that ignorance is slowly removed.

The second half of the chapter shifts from body and ground to intellect and memory. The prince must read histories and study the actions of illustrious men, paying attention to how they bore themselves in war and to the causes of their victories and defeats. Machiavelli's point is concrete: imitate the former, avoid the latter. That is why he names exemplary chains of imitation. Alexander the Great took Achilles as a model. Caesar took Alexander. Scipio took Cyrus, as known through Xenophon, and found in that imitation part of his glory. History here is not a shelf of noble stories. It is a training ground for recognition. By studying earlier conduct under strain, the ruler acquires patterns before the present forces him to improvise them badly. Chapter 14 joins field knowledge to historical memory so that neither raw experience nor bookish learning stands alone.

The close gathers these strands into a rule for peace. A wise prince never stands idle in peaceful times. He increases his resources with industry so that if fortune changes, she finds him prepared to resist her blows. That sentence explains the chapter's whole pressure. Preparation is not only about winning campaigns. It is about refusing to let fortune meet you soft, ignorant, or surprised. Once Machiavelli has ruled out borrowed arms, he must say what internal strength actually requires. It requires drill, bodily hardening, learned ground, rehearsed contingencies, remembered histories, and a prince who treats quiet as the time to make himself harder to overturn. Chapter 14 therefore sets the standard before the moral chapters begin. Before Machiavelli asks which praised qualities a ruler can safely afford, he insists that rule begins with serious readiness for adversity.
