Chapter 21 begins from a different ambition than the chapter before it. Chapter 20 asked what devices actually secure a prince. Chapter 21 asks what makes him esteemed. Machiavelli's answer is not quiet safety, moderation, or administrative calm. Nothing makes a prince so much esteemed, he says, as great enterprises and setting a fine example. Renown is not a mist around power. It is something built by visible action. That is why the chapter opens with Ferdinand of Aragon. Machiavelli does not admire him because he looked impressive in the abstract. He admires the way one action followed another, each large enough to command attention, each arranged so that subjects and rivals alike remained occupied by what he was doing next.

Granada is the foundation case. Ferdinand attacks it early and does so in a way that keeps the barons of Castile fixed on the war instead of on innovations in their own political relation to him. While their minds are occupied, he acquires power and authority over them. The enterprise funds armies, creates military skill, and lays the base for later greatness. Machiavelli then makes the method even clearer by noting Ferdinand's use of religion as a plea and his devotion, with what Machiavelli calls pious cruelty, to driving out the Moors. The point is not hidden hypocrisy alone. It is sequence. Under the same cloak he moves from Granada to Africa, from Africa to Italy, from Italy to France. His designs keep the minds of his people in suspense and admiration, always absorbed by the issue of the next action. Men are never given time to work steadily against him because the political field is constantly being reshaped by visible enterprise.

The chapter then narrows from international action to internal example. A prince gains esteem not only by conquering abroad, but by making his rule memorable at home. Bernabo da Milano becomes the emblem here. When anyone in civil life did something extraordinary, either good or bad, he made the reward or punishment conspicuous enough to be spoken about widely. The point is not random theatricality. It is that rule must acquire public shape. A prince should endeavor in every action to gain the reputation of being a great and remarkable man. Chapter 21 therefore treats esteem as an effect of pattern and example, not merely of results hidden inside government offices.

From there Machiavelli turns to one of the chapter's most severe claims: a prince is respected when he is either a true friend or a downright enemy. In other words, he should declare himself for one side against another rather than standing neutral. Neutrality looks cautious, but Machiavelli thinks it usually leaves the prince exposed without credit from either victor or victim. If two powerful neighbours fight and one is dangerous whichever way the outcome goes, then declaring yourself and fighting strenuously is still better than hesitation. If you stay neutral and one side wins, you fall prey to the conqueror with no claim on his gratitude and no refuge with the loser. The conqueror has no use for doubtful friends who would not help in trial, and the loser has no reason to shelter you because you would not court his fate sword in hand.

The Achaean example sharpens the point. Antiochus urged the Achaeans to remain neutral, while the Romans urged them to take up arms. The Roman reply is the chapter's anti-neutrality principle in compact form: by not interfering, you are left without favor or consideration as the guerdon of the conqueror. Machiavelli generalizes from this that the man who is not your friend will ask for your neutrality, while the one who is your friend will ask for your armed declaration. Neutrality, then, is not a middle point above politics. It is often the position most convenient to those who want to use you without owing you anything. That is why irresolute princes follow the neutral path to avoid present dangers and are generally ruined.

Machiavelli immediately complicates the lesson so it does not become empty bravado. There are cases in which the ally who wins will be powerful and potentially threatening, and other cases in which the victor will not greatly endanger you. In both, declared alliance is still generally wiser than neutrality, but prudence must notice the difference. If you can fear the conqueror afterward, open support may still buy debt and amity where neutrality buys none. If you do not especially fear either side, alliance becomes even more useful because with your aid one destroys the other and the victor remains, in some degree, at your discretion. Yet Machiavelli adds a sharp warning: never ally with one stronger than yourself in order to attack others unless necessity compels it, because if he conquers you are at his discretion instead. The Venetians' alliance with France against Milan becomes the ruinous example. The Florentines, when Pope and Spain moved on Lombardy, show the necessity case. From this the chapter draws its broader prudential rule: no government chooses perfectly safe courses. One avoids one trouble only by running into another. Prudence means distinguishing the character of troubles and choosing the lesser evil.

The last movement of the chapter widens renown beyond war and alliances. A prince should show himself a patron of ability and honor the proficient in every art. He should make citizens feel safe enough to improve possessions, open trade, and practice their callings without fearing confiscation or predatory taxation. He should reward those who honor the city or state. Then, at convenient seasons, he should entertain the people with festivals and spectacles and show regard to the guilds and societies into which city life is divided. But Machiavelli immediately adds the limit that keeps this from turning into populist softness: the prince must associate with such bodies sometimes and show courtesy and liberality while always maintaining the majesty of his rank. That last phrase protects the entire chapter from misreading. Renown is built by visible greatness, decisive alignment, public rewards, and ceremonial presence. It is not built by dissolving the distance that makes princely authority legible. That is why the next chapter can move inward from public renown to judgment in appointments. Once the prince has learned how to appear great before the city, the next test is whether his chosen secretaries prove that the greatness is real.
