Chapter 15 changes the book's method before it changes any single moral verdict. Machiavelli knows many writers have already described how princes ought to conduct themselves, and he expects to sound presumptuous because he will depart from them. His reason is usefulness. He says he wants to follow the real truth of the matter rather than the imagination of it. Many political writers, he says, have pictured republics and principalities that have never been known or seen. Machiavelli refuses that register. He will not flatter the ruler with an ideal world that does not exist. The chapter's severity begins there. The problem is not that virtue is contemptible. The problem is that imaginary virtue can become politically useless when conditions do not allow it to rule events.

That is why the chapter's next sentence matters so much: how one lives is far distant from how one ought to live. A ruler who neglects what is done for what ought to be done, Machiavelli says, works his ruin rather than his preservation. This is one of the book's cleanest breaks with moral comfort. It does not mean ideals are meaningless in every domain. It means governing takes place among human beings, pressures, ambitions, dangers, and necessities that do not wait for the world to become good first. If a prince chooses conduct as though everyone else were already behaving as they ought, he gives up judgment at the very moment judgment is most needed. Machiavelli is not admiring corruption here. He is describing the cost of pretending it is absent.

The famous consequence follows. A prince wishing to hold his own must know how to do wrong, and to use that knowledge or not according to necessity. The phrase is easy to vulgarize and important to narrow correctly. Machiavelli is not telling the ruler to love wrongdoing, or to discard all restraint, or to become impressive through wickedness. He is saying the prince must not be morally helpless when conditions require conduct that will not look pure. The chapter's standard is preservation, and preservation is judged under actual conditions. That is why necessity matters so much. It is the test that separates prudence from mere appetite. The prince must be able to depart from goodness without becoming unable to return, and he must be able to tolerate that such departures may bring blame.

Machiavelli then widens the field. All men, and especially princes, are spoken of in terms of paired qualities that bring praise or blame. Liberal or miserly. Generous or rapacious. Cruel or compassionate. Faithless or faithful. Bold or cowardly. Affable or haughty. Chaste or lascivious. Sincere or cunning. Grave or frivolous. Religious or unbelieving. Everyone would agree, he says, that it would be praiseworthy if a prince could display all the qualities counted good. But human conditions do not permit full possession or observance of them. That sentence matters because it keeps the chapter from becoming merely cynical. The problem is not only that the world is bad. It is that the ruler cannot simultaneously embody every praised trait in every situation without contradiction, weakness, or loss.

So the method of prudence becomes harsher and more exact. The prince should avoid the vices that would lose him his state. If possible, he should also avoid the others. But when that is not possible, he should feel less hesitation about incurring reproach for the faults without which the state can hardly be saved. This is the chapter's real hinge. Public blame is not the final measure. Political loss is. The hardest sentence comes at the close: some things that look like virtue would ruin a ruler if followed, while some things that look like vice bring security and prosperity. Chapter 15 does not yet tell us which cases these are. It does something more important first. It tells us that the ruler must stop asking only what sounds admirable and start asking what preserves. That question will govern the chapters on liberality, cruelty, faith, and the rest.
