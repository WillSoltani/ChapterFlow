Chapter 12 begins where the earlier principality chapters left off and asks a harsher question than it first appears to ask. Once a state has been acquired or held, what force actually keeps it standing? Machiavelli answers by returning to foundations. Good laws and good arms belong together, and he leaves laws aside because the military side has to be faced directly. The opening classification is simple: the arms of a prince are either his own, mercenary, auxiliary, or mixed. But the chapter's force comes from how quickly that classification becomes a political judgment. A prince who rests on hired force does not merely use an imperfect tool. He builds his safety on men whose stake lies elsewhere.

The first danger is in the ordinary mercenary soldier. Machiavelli's description is relentless because he wants the reader to stop confusing temporary service with real loyalty. Mercenaries are held by stipend, not by a shared fate. They can stand around a camp, appear orderly among friends, and live off the prince in times of peace, but when actual danger arrives they either disappear or collapse. That is why Machiavelli says a state based on such arms stands neither firm nor safe, and why he sharpens the point into the cruel summary that in peace one is robbed by them and in war by the enemy. The ruin of Italy proves the point for him. Mercenary troops could show form and reputation so long as the pressure remained internal and familiar. When foreigners arrived, the display broke, and Charles of France passed through Italy "with chalk in hand," as if the country were already half surrendered before swords had done their work.

The second danger is higher up, in the captain. Chapter 12 matters because Machiavelli refuses to let the prince comfort himself with the idea that better hiring solves the problem. If the mercenary captain is not skilful, the result is obvious ruin. But if he is skilful, the danger changes rather than disappearing. A capable hired commander has his own ambitions and can turn his force either against the prince who pays him or toward conquests that cease to belong to the prince's design. In other words, the prince is trapped between incompetence below and overmighty service above. Machiavelli answers the obvious objection at once. Is not every armed man dangerous? Only partly. When a prince or republic uses its own arms, command remains politically rooted. The prince goes in person, or the republic sends citizens and binds commanders by laws. Rome, Sparta, and the Swiss matter here not as ornaments of republican nostalgia, but as proof that communities armed from within remain harder to dominate than those that borrow their force from outside themselves.

The historical examples drive the point from theory into threat. Carthage was oppressed by its own mercenary soldiers. The Thebans raised Philip of Macedon to command and lost their liberty after victory. Francesco Sforza was hired by the Milanese and turned from their servant into their crusher. His father abandoned Queen Johanna and pushed her toward dependence on another king. Machiavelli's point is not that every hired captain always seizes a throne, but that the prince has accepted a relationship in which safety depends on the private calculations of someone whose greatness is no longer identical with the master's. Even the cases that seem to contradict him only help him. Florence survived some dangerous captains not because its principle was sound, but because chance interrupted their rise or redirected their ambition.

Venice makes the lesson sharper. Machiavelli says Venice did well so long as it fought with its own men at sea. The trouble deepened when it turned to land war and adopted the custom of Italy. At first, reputation and limited territory reduced the risk. Later, under Carmignuola and those who followed, the republic found itself squeezed by the very men it had trusted to enlarge it. A captain too sluggish to win quickly could still not be safely dismissed. A captain too successful might have to be feared. The result was the kind of distorted prudence that forces a state to murder its own commander in order to keep what it has gained. From such arms, Machiavelli says, conquests come slowly and losses suddenly. That line carries more than military frustration. It describes a regime that can accumulate appearances for a while but cannot secure what it accumulates.

The chapter's largest move comes when Machiavelli widens the frame from individual captains to the whole mercenary order that settled over Italy. Church territories and republics, led by men unused to arms, hired foreigners. Condottieri then reshaped the very form of war to suit their own survival and prestige. They lowered the credit of infantry, leaned on cavalry, kept numbers manageable for their pay, and reduced danger wherever they could. Night attacks disappeared. Winter campaigning disappeared. Camps went unfortified. Battles were softened by customs that preserved captains and soldiers at the expense of political reality. The point is not antiquarian detail. Machiavelli is showing what happens when the military instrument is built around the comfort and continuity of hired commanders rather than around the survival of the state. War itself becomes safer for the mercenary and more dangerous for the polity.

That is why the chapter ends not with one more anecdote but with a national verdict. Italy was not merely unlucky. It was trained into weakness by a system that detached arms from civic ownership and taught soldiers to preserve themselves rather than the countries that employed them. Chapter 12 therefore does more than warn against timid troops. It says a state that rents its danger rents its future. The prince who wants firmness cannot begin by asking only how brave the hired men look or how famous the captain sounds. He has to ask where command, stake, and risk truly sit. The next chapter tightens the screw further, because auxiliaries will prove even worse: foreign force that arrives already brave, already coherent, and already obedient to someone else.
