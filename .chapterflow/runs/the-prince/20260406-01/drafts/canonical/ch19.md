A prince can survive a surprising amount of criticism. He can be called stern, calculating, or unlovely and still remain secure. What Chapter 19 will not let him survive is a deeper change in public feeling: becoming hated or despised. That is Machiavelli's narrowing move here. He is no longer asking which virtues sound admirable in the abstract. He is ranking dangers. A ruler who avoids hatred and contempt has answered the main practical question, because those two states turn every later shock into an opening for enemies. Other reproaches may sting without breaking rule. Hatred and contempt are different. Once they harden, they change how subjects judge injury, how rivals calculate risk, and how conspiracy begins to look possible.

Machiavelli is precise about where each danger comes from. Hatred comes above all from rapacity and from violating what subjects count as theirs, especially property and women. This is why the chapter sounds less like moral sermon than political diagnosis. The prince who leaves property and honor untouched leaves most people content and only has to contend with the ambition of a few. Contempt has a different source. A ruler becomes contemptible when he looks fickle, frivolous, weak, vain, irresolute, too small for his own station. So Machiavelli pairs restraint with posture. The prince must not plunder. He must also not look easy to fool or easy to push around. His judgments should appear irrevocable. His conduct should suggest gravity and fortitude. Chapter 19 is therefore not one warning but two: do not make people feel injured, and do not make them feel you are beneath respect.

From there the chapter makes its most practical turn. Esteem is not just decoration around power. It is one of the best defenses against conspiracy. Machiavelli says a prince has two fears, one from within and one from without. External powers are checked by arms and allies. Internal danger is subtler. When affairs are disturbed, the prince must fear secret conspiracy among subjects. Yet conspiracies are weak when the people are not hostile, because the conspirator expects success only if the prince's removal will please others. If public feeling runs the other way, the plotter faces fear before the deed and fear after it. Accomplices can betray him for profit. Escape routes close. The Bentivogli case in Bologna gives the point its edge. Annibale Bentivogli was murdered, yet the people's goodwill toward his house was so strong that they rose and murdered the conspirators themselves. Machiavelli's point is not that affection is sentimental armor. It is that public goodwill alters the conspirator's arithmetic before the blade is ever drawn.

That is also why the chapter pauses over France. The kingdom is not praised only for being well ordered. It is praised for managing blame intelligently. Parliament stands between nobles and people, checking the great and protecting the lesser without making the king bear every reproach himself. From this Machiavelli draws a rule with far wider use: princes should leave affairs of reproach to others and keep grace in their own hands. The leader does not preserve himself by charming every party personally. He preserves himself by making sure necessary punishments, refusals, and restraints do not all attach directly to his own face, while visible benefits still do. This keeps another distinction alive. The prince should cherish the nobles, but never in a way that makes him hated by the people. So the chapter's politics of reputation is not mere image management. It includes institutional design, the routing of blame, and a sober sense that one body can be steadied only if another is not driven to fury.

The Roman emperors enter because they seem at first to break the rule. Some lived nobly and still died badly. Machiavelli answers by saying that emperors had a harder task than most princes: they had to manage not only nobles and people, but soldiers as a third force with their own cruelty and avarice. The people wanted peace and an unaspiring ruler. The soldiers wanted a warlike ruler bold enough to indulge their appetite. In that world, a prince could not avoid being hated by someone. The problem became which hatred mattered most and whether he could maintain authority over the body he had to satisfy. This is where the chapter grows morally dangerous and analytically sharp at once. Marcus succeeds because hereditary title and exceptional respect let him hold both orders in place. Pertinax fails because he tries to impose honest discipline on soldiers corrupted under Commodus. Here Machiavelli states one of his hardest lines: hatred can be acquired as much by good works as by bad ones. Alexander fails from the other side. His goodness is not enough because he becomes despised as effeminate and ruled by his mother. The chapter is now doing more than praising esteem. It is testing whether moral quality fits the political body it must govern.

The opposite set of emperors shows the same logic from the harsher side. Commodus, Severus, Caracalla, and Maximinus are all cruel and rapacious, yet only Severus reigns successfully. Why? Not because cruelty by itself works, and not because the chapter has abandoned its earlier warnings. Severus combines force, cunning, and renown strongly enough to keep soldiers satisfied and people awed. He knows, as Machiavelli says earlier, how to counterfeit the fox and the lion. Caracalla, by contrast, becomes hated almost universally and then commits the more immediate error of gravely injuring one of the very men around him. Commodus should have ruled easily as Marcus's son, but he throws away dignity, indulges the soldiers, stoops to theater and gladiatorial display, and so becomes both hated and despised. Maximinus, though warlike, is undone by the public indignity of his low birth, the terror of his ferocity, and the breadth of opposition that gathers against him. Different roads, same end: hatred or contempt makes rule brittle enough to break.

So Chapter 19 closes where it must, not with a single personality to imitate, but with a classification problem. A new prince cannot simply copy Marcus, because Marcus inherited a stable authority he did not have to found. Nor should every ruler copy Severus, because the violence and cunning that establish a precarious state are not the same qualities that gloriously preserve one already firm. The wiser lesson is selective extraction. Take from Severus what is necessary to found. Take from Marcus what is proper to keep. Underneath that final distinction sits the chapter's first rule still unchanged: if a prince cannot avoid every reproach, he must still avoid the conditions that make people feel injured and make him look contemptible. Once those take hold, plots multiply, loyalty thins, and even institutions or force begin to wobble. That is why the next chapter can turn to fortresses and other devices. Before asking which instruments help a prince, Machiavelli has first clarified which public feelings make instruments too weak to save him.
