Chapter 23 starts from a problem that has no easy cure. Princes need truth badly because courts fill themselves with flatterers. Yet the obvious remedy fails. If the prince lets everyone tell him the truth whenever they like, respect for him falls away. Machiavelli's point is not that truth is dangerous in itself. It is that truth around power has to be governed. Too little of it leaves the prince drugged by flattery. Too much of it, from every direction and on every subject, lowers authority and turns rule into a public contest of voices.

That is why he recommends what he calls a third course. The prince should choose wise men in his state and give only them the liberty to speak the truth to him, and then only about the things he inquires into. This is narrower than a culture of general candor and broader than isolation. The prince is supposed to question them upon everything, listen to their opinions, and afterwards form his own conclusions. Truth is invited, but not allowed to become a flood. The counsellors know that freer speech within this gate is preferred, yet outside the gate the prince listens to no one and remains steadfast in what he resolves. Machiavelli is building a speech regime, not merely praising honesty.

The reason for the gate is not only to keep flatterers out. It is also to keep the prince from becoming contemptible through instability. A ruler who is constantly altered by varying opinions does not look open-minded. He looks weak, reversible, and impossible to rely on. Chapter 23 says the prince who does not keep counsel in order is either overthrown by flatterers or changed so often that he falls into contempt. The danger lies at both edges. If the prince cannot hear truth, flatterers steer him. If he hears every voice equally, he starts reacting instead of ruling. Steadfastness, in this chapter, is not stubborn deafness. It is judgment that remains judgment after listening.

The example of Maximilian shows the opposite failure. Fra Luca says he consulted no one, yet never got his own way in anything. At first that sounds paradoxical. Machiavelli explains it by joining secrecy to pliancy. Maximilian did not communicate his designs or receive opinions on them; but once the plans became visible in action, the men around him obstructed them, and he, being pliant, abandoned what he had begun. So he became both closed and weak at once. One day he did a thing, the next day he undid it, and no one could rely on his resolutions. The lesson is precise. Silence alone does not protect the prince from influence. If his judgment is not disciplined beforehand, pressure arrives later and changes him in public.

Machiavelli therefore restates the rule more exactly. The prince should always take counsel, but only when he wishes and not when others wish. He should discourage unsolicited advice, yet be a constant inquirer and a patient listener when he has asked. If someone has not told him the truth, he should let his anger be felt. The prince is not passive here. He summons truth, tests it, and signals that concealment is dangerous. The arrangement depends on initiative remaining with him. Counsel is not supposed to seize the agenda. It is supposed to answer it.

The chapter ends by reversing a flattering illusion about advisers themselves. Some think a prince seems wise only because of the good advisers around him. Machiavelli says that is backward. An unwise prince will never take good advice well, unless he surrenders everything to one prudent man, who would soon take away his state. If he instead takes counsel from many, he will not unite their counsels, will not see through their interests, and will not know how to control them. Men, Machiavelli adds, are kept honest by constraint. So good counsels, wherever they come from, are born of the wisdom of the prince. Chapter 23 is therefore less about finding pure advisers than about becoming the kind of ruler who can invite truth without being ruled by the people who bring it. From there the book can move into Chapter 24, where lost states become the hard evidence of what happens when princes fail these disciplines.
