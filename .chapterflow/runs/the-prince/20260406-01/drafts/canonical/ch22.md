A prince can reveal himself before he gives a speech, issues an order, or appears in triumph. People look first at who is nearest to him. Chapter 22 begins from that hard social fact. The first opinion formed of a prince and of his understanding comes from the men around him. If they are capable and faithful, the prince appears wise because he recognized the capable and kept them faithful. If they are not, the prince has already exposed a prime error. Machiavelli is not talking about private chemistry or vague trust. He is making appointments into a public diagnostic. A ruler is judged not only by what he says, but by the quality and alignment of the people through whom he acts.

That is why the chapter turns so quickly to Pandolfo Petrucci and Antonio da Venafro. Anyone who knew Venafro as Pandolfo's servant would, Machiavelli says, have judged Pandolfo clever for having him. The example matters because it leads into the chapter's deeper distinction among three classes of intellect. One mind comprehends by itself. Another appreciates what others have comprehended. A third does neither. The first is most excellent, the second is good, the third is useless. The point is crucial. A prince does not need to originate every best idea in the room in order to rule wisely. If he is not first-rank, he can still be second-rank, which means he can hear, weigh, distinguish, and judge. Pandolfo may not have been the man who discovered everything alone, but by choosing Venafro he showed that he could recognize excellence when he saw it.

Machiavelli presses the point harder in the next step. The prince who can recognize the good and the bad in his servant, praise the one, and correct the other, keeps the servant from hoping to deceive him. That is the real advantage of second-rank intelligence. It is not passive borrowing. It is active discrimination. A clever secretary becomes proof of the prince's judgment only when the prince can still evaluate him, reward what serves the state, and check what drifts. Without that power of judgment, a gifted subordinate stops being evidence of wisdom and becomes evidence that the ruler is being carried along by someone else's mind. Chapter 22 is therefore less interested in raw brilliance than in whether the prince can keep brilliance politically subordinate.

From there the chapter offers what it calls a test that never fails. Watch whether the servant thinks more of his own interests than of yours and seeks inwardly his own profit in everything. If he does, he will never make a good servant, nor will you ever be able to trust him. The line is harsher than ordinary advice about ambition. Machiavelli is saying that the office itself is corrupted when the man holding another's state in his hands thinks first of himself. Talent does not rescue that defect. Fluency, speed, strategic charm, and even practical success are secondary if the underlying motion is self-advancement. The chapter's standard is alignment. A prince may use a capable servant, but he cannot safely rely on one whose center of gravity lies elsewhere.

The final paragraph turns the burden back onto the prince. Good service is not preserved by suspicion alone. The prince must study the servant, honour him, enrich him, do him kindnesses, and share with him honours and cares. This is not soft ornament. It is structure. A capable servant stays faithful when service is made valuable and visible, when he is drawn close enough to feel both reward and responsibility. Shared cares matter as much as honours because they make the servant carry part of the prince's burden rather than merely enjoy proximity to power. Machiavelli is describing a relationship in which trust is cultivated by material and political arrangements, not by hope that a good man will remain good without attention.

Yet the chapter does not let that arrangement become sentimental. The servant must also see that he cannot stand alone. Many honours should not make him desire more, many riches should not make him wish for more, and many cares should make him dread chances. That last move is the chapter's hardest edge. The prince binds a good servant not only by esteem but by dependence. The result, when both sides are rightly disposed, is mutual trust. When it fails, the end is disastrous for one or the other. That close prepares the next chapter exactly because it leaves counsel under tension. Chapter 22 shows how a prince chooses and keeps honest the people nearest him. Chapter 23 will ask what happens when speech around the prince is no longer disciplined by judgment and alignment, but corrupted by flatterers.
