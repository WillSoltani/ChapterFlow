Chapter 10 sounds at first like a pause for measurement, but it changes the book's angle in a serious way. Machiavelli has spent earlier chapters naming kinds of principalities and the routes by which rulers rise. Here he asks a harder question: when pressure comes, can the prince support himself with his own resources, or does he always need the assistance of others? That is not a small administrative detail. It is a test of whether the principality can stand on its own legs in danger. The chapter's distinction is severe. Some rulers have enough men or money to raise a sufficient army and meet an attack in the field. Others cannot show themselves openly against the enemy and must defend themselves by sheltering behind walls. Machiavelli does not treat those as minor variations. They are different strategic conditions, and a prince who confuses them will spend his force in the wrong place.

Once that line is drawn, the chapter becomes more concrete than the word strength first suggests. If a prince belongs to the second kind, the advice is not to attempt everything at once. He should provision and fortify his town and not on any account defend the whole country. The point is easy to resist because it sounds like surrendering the open ground. Machiavelli means the opposite. A ruler who cannot fight in the field should not pretend otherwise by dissipating energy across every exposed perimeter. Strength in that case lies in making the defended core costly to attack and durable enough to endure a siege. Yet walls alone are not enough. Machiavelli adds a condition that carries political weight: the town must be well fortified, and the prince must not be hated by his people. A fortified city governed by odium is not the same thing as a strong city. The chapter binds military difficulty and civic feeling together from the start.

That is why the German cities matter so much. Machiavelli presents them as visibly difficult places to assault: little surrounding territory, proper ditches and walls, sufficient artillery, and public stores enough for a year's eating, drinking, and firing. But the example does not stop at masonry and stockpiles. These cities also keep the people quiet and without loss to the state by providing work in the labours that sustain the city's life and strength. They honor military exercises and maintain ordinances to uphold them. The lesson is broader than hardware. A defensible city is organized so that endurance looks plausible to its own inhabitants as well as to the enemy. The attacker sees tedious difficulty. The citizens see that the city has not merely been enclosed but prepared. Machiavelli is precise here: visible difficulty discourages attack because men are adverse to enterprises whose obstacles can already be seen.

He then stages the most obvious objection. What if citizens own property outside the city and watch it burn? Would not long siege and self-interest make them forget their prince? Machiavelli does not dismiss the force of that question. He answers by shifting from stores and walls to the management of minds under damage. A powerful and courageous prince, he says, can overcome this difficulty by giving hope that the evil will not be long, by awakening fear of the enemy's cruelty, and by preserving himself adroitly from subjects who are too bold. None of that erases the loss. The important point is the timing. The enemy will naturally burn and ruin the country at the beginning, when spirits are still hot and ready for defence. That means the injury arrives before later discouragement fully settles in. The prince should therefore hesitate less, not more, because the damage that seemed likely to dissolve loyalty may, after the first shock, change the emotional balance in the opposite direction.

This is the chapter's strangest and sharpest turn. Once the ills are already incurred and there is no remedy, citizens may become more ready to unite with their prince because their houses and possessions were ruined in his defence. Machiavelli explains that human beings are bound not only by benefits they receive but also by benefits they confer. Loss borne for a common defence can therefore produce a harder kind of attachment than comfort ever would. That claim only works, however, if the prince does not fail to support and defend them. He cannot ask citizens to interpret ruin as shared sacrifice while leaving them abandoned inside the walls. The chapter's final image of steadfast minds depends on practical reciprocity: the ruler protects the city, and the citizens come to see their losses as part of a defence that still holds them together.

So Chapter 10 is not a generic praise of fortresses, nor a sentimental speech about solidarity under siege. It is a diagnostic chapter about matching political behavior to real capacity. If you can stand in the field, that is one condition. If you cannot, then prudence lies in fortifying the town, stocking it, organizing its work, deterring attack through visible difficulty, avoiding hatred, and holding the people steady when the outer country burns. Strength is measured by what kind of attack you can survive without pretending to be stronger than you are. That makes the next chapter feel even stranger, because ecclesiastical principalities will appear to sit outside this whole ordinary scale of resources, walls, and civic endurance.
