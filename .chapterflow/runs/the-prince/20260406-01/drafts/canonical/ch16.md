Chapter 16 is the first place where Machiavelli's method from Chapter 15 cuts directly across an admired virtue. It would be good, he says, to be reputed liberal. He does not begin by denying that. The problem emerges the moment the prince tries to keep that reputation alive in public. Liberality that is exercised properly may not become known, and liberality that is made visible enough to secure the name becomes expensive. Once the ruler is determined to seem liberal, he cannot avoid magnificence. He spends to maintain the reputation, and the reputation starts to govern him. That is the chapter's first hard move. Generosity is not judged by moral beauty alone, but by the political machinery required to sustain the appearance of it.

From there the cost escalates quickly. A prince who keeps buying the name of liberal, Machiavelli says, consumes his property and is then forced to burden his people, tax them, and do whatever he can to get money. The result is not stable love. It is odium. He becomes poor and little valued, having offended many and rewarded few. Then comes the humiliating turn. Once he recognizes the danger and tries to pull back, he immediately runs into the reproach of being mean. So the generous reputation first empties him and then stains him anyway. The chapter is not arguing that rulers should enjoy stinginess. It is arguing that generosity pursued as a public identity can trap an established prince between poverty and hatred.

That is why Machiavelli says a wise prince should not fear a reputation for being mean. In time, he says, such a prince will be more considered than if liberal, because his revenues are enough, he can defend himself, and he can undertake enterprises without burdening his people. This is one of the chapter's sharpest reversals. Meanness sounds like a vice, yet it can spare the public from the extractions that visible liberality eventually requires. Machiavelli compresses the point in a memorable way: the ruler becomes liberal toward the numberless people from whom he does not take, and mean toward the few to whom he does not give. The argument is not sentimental. It is distributive and political. Better reproach from a few than hatred from many whose resources you have consumed.

Recent examples then harden the claim. Machiavelli says the great deeds of his own time were done by rulers considered mean, while the others failed. Pope Julius II benefited from a reputation for liberality on the way to the papacy, but once he held power and made war, he did not try to keep that reputation up by extraordinary taxes on his subjects. The present King of Spain, Machiavelli says, would not have undertaken or conquered so much if he had been reputed liberal. The point is not that these rulers were morally noble misers. It is that established rule depends on resources, defence, and endurance more than on the admired image of open-handedness.

Still, Machiavelli does not flatten every case into one rule. He distinguishes sharply between a prince already in power and one trying to become one. If you are a prince in fact, liberality is dangerous. If you are on the way to becoming one, it may be very necessary to be considered liberal. Caesar fits the second case. A man still acquiring supremacy may need largeness for recruitment, loyalty, or momentum in a way an established ruler does not. So Chapter 16 is not a sermon on smallness. It is a ranking of contexts. The same generosity that helps a climber may ruin a ruler who has already climbed and now has to preserve what he holds.

The final distinction is even sharper. A prince may spend his own goods or those of others. If he spends his own or his subjects' goods, he ought to be sparing. If he spends others' goods, he should not neglect any opportunity for liberality. That is why Cyrus, Caesar, and Alexander can appear magnificently generous. They are squandering what is not theirs in the narrow sense Machiavelli cares about here, and that spending adds to their reputation rather than injuring their capacity. The established ruler who squanders his own base cannot do the same safely. The close gathers the whole argument into one warning: liberality wastes quickly, and the prince becomes either poor and despised or, trying to avoid poverty, rapacious and hated. Better, then, a reputation for meanness that brings reproach without hatred than a pursuit of liberality that ends in rapacity with hatred. Chapter 16 does not ask whether generosity is lovely. It asks who pays for it, and what that payment does to rule.
