Chapter 24 begins by overturning an easy assumption about political safety. You might think long possession is safer than recent acquisition, and that a ruler born into power stands on firmer ground than one who has only just taken hold of a state. Machiavelli says the opposite can be true. A new prince, precisely because he is watched so closely, can become more secure and fixed than a hereditary ruler if his actions are seen to be able. That opening matters because it shifts legitimacy away from blood and toward visible present performance. Men, he says, are attracted more by the present than by the past. If the present government works, if the prince fails them not in other things, they defend him vigorously. Chapter 24 therefore is not yet about collapse. It first explains why collapse is especially shameful. The prince who starts new and still wins loyalty has achieved something difficult and visible. The prince who inherits and still loses exposes weakness where habit should have made things easier.

From there Machiavelli sharpens the contrast into a moral and political verdict. A new prince who establishes a principality and strengthens it with good laws, good arms, good allies, and good example wins what he calls double glory. He has not merely held what he found. He has founded, adorned, and secured it. The hereditary prince who loses his state suffers double disgrace for the same reason in reverse. He did not fall from a precarious beginning. He fell from an inherited position that should have given him time, memory, and established obedience. That is why the chapter feels less like fresh instruction than like judgment after the fact. Machiavelli is gathering the book's previous lessons and saying: if rulers still lost their states, it was not because the standards were impossible. It was because they neglected them while possession made them sleepy. The double-glory and double-disgrace contrast also keeps another idea in view. Political honor does not belong automatically to old blood. It follows the ruler who actually does the work of securing the state.

That work appears in the chapter as a concrete diagnosis of failure. When Machiavelli looks at the Italian seigniors who lost their states, he does not offer a mysterious explanation. He says they share one common defect in regard to arms. Then he adds the political defects that go with military weakness: either the people were hostile, or, if the people were friendly, the ruler did not know how to secure the nobles. The chapter is thin, but the compression is deliberate. Earlier parts of the book have already argued over arms, popular goodwill, and elite management. Here those earlier themes are gathered into a short retrospective formula. A state that can keep an army in the field, and that does not suffer those political defects, cannot simply be brushed away. So Machiavelli is not saying that all rulers who fall deserved it in some moral sense. He is saying that the structure of their weakness can usually be named. The state was undermanned, politically unsupported, or both. Fortune may decide timing, but it does not have to invent the weakness from nothing.

Philip of Macedon is introduced to prevent a predictable excuse. A failed ruler might say that the enemy was too large, the coalition too strong, the territory too small. Machiavelli points to Philip, not Alexander's father but the Philip defeated by Titus Quintius, as proof that limited scale is not enough to explain political loss. Philip did not possess great territory compared with Rome and Greece, yet he was warlike, knew how to attract the people, and secured the nobles. Because of that, he sustained war for many years and, even after losing some cities, still retained the kingdom. The example answers two evasions at once. First, it blocks the excuse that only vast resources make endurance possible. Second, it blocks the excuse that arms alone are sufficient. Philip is not praised simply for fighting. He is praised for fighting as a ruler who held together the political supports that let fighting continue. The people and the nobles are not secondary decorations around military power. They are part of the reason a ruler can absorb losses without losing everything.

Only after setting that standard does Machiavelli turn directly on the princes who failed. Do not accuse fortune, he says, for the loss of principalities held for many years. Accuse sloth. The word is harsh because the offense happened before the emergency. In quiet times they never thought there could be a change. Like most people, they made no provision in the calm against the tempest. That line is the chapter's center of gravity. The real indictment is not panic under pressure, though panic comes later. It is negligence during peace. Once bad times arrived, these rulers thought of flight instead of defense. Worse, they hoped the people, once disgusted with the insolence of conquerors, would recall them. Machiavelli does not deny that this sometimes happens. He admits it may be good when other remedies fail. But the phrase that matters is the one that follows: it is very bad to have neglected all other expedients for that. The rulers he condemns are not guilty because restoration never occurs. They are guilty because they treated hoped-for restoration as though it were a plan.

That is why the chapter ends by cutting away every consoling version of rescue. You would never want to fall, Machiavelli says, because you trusted that someone later would restore you. Even if such deliverance arrives, it is not secure when it does not depend on yourself. Only those defenses are reliable, certain, and durable that depend on yourself and your valour. This is not yet the full argument about fortune that comes next. It is the disciplinary clearing of the ground before that argument. Chapter 24 strips rulers of the excuse that contingency alone destroyed them. It says that what looked like a sudden fall was often the revelation of earlier laziness, weak arms, hostile people, unsecured nobles, and a mind that mistook possession for permanence. It also preserves a harder hope. New rulers are not doomed by novelty. They can bind people more tightly than ancient blood if they govern ably in the present. The disgrace belongs not to the ruler who begins in difficulty, but to the one who inherits advantages, neglects the work of preserving them, and then calls the collapse fortune. From there the book can turn, in Chapter 25, to the question that remains once excuses have been stripped away: what can fortune actually do, and what can prepared human action still resist?
