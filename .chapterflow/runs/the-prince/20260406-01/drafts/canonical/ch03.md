The first trap in a mixed principality is that the new ruler often arrives on a wave of welcome. People change rulers hoping things will improve. That hope makes conquest look easier than it is, because the same people who help unseat the old order are judging the new one almost at once. Machiavelli's twist is that the verdict turns quickly. Experience runs ahead of hope. The new prince brings soldiers, obligations, disruptions, and the ordinary burdens of possession, so the people who thought they were moving upward may feel they have gone from bad to worse. Chapter 2 was about the safety a ruler inherits when habit already leans toward him. Here the problem is reversed. The acquired territory may be attached to his old state, but its memories, grievances, and expectations do not become his just because he has entered.

That is why mixed acquisition begins in a double bind. The ruler has enemies in those he injured by seizing the territory, yet he cannot fully satisfy the allies who opened the gates for him either. They expected a better bargain than rule can actually deliver, and he cannot crush them as easily because he is indebted to them. Armed force does not solve this. Machiavelli insists that even a strong prince entering a province needs the goodwill of the natives. Louis XII in Milan is the proof. He took the city quickly and lost it quickly because those who had welcomed him discovered that the reality of the new rule did not match their hopes of benefit. The speed of the reversal matters. In a mixed principality, disappointment is not a later public-relations problem. It is part of the structure of entry itself.

Machiavelli then adds a wrinkle that keeps the argument sharp. A rebellious province taken a second time is not always so lightly lost, because rebellion teaches the new ruler where the state is weak. He can punish delinquents, identify suspects, and strengthen the exposed points. France lost Milan the first time when Lodovico could stir the borders. To drive the French out the second time, far more had to be mobilized. Even that detail serves a larger division. Mixed dominions do not all behave the same way. Some are of the same country and language as the old state. Others differ in language, customs, or laws. The rest of the chapter depends on refusing to blur those two cases together.

If the annexed territory resembles the older principality, the path to stability is almost plain. Machiavelli points to places like Brittany, Burgundy, Gascony, and Normandy, territories tied to France long enough that their customs can live together. In that case the new ruler does not need to recast the whole order. He needs to extinguish the family of the former prince and leave laws and taxes alone. Those two moves work together. Eliminate the living line around which memory can rally, but keep everyday conditions steady enough that the annexed population is not reminded at every turn that conquest has changed their lives. When the territory has not been used to ruling itself and its customs are near the old state's customs, minimal disturbance lets it settle into one body with the existing dominion.

The harder case begins when the conquered territory is unlike the old one. Here Machiavelli's first remedy is personal presence. The ruler should go and reside there. He gives the Turk in Greece as proof that even otherwise sensible measures would not have held such a state without settlement on the spot. Residence matters because disorders are easiest to remedy when they are still small. If the prince is present, he sees them as they spring up. If he governs from afar, he hears of them only after they have swollen. Presence also restrains official abuse. The country is not pillaged so easily by intermediaries, subjects have prompt recourse to the prince, and outside attackers face a ruler who is already embedded rather than one who must return to a crisis after the fact. Mixed rule, in other words, cannot be safely outsourced to distance.

If the prince will not reside, Machiavelli prefers colonies planted in key places over heavy garrisons. Colonies are cheaper, more faithful, and politically narrower in their damage. They dispossess only a minority, and that minority is left poor and scattered rather than collectively dangerous. Garrisons do the opposite. They turn the entire acquisition into a cost, force the whole territory to live under shifting military hardship, and make nearly everyone feel the weight of conquest. That is why the chapter delivers its most notorious sentence here. Men, Machiavelli says, should be either well treated or crushed, because they avenge lighter injuries and cannot avenge the graver ones. The point is brutal, but it follows the chapter's logic exactly. In mixed acquisition, partial injury teaches resentment without removing the injured party's capacity to answer it.

Yet the ruler's problem is not only inside the annexed territory. He also has to manage the surrounding powers. In a differing country he should become the head and defender of the lesser neighbors, weaken the stronger, and keep any foreign equal from getting a foothold. Discontent, ambition, and fear will always invite such an outsider in. The Romans in Greece are Machiavelli's master example because they combined internal settlement with external sequencing. They kept the Achaeans and Aetolians friendly without letting them grow too strong, humbled Macedonia, drove out Antiochus, and refused to let Philip bargain his way into tolerated strength. What makes Rome admirable here is not simple aggression. It is foresight. Machiavelli's fever analogy explains why: the evils hardest to detect at first are easiest to cure then, but once they are obvious to everyone they are harder to remedy. So war deferred is not war avoided. It is trouble postponed to somebody else's advantage.

All of this returns in the long case against Louis XII. Machiavelli does not deny that Louis had an opening in Italy. The Venetians brought him in, Lombardy gave him a foothold, and a ring of weaker powers moved toward him for protection. What condemns him is that he used the opening to rearrange the whole field against himself. He strengthened the Church by helping Alexander into the Romagna. He brought Spain into Naples. He did not settle in the country. He did not send colonies. He weakened minor powers that might have depended on him, and then, after taking those earlier steps, helped destroy the Venetians, who might otherwise have blocked further designs on Lombardy. The errors cascade because each one manufactures a stronger rival or a safer shelter for malcontents. That is why the closing rule reaches beyond Louis. Whoever causes another power to become powerful is usually ruined by that very act. Chapter 4 turns that lesson into a sharper puzzle. If mixed acquisitions breed so much aftershock, why did Alexander's successors inherit Darius's kingdom without facing the same rush of rebellion?
