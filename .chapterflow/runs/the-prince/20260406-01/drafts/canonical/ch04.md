Alexander dies before Asia has had much time to settle, and the obvious expectation is trouble. A conqueror has moved fast, the empire is still new in his hands, and the man who bound it together is gone. If the previous chapter showed how unstable acquisitions can be, why does this one large acquisition not shatter at once? Machiavelli answers by shifting attention away from the glamour of conquest and toward the inner shape of the conquered state. The puzzle is not simply why Alexander won. It is why his successors inherited so little resistance once he was gone.

His first move is to divide principalities into two kinds. Some are ruled by a prince through servants who govern only by his favor and permission. Others are ruled by a prince alongside barons whose standing rests on antiquity of blood, their own states, and the natural affection of their own subjects. That difference sounds like a detail about rank, but it changes the whole politics of takeover. In the first kind, nobody in the country stands above the ruler in any independent way. In the second, power is already distributed through people who carry real loyalty of their own. Before asking whether a kingdom can be taken, Machiavelli asks who inside it can help a foreign entrant, who can rally resistance later, and where political credit actually lives.

That is why the Turk becomes the model of the first type. The realm is unified under one lord, and the officials beneath him do not stand on inherited local authority. They can be replaced at will, and even if one were corrupted, he could not bring a whole people with him. So the kingdom is hard to seize. An invader cannot expect some powerful intermediary to open the door for him. He has to meet a united front and rely on his own force in the field. Yet the same structure creates the opposite condition after victory. If the prince is overthrown, his armies broken, and his family removed, there is no second rank of beloved lords ready to claim the country. What made entry difficult now makes possession comparatively secure, because the conqueror has cut away the one line around which the old obedience gathered.

The kingdom of France shows the contrary. There the ruler stands among ancient lords whose own subjects know them and are attached to them. That arrangement makes entry easier for an outsider, not because the country is weak in some simple sense, but because a dissatisfied baron can help crack it open. Somebody inside the regime can invite change, and the invader can use those divisions to gain access. But once the new ruler has entered, the victory begins to sour. The helpers expect reward. The defeated do not disappear. Even killing the old princely family does not solve the problem, because the remaining lords become heads of fresh movements. In other words, the same plurality that makes conquest possible also preserves the materials for later revolt. Easy entrance is already a warning that keeping the state will be harder than taking it.

Now the opening puzzle sharpens. Darius's empire resembled the first kind, not the second. Alexander did not inherit a country stitched together by rival hereditary barons who could survive the fall of the crown and reassemble resistance under new banners. He defeated Darius in the field, took the country, and once Darius was killed the political structure underneath did not generate a broad rebellion on its own. Machiavelli even adds a condition that keeps the claim precise: if Alexander's successors had remained united, they could have held Asia securely and at ease. Their real difficulties came from their own ambitions, not from the empire's internal barons rising in remembered allegiance. That point quietly pulls the chapter away from hero worship. Alexander's successors did not enjoy calm only because they were impressive men. They benefited from the kind of empire they had inherited.

The Roman comparison widens the rule without making it vague. Rome faced repeated rebellions in Spain, France, and Greece because those lands contained many principalities whose memory endured. As long as the memory of those political bodies remained alive, Roman possession stayed insecure. Only with time and the long continuance of empire did that memory pass away enough for rule to settle. The final sentence therefore lands as a correction to a lazy habit of explanation. Stability after conquest is not measured simply by the conqueror's abundance of ability, nor failure by his lack of it. Much depends on whether the subject state was uniform under one ruling line or divided among multiple living centers of authority. That leaves a harder problem waiting at the edge of the page. If many lords can preserve a dangerous memory after conquest, what happens when the thing remembered is not a set of barons but freedom itself?
