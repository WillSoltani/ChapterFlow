Not every prince arrives by armies, inheritance, or massacre. Chapter 9 opens on a quieter but no less political route: a leading citizen becomes prince through the favor of fellow citizens. Machiavelli calls this a civil principality, and the word matters because it shifts attention from conquest to conflict inside the city itself. He says every city contains two opposed desires. The people do not wish to be ruled and oppressed by the nobles. The nobles wish to rule and oppress the people. Out of that pressure can come principality, self-government, or anarchy. So the real question is not simply how a prince gains support. It is which side's desire placed him where he stands.

That source of rise immediately shapes the difficulty of rule. A prince may be raised by the nobles when they need a figure under whose shadow they can vent their ambition, or by the people when they need protection against those same nobles. Machiavelli thinks the second route is safer. The prince raised by nobles finds himself surrounded by men who consider themselves his equals. That means he cannot rule or manage them freely. The prince raised by the people stands more alone and finds fewer around him who resist obedience from the start. The point is not romance about the crowd. It is structural. Noble support is dangerous because it comes attached to rival claims of status and entitlement.

Machiavelli presses the contrast further by asking what each side really wants. The nobles wish to oppress. The people wish only not to be oppressed. For that reason the people are easier to satisfy. A prince can never feel secure against a hostile people because they are too many, but the danger they pose is chiefly abandonment. Hostile nobles are fewer, yet worse. They not only abandon. They also rise, intrigue, and save themselves in advance by attaching to whoever seems likely to prevail. The prince can dispense with one set of nobles and make another, but he cannot dismiss and replace the people in the same way. Chapter 9's hard claim is therefore that popular friendliness is not optional softness. It is the core of security in adversity.

The chapter then refines its picture of the nobles. Some bind themselves entirely to the prince's fortune. If they are not rapacious, they should be honored and loved. Others do not bind themselves. Even that group has to be split in two. Some fail from weakness or want of courage; those can still be used, especially when they give good counsel, because they will not endanger the prince in hard times. The more dangerous nobles are those who refuse to bind themselves because they are thinking of their own advancement before the prince's safety. Machiavelli says such men should be feared like open enemies, because in adversity they help to ruin the ruler. This is one of the chapter's most practical turns: elite allies are not a single block, and the prince has to learn which of them are merely limited and which are future traitors.

From there Machiavelli gives the rule that organizes the chapter. A prince raised by the people must keep them friendly. A prince raised by nobles must, above all things, seek to win the people over. He can often do this by taking them under his protection, because benefits received from someone once expected to do harm bind more tightly than benefits received from an expected friend. That is why popular favor can become stronger after conversion than it was at first. Machiavelli is careful not to reduce this to a fixed recipe. Circumstances differ. But the underlying point does not change: if the people are not friendly, the prince has no security in adversity.

Nabis helps Machiavelli answer the proverb that he who builds on the people builds on mud. Machiavelli says that proverb is true only when a private citizen imagines the people will rescue him against enemies or magistrates. It is not true of a prince who can command, keeps courage in adversity, and knows how to sustain the people's confidence. Such a ruler will not find himself deceived in them. That claim matters because it keeps the chapter from turning popular support into mere sentiment. The people are not magic. Their support holds when the prince can actually rule and when they see their protection tied to the state's survival.

The final warning deepens the argument. Civil principalities become fragile when they pass from a more ordinary civic order toward a more absolute one through magistrates rather than through the prince's own direct command. In calm times, everyone promises loyalty. In troubled times, few can be trusted. Citizens accustomed to obeying magistrates are not suddenly ready to obey the prince personally amid confusion. So a wise prince must build things in such a way that citizens always, in every sort of circumstance, have need of the state and of him. That is the chapter's final measure of political success. Not affection alone. Not elite backing alone. Durable need. It also prepares the next step in the book, because once the source of support has been clarified, Machiavelli can ask in Chapter 10 how to measure whether a principality is truly strong enough to stand under pressure.
