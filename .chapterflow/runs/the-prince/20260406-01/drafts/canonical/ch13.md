Chapter 13 does not soften the military warning that Chapter 12 has just delivered. It intensifies it. If mercenaries are dangerous because they are hired and loosely tied to you, auxiliaries are worse because they are not even loosely yours. They come already formed, already obedient, and already attached to another prince. That is why Machiavelli opens the chapter with a trap that is almost too neat to escape: if such forces lose, you are undone; if they win, you are their captive. The point is not merely that foreign help may disappoint. It is that even successful foreign help can transfer the center of power away from the prince who requested it.

Pope Julius gives Machiavelli the immediate case. Having had poor proof of mercenaries against Ferrara, he turned to Ferdinand of Spain for armed help. The hazard is visible at once. Julius put his enterprise into another ruler's hands. Fortune saved him from the full consequences only because events broke in an unexpected third direction: his auxiliaries were routed, other forces intervened, and he escaped becoming either his enemies' prisoner or his allies' dependent. Machiavelli adds the Florentines, who invited French force against Pisa, and the Emperor of Constantinople, who invited Turks into Greece and found that the invited strength did not leave. The pattern is clear. When the force belongs to another command, the prince is not borrowing muscle in the same way he borrows a paid captain. He is inviting in a completed instrument that can solve the immediate problem only by making the borrower secondary.

That is why Machiavelli says auxiliaries are more hazardous than mercenaries. Mercenaries are scattered, slower to turn, and held together by a relation you at least initiated and pay for. Auxiliaries arrive united and obedient to someone else. Their ruin is ready made. If they are weak, they expose you immediately. If they are strong, they win on terms that leave you subordinate. This is one of the book's cleanest statements of political realism: a wise prince would rather lose with his own arms than conquer with the arms of others, because what looks like victory under another's force is not a real victory. The chapter is not glorifying self-sacrifice. It is insisting that command and security cannot be separated.

Cesare Borgia becomes the proof by progression. The duke entered Romagna with French auxiliaries. Then he turned to mercenaries in the Orsini and Vitelli. Then he discarded both and relied on his own men. Machiavelli does not tell this sequence as a biography flourish. He uses it to show how danger declines as force becomes internally mastered. The duke's reputation rose with each step. He was never more esteemed than when observers saw that he was complete master of his own forces. That line matters because Chapter 13 is about more than survival. It is also about public judgment. A prince who depends on foreign or hired force may look active, but he does not look whole.

The chapter then compresses its principle through two memorable images. Hiero of Syracuse recognized that mercenary soldiers like the Italian condottieri were useless, so he cut them away and fought with his own. David rejects Saul's armor because another man's weapons do not fit his body or his way of fighting. Machiavelli turns that biblical image into one of his hardest political formulas: the arms of others either fall from your back, weigh you down, or bind you fast. Each possibility names a different mode of dependence. Foreign strength may fail you at the moment of need. It may burden your own action with habits that are not yours. Or it may work so effectively that it leaves you restrained by the very force that seemed to rescue you.

France lets Machiavelli insert a middle case. Charles VII armed the kingdom on its own foundations and made it formidable. Louis then reduced that independence by abolishing infantry and relying on the Swiss. The result was a mixed force: better than pure mercenary or auxiliary reliance, but still inferior to one's own. Here the chapter resists false neatness. Mixed arrangements are not nonsense. They are simply unstable resting places. A state that has trained itself to need someone else's excellence has already transferred part of its vigour outward. Apparent early success hides poison that becomes visible later, just as the Roman Empire began to decline once the Goths were enlisted and the strength that had raised Rome started passing away to others.

That is why the chapter closes with one of Machiavelli's broadest rules. No principality is secure without its own forces, meaning forces composed of subjects, citizens, or dependents. Power not founded on its own strength is uncertain and unstable because adversity exposes who really carries it. Chapter 13 therefore finishes the classification that Chapter 12 began. Mercenaries are dangerous because they are rented. Auxiliaries are worse because they are coherent and commanded elsewhere. Mixed forces are a compromise that still leaks strength. Only one's own arms join obedience, stake, and command in the same body. Once that has been established, the next chapter can turn from bad military dependence to the prince's continuing obligation to study the art of war itself.
