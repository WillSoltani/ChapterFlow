A city can look settled and still be waiting for its next chance. It may pay tribute, keep some of its old laws, and even let a new ruler install a friendly inner circle, yet none of that proves the deeper problem has gone away. Machiavelli opens with three possible ways to hold a place used to living under its own laws and in freedom: ruin it, reside there in person, or let it keep its laws while drawing tribute through an oligarchy that depends on your favor. The list sounds at first like a menu of alternatives. As the argument moves, it becomes clear that the softer option is also the one most likely to deceive the conqueror about what remains alive underneath.

The third course has an obvious appeal. A local oligarchy created by the prince knows it cannot stand without his friendship and interest, so it has reason to support him. Rule through the city's own citizens can seem cleaner than permanent occupation. It looks cheaper, less violent, and more intelligent than breaking everything apart. But the chapter refuses to let that appearance stand by itself. A city accustomed to freedom does not merely possess administrative habits that can be reused under new management. It remembers ruling itself. Its laws and privileges are bound up with a prior identity, and leaving them in place can preserve the very language in which resistance will later gather. That is why the mild arrangement is not automatically the prudent one. It may leave the conqueror relying on people whose authority remains local while the city's deepest loyalty remains elsewhere.

Sparta and Rome test that danger. The Spartans held Athens and Thebes by establishing oligarchies there, yet they lost them. The Romans, by contrast, dismantled Capua, Carthage, and Numantia and did not lose them. More pointedly, Rome also tried the Spartan route in Greece, keeping cities free and permitting their laws, and did not succeed. Machiavelli is not offering these cases as raw celebration. He is narrowing the lesson. Where a city has lived in freedom, distance plus indirect control may preserve exactly what the conqueror most needs to dissolve. So he pushes to the brutal line: there is no safe way to retain such cities other than by ruining them. The sentence shocks because it exposes the chapter's scale of judgment. The question is not which policy feels milder. It is which policy actually removes the political basis for renewed resistance.

That basis is named with unusual bluntness. In rebellion, a free city has always the watchword of liberty and its ancient privileges as a rallying point, and neither time nor benefits will ever cause it to forget. Those are not decorative words. They explain why outward calm can be a false reading. Benefits do not cancel remembered independence. Time does not automatically convert a self-governing people into subjects who have forgotten their former standing. Machiavelli even narrows the claim: unless such people are disunited or dispersed, they do not forget that name or those privileges. His example of Pisa makes the point severe. Even after a hundred years under Florentine bondage, the city still rallied to the memory of what it had been. So a conqueror who points to present quiet, improved conditions, or cooperative elites may only be describing the surface interval before memory finds its next opening.

The chapter ends by contrasting this case with places used to living under a prince. Once the old princely family has been exterminated, such places are slower to take up arms. They are accustomed to obedience, cannot agree in making one from among themselves, and do not know how to govern themselves. Republics differ because the memory they carry is not simply attachment to a person or line. It is practice in self-rule, joined to vitality, hatred, and a desire for vengeance. That is why the conclusion narrows so hard: the safest way is to destroy them or to reside there. The next problem turns in a different direction. After asking how to hold cities that remember liberty, Machiavelli moves to rulers who rise by their own arms and ability, where the question is not how to smother an old freedom but how new rule is founded in the first place.
