Chapter 11 arrives right after Machiavelli has measured ordinary strength by resources, walls, and the temper of a city's people. That is why its opening feels so abrupt. Ecclesiastical principalities do not fit easily on that scale. Machiavelli says all their difficulties lie before possession. They may be acquired by capacity or by good fortune, but afterward they can be held without either, because they are sustained by the ancient ordinances of religion. This is not just another variation on ordinary rule. It is the book's clearest exception. The prince of such a state does not have to defend it in the usual way, and he does not have to rule subjects in the usual way, yet the principality remains secure.

Machiavelli underlines the strangeness by stacking paradoxes. These princes have states and do not defend them. They have subjects and do not rule them. The states, although unguarded, are not taken from them. The subjects, although not ruled, neither desire nor are able to alienate themselves. Such principalities alone, he says, are secure and happy. He even half-withdraws from analysis, saying that they are upheld by powers to which the human mind cannot reach, and that it would be presumptuous to discuss them too far. The important point is that he does not mock this exemption, but neither does he leave the chapter there. He still asks a worldly question: if the Church enjoyed this unusual security, how did it also become so great in temporal power?

That historical question changes the chapter from pure exception into institutional history. Before Charles, King of France, entered Italy, the country stood under a balance among the Pope, the Venetians, the King of Naples, the Duke of Milan, and the Florentines. Those powers chiefly worried about two dangers: that a foreigner would enter Italy under arms, and that one of their own number would seize too much territory. The Pope and the Venetians were the greatest concerns. To restrain Venice the others had to unite. To keep down the papacy, they relied on the Roman barons, especially the factions of Orsini and Colonnesi. Those armed factions, standing near the Pontiff himself, kept the pontificate weak and disorderly. Even a courageous pope could not easily free himself from them. Short papal lives made the problem worse, since a pope rarely had time to crush one faction before the larger cycle of rivalry began again.

Alexander VI marks the break in that earlier weakness. Machiavelli says he showed more clearly than any pontiff before him what a pope with money and arms could do. Through the instrumentality of the Duke Valentino and because of the entry of the French, he carried out many of the moves Machiavelli had already described in the duke's rise. The key point is delicate. Alexander's immediate intention was not to aggrandize the Church but the duke. Yet what he did strengthened the Church anyway, because after his death and the duke's ruin, the Church inherited the fruits of those labours. So the chapter does not say that ecclesiastical principalities grow only by miracle or by sanctity. It says their holding rests on something exceptional, while their worldly greatness can still be enlarged through force, money, and political opportunity.

Julius II then receives a much stronger inheritance and adds to it. He finds the Church already in possession of the Romagna, the barons of Rome reduced, the factions weakened by Alexander's punishments, and the means of accumulating money opened more widely than before. Julius follows these gains but also improves them. He seeks Bologna, moves against the Venetians, and drives the French from Italy. Machiavelli gives him extra credit for one reason above all: Julius acts to strengthen the Church and not any private person. That distinction matters because it separates institutional consolidation from favoritism. Julius also keeps the Orsini and Colonnesi within bounds and refuses to let them possess their own cardinals, because factional cardinals would revive disorder among the barons. In other words, he does not simply wield temporary force. He narrows the channels through which the old weakness kept reproducing itself.

By the time Leo X inherits the pontificate, Machiavelli can describe it as most powerful. The chapter's strangeness lies in what it asks the reader to hold together. Ecclesiastical principalities are uniquely secure because ancient religious ordinances uphold them in a way ordinary politics cannot fully explain. Yet the Church's temporal greatness in Italy still has a very ordinary political history: factions once kept it weak, Alexander used money, arms, and opportunity to enlarge it indirectly, and Julius consolidated that enlargement for the Church itself. Chapter 11 is therefore neither a mystical aside nor a simple power chronicle. It is an exception chapter that still records accumulation. That is why it also prepares the return to general rules in Chapter 12, where Machiavelli will move back from the peculiar security of ecclesiastical rule to the very ordinary dangers of soldiery and dependence.
