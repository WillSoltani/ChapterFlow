What if the easiest rise is the most dangerous one? Machiavelli opens this chapter by stripping glamour from sudden elevation. The man raised by good fortune or by the arms of others has little trouble on the way up because he flies. The trouble comes after he lands. He has the state before he has the habits, loyal forces, or fixed relations that let him hold it. Title and territory arrive ahead of roots. That is why Machiavelli compares such states to things in nature that spring up fast and therefore cannot survive the first storm. What self-made rulers built before accession, these rulers have to build afterward, while already exposed. The whole chapter turns on that reversed sequence. Rising is easy precisely because someone else carried the weight. Keeping is hard because the foundations are still missing when the real pressure begins.

The contrast with Chapter 6 is explicit in the pair Machiavelli chooses: Francesco Sforza and Cesare Borgia. Sforza rises by his own means and great ability, suffers a thousand anxieties in the gaining, and keeps with little trouble. Borgia, Duke Valentino, rises during the ascendancy of his father and loses after that ascendancy declines. Yet Machiavelli does not choose him as a mere warning. He chooses him because, despite the borrowed elevation, Borgia does almost everything an able new prince should do once he understands the danger of standing on another man's fortune. The problem is no longer whether roots matter. The problem is whether roots can be laid fast enough after accession to turn borrowed power into self-standing power.

Borgia's first lesson is that the same forces that elevate you can later confine you, seize you, or abandon you. Alexander VI can only aggrandize him by rearranging the powers around the Church, calling the French back into Italy, and using papal influence to open Romagna. That gets the duke land, but it does not yet get him independence. The moment he tries to advance, he sees the limits of the arrangement. The Orsini forces he is using may refuse him or turn on him. The king of France may stop him whenever his interests shift. Borrowed arms have carried him upward, but they have also left him answerable to patrons and factions whose loyalty does not belong to him. So the chapter makes its decisive turn. Borgia resolves to depend no more on the arms and the luck of others. That single decision organizes almost everything that follows.

The next steps show how hard that decision is to carry out in practice. He weakens the Orsini and Colonna factions in Rome by drawing their gentlemen into his service with pay, office, and honor. He waits for the chance to crush the Orsini more directly, survives the tumults that follow the Magione meeting, then uses reconciliation itself as a trap at Sinigalia. Machiavelli lingers on this because it shows a ruler cutting loose from dependence not by proclamation but by sequence. He detaches adherents, isolates rival leaders, and turns yesterday's necessary partners into today's contained danger. The point is not simple cleverness. It is that a ruler raised by others cannot remain safe while the key levers of force and faction still answer elsewhere.

The Romagna episode deepens the chapter by adding public order to elite dependence. Borgia finds the region under weak rulers who produce robbery, quarrels, and violence. He installs Ramiro d'Orco with full power, and the man restores peace quickly through stern means. Then Borgia changes the form of rule. He creates a court under a respected president so that justice no longer appears as one minister's naked severity. After that, he stages the most notorious act in the chapter: Ramiro's execution in the piazza at Cesena, with the block and bloody knife left beside him. Machiavelli does not present this as moral cleanliness. He presents it as political calculation. The duke wants the fruits of severity without letting the full hatred of that severity remain attached to him. The result, he says, is that the people are at once satisfied and dismayed. Order has been restored, and its most odious instrument has been publicly separated from the ruler who used it.

From there Borgia thinks beyond the present and turns to the real horizon of borrowed power: succession. Alexander will die. A new pope may not be friendly. So the duke works on four fronts at once: eliminate dispossessed families who could appeal to the next papacy, win the Roman gentlemen, bend the college toward himself, and gather so much strength that he can withstand the first shock after his father's death. Machiavelli's judgment is that he accomplishes three of the four and is close to the fourth. He is already spreading beyond Romagna, already gaining enough reputation that he might soon have stood by himself. Here the chapter's tension becomes sharpest. Foundations laid afterward can be real. Borgia has not simply improvised. He has almost converted a fortune-made ascent into a power that no longer depends on fortune's first gift.

And yet the chapter refuses the comfort of mastery. Alexander dies. Borgia is sick almost to death. Two hostile armies remain in the field. Timing, health, and succession strike together. Machiavelli still insists the foundations were good, because Romagna waited for him, Rome did not immediately break him, and much of his plan held longer than it had any right to if the roots were false. That is why he offers Borgia as the liveliest example for rulers raised by others' arms or fortune. The duke shows what such a ruler must do: win friends, break rivals, create new force, reorder the governed territory, and prepare for the patron's disappearance. But Machiavelli also closes with the chapter's hardest correction. Borgia makes a bad choice in Julius II, wrongly believing fresh benefits can erase old injuries. They cannot. Men continue to fear or hate according to what has already passed between them. If Chapter 6 showed the founder who pays first and stands later, Chapter 7 shows the ruler who is lifted first and then races to build the ground beneath himself before fortune changes again.
