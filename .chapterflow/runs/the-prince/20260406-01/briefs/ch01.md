Core Claim:
Before asking how rule can be kept, Machiavelli first forces the reader to classify what kind of principality is in front of them. A hereditary state, a wholly new state, and an annexed member of an existing state do not present the same governing problem. A territory used to living under a prince is not the same material as one used to freedom. The route of acquisition, whether by one's own arms, by others, by fortune, or by ability, is not background detail; it helps define the case itself.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
This chapter does not yet give tactics. Its distinctiveness lies in its restraint. It turns statecraft into a problem of diagnosis before it becomes a problem of action, and it refuses to let "new" function as one undifferentiated label.

Author Logic Chain:
First the author divides all states into republics or principalities and narrows the book toward principalities.
Then the author splits principalities into hereditary and new, and splits the new into wholly new and annexed members.
Then the author shows that prior civic habit and means of acquisition add more decisive distinctions before any advice about holding power can make sense.

Required Anchors:
- Francesco Sforza and Milan: use as the named case of an entirely new principality.
- Naples and the King of Spain: use as the named case of territory annexed to a hereditary state.
- The contrast between populations accustomed to a prince and populations accustomed to freedom, plus the contrast between one's own arms and those of others, fortune, and ability.

- hereditary vs new: the first principality split.

- accustomed to a prince vs accustomed to freedom: prior civic habit as a governing variable.

Specific Applications:
- diagnosing whether a new leadership role is inherited, newly founded, or attached to an older power base
- distinguishing between a group that expects centralized direction and one used to self-rule
- separating a position won through personal credibility from one held through borrowed backing

Common Misreadings:

- collapsing all new principalities into one case
- assuming means of acquisition are secondary once power has been obtained
