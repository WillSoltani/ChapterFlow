Core Claim:
Cities used to living under their own laws and in freedom are uniquely dangerous to hold because memory of liberty and ancient privileges survives conquest. Machiavelli gives three courses for ruling them: ruin them, reside there, or let them keep their laws under tribute and a friendly oligarchy. Yet the chapter's hard conclusion is that there is no truly safe way to retain such cities other than destroying them or residing there, because free cities rally around remembered liberty in a way that time and benefits do not erase.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
Chapter 4 explained how regime structure changes conquest difficulty. Chapter 5 focuses on a more volatile memory: self-government itself. The chapter is short, but it carries one of the book's sharpest claims about freedom, vengeance, and the persistence of political identity.

Author Logic Chain:
First the author says that cities used to their own laws and freedom present three possible courses: ruin, reside, or leave their laws while drawing tribute through a friendly oligarchy.
Then the author explains why the oligarchic option can appear workable: the new local government knows it stands by the prince's favor.
Then the author tests the options through examples: Sparta lost Athens and Thebes after ruling through oligarchy, while Rome destroyed places like Capua, Carthage, and Numantia and kept them.
Then the author says Rome also failed when trying the Spartan method in Greece.
Then the author gives the chapter's hardest rule: a city accustomed to freedom that is not destroyed may destroy the conqueror, because liberty and ancient privileges remain a rallying cry.
Then the author uses Pisa under the Florentines to show how long bondage still does not erase that memory.
Then the author contrasts this with cities used to a prince, which are slower to arm once the old family is gone because they do not know how to govern themselves.
Then the author ends by saying republics have more vitality, hatred, and desire for vengeance, so the safest way is to destroy them or reside there.

Required Anchors:
- The three courses: ruin, reside, or permit old laws under tribute and a loyal oligarchy.
- Sparta losing Athens and Thebes despite oligarchic control.
- Rome keeping Capua, Carthage, and Numantia by dismantling them.
- Rome failing when trying to keep Greece free under its laws.
- The watchword of liberty and ancient privileges as a permanent rallying point.
- Pisa still rallying after a hundred years under Florentine bondage.
- The contrast between free cities and places used to a prince once the old family is exterminated.
- The closing line that republics have more vitality, hatred, and desire for vengeance, so the safest way is destruction or residence.

Frameworks or Terms Introduced:
- three-course rule: ruin, reside, or preserve laws under tribute and oligarchy
- liberty memory: free cities remember self-rule as a living rallying cry
- friendly oligarchy: local elites whose survival depends on princely support
- time-and-benefits failure: ordinary advantages do not erase remembered liberty
- prince-accustomed contrast: places used to obeying a prince are easier once the old family is removed
- republic vitality: freedom produces longer memory, stronger hatred, and sharper vengeance

Specific Applications:
- taking over a previously self-governing group and discovering that nominal autonomy does not dissolve remembered independence
- trying to rule through a friendly local council that survives only by outside backing
- misreading gratitude or improved benefits as enough to erase a group's older identity

Common Misreadings:
- treating the three courses as equally safe options when the chapter itself sharpens toward destruction or residence
- reading liberty here as a pleasant ideal rather than as a stubborn organizing memory
- assuming time, prosperity, or benefits will gradually solve the problem
- forgetting the contrast with cities used to obeying a prince

Counterarguments or Limits:
This chapter is short and extreme. Modern examples should translate the mechanism of remembered self-rule, not the literal violence. Do not soften the chapter into generic decentralization talk, but do keep explicit moral distance from the destruction logic.

Previous Chapter Bridge:
- Chapter 4 showed that different internal structures change how conquest behaves. Chapter 5 turns to the case where the old structure was self-government, so the hardest surviving memory is liberty itself.

Next Chapter Bridge:
- the next chapter will move to new principalities acquired by one's own arms and ability, where the issue becomes founding power rather than retaining a city that remembers its own laws

Moral Complexity:
High. The chapter openly tests destruction against indirect rule and residence. Keep analytic distance and let the harshness remain visible. Do not naturalize the recommendation or turn it into motivational toughness.

Threshold Question:
