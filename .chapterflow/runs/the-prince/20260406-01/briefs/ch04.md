Core Claim:
Whether a conquered state is hard to seize or hard to hold depends on how it was governed before conquest. A centralized monarchy of prince plus servants, like the Turk or Darius, is difficult to enter because there are no powerful barons to invite an outsider in, but once conquered and the ruling family removed, it is comparatively easy to hold. A kingdom organized through ancient barons, like France, is easier to enter because malcontent nobles can open the way, but harder to keep because those same nobles survive as centers of renewed resistance. Alexander held Asia because Darius's empire resembled the first kind, not the second.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
Chapter 3 explained why mixed acquisitions destabilize the new ruler. Chapter 4 narrows from that turmoil to a more precise variable: the internal structure of the conquered state. The chapter matters because it shows that apparent conquest success or failure can come from the subject state's design rather than from the conqueror's raw brilliance.

Author Logic Chain:
First the author poses the puzzle: Alexander dies before Asia is fully settled, yet his successors face no broad rebellion.
Then the author classifies regimes into two kinds: prince with servants, or prince with barons.
Then the author explains the first kind through the Turk: difficult to seize, because no nobles can help the invader, but easy to hold once the ruling family is destroyed.
Then the author explains the second kind through France: easier to enter through malcontent barons, but hard to keep because surviving lords become heads of fresh movements.
Then the author applies the distinction to Darius and Alexander: Darius governed like the Turk, so once he was overthrown and killed, the state remained secure.
Then the author contrasts that with Roman difficulty in lands full of principalities whose memory endured.
Then the author ends with the general correction: the difference comes not from conqueror ability alone, but from the lack or presence of uniformity in the subject state.

Required Anchors:
- The opening puzzle of Alexander's successors holding Asia after Alexander dies.
- The two regime types: prince with servants versus prince with barons.
- The Turk example: hard to seize because the realm is unified and no ministers can carry the people, but easy to hold once the ruling family is exterminated.
- The France example: easy to enter through some baron, hard to hold afterward because the remaining lords head new movements.
- Darius as similar to the Turk and therefore secure after Alexander's victory and Darius's death.
- Roman rebellions in Spain, France, and Greece because many principalities remained in memory.
- The final line that the difference turns on the lack of uniformity in the subject state, not merely on conqueror ability.

Frameworks or Terms Introduced:
- regime-structure split: prince-with-servants versus prince-with-barons
- inverse conquest curve: the traits that make a state hard to seize can make it easier to hold afterward
- entry assistance problem: barons can help an invader enter but remain rival centers after victory
- exterminated family rule: in centralized monarchies, removing the ruling line removes the only credible focus of loyalty
- memory of many principalities: decentralized political memory sustains later rebellion
- subject-state uniformity: the conquered state's internal design explains later stability more than heroics alone

Specific Applications:
- taking over an organization run through one central chain of command versus one built around semi-independent partners
- misunderstanding how easy entry through local allies predicts later holding difficulty
- attributing stability after takeover to personal brilliance when the old structure did much of the work

Common Misreadings:
- reading the chapter as praise of centralized monarchy rather than a diagnostic comparison
- assuming easy conquest always means easy rule afterward
- treating Alexander's success as proof of exceptional genius alone
- flattening the Roman example into generic persistence instead of memory of many principalities

Counterarguments or Limits:
This chapter is narrower than Chapter 3. It does not give a general rule for all conquest. It isolates one variable, internal regime structure, through a comparison between the Turk, France, Darius, and Rome. Do not import later arguments about cruelty, faith, or military organization to enlarge it.

Previous Chapter Bridge:
- Chapter 3 asked why mixed states so often resist new rule. Chapter 4 now asks why one huge conquest, Alexander's, did not behave that way after the conqueror died.
