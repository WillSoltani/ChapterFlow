Core Claim:
Chapter 11 argues that ecclesiastical principalities are uniquely hard to gain but easy to hold, because they are sustained by the ancient ordinances of religion and therefore do not depend on the ordinary combination of arms, rule, and defence. Yet Machiavelli does not leave the matter at exception alone. He also explains how the Church's temporal power in Italy became great: first papal weakness under Roman baron factions and short papal lives, then Alexander VI's use of money, arms, the Duke Valentino, and French entry, then Julius II's strengthening of the Church itself rather than a private favorite, leaving Leo X an unusually powerful pontificate.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
This chapter is the book's clearest exception chapter. It suspends the ordinary logic just stated in Chapter 10, because these principalities are held without the usual requirements of defence or active rule. At the same time, it gives a concrete historical account of institutional accumulation. The oddity lies in holding both truths together: religious ordinances sustain possession, yet temporal greatness still grows through specific political moves.

Author Logic Chain:
First the author says ecclesiastical principalities are difficult before possession but easy afterward.
Then the author says they are acquired by capacity or good fortune but held without either because ancient religious ordinances sustain them.
Then the author underscores the exception by saying these princes have states and do not defend them, subjects and do not rule them, yet remain secure.
Then the author says he could stop there because such matters are upheld by powers beyond ordinary human reach.
Then he still asks how the Church acquired such temporal greatness in Italy.
Then he reconstructs the earlier Italian balance before Charles of France entered Italy and explains why the papacy had been kept weak, especially by Roman baron factions and short papal lives.
Then he says Alexander VI showed what a pope with money and arms could do, even though his immediate intention favored the duke rather than the Church.
Then he says Julius II inherited that stronger base and expanded it while strengthening the Church itself, reducing factions and curbing factional cardinals.
Then he ends with Leo X inheriting the most powerful pontificate so far.
