Core Claim:
Chapter 22 argues that the first public proof of a prince's understanding is the quality of the servants around him. Wise rule does not require the prince to originate every best idea himself. It does require him to recognize capable ministers, keep them faithful, detect self-seeking servants, and bind trustworthy ones through honour, wealth, shared cares, and dependence. The chapter's central move is diagnostic: a good secretary proves the prince's judgment, while a bad one exposes his first serious error.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
This chapter is not generic advice to hire talented people. It says appointments are evidence about the prince himself. The Venafro and Pandolfo example matters because it introduces a second form of intelligence: even if the prince is not the mind that comprehends everything by itself, he can still be wise enough to judge good and bad in another. The closing movement also matters. Faithfulness is not treated as a mysterious virtue. It is maintained by a relationship in which the servant is honored, enriched, burdened with cares, and kept from imagining that he can stand alone.

Author Logic Chain:
First the author says the choice of servants is highly important to a prince.
Then the author says the first opinion formed of a prince and of his understanding comes from observing the men around him.
Then the author says capable and faithful servants show that the prince recognized the capable and kept them faithful.
Then the author says poor servants reveal the prince's prime error in choosing them.
Then the author gives Antonio da Venafro serving Pandolfo Petrucci as a case that made Pandolfo appear clever.
Then the author distinguishes three classes of intellect: one comprehends by itself, one appreciates what others comprehend, and one comprehends neither.
Then the author says that if Pandolfo was not in the first rank he was in the second, because he could recognize good and bad in his servant, praise one thing, and correct another.
Then the author says such judgment keeps the servant from hoping to deceive the prince.
Then the author offers a test that never fails: the servant who thinks more of his own interests than the prince's will never be trustworthy.
Then the author says the servant who holds another's state in his hands ought never to think of himself first.
Then the author turns to the prince's reciprocal duty: study the servant, honour him, enrich him, do him kindnesses, and share honours and cares with him.
Then the author adds the harder limit that the prince must let the servant see he cannot stand alone.
Then the author says the result of this mutual disposition is trust; when that structure fails, the end is disastrous for one or both.

Frameworks or Terms Introduced:
- appointments as diagnostic: chosen servants reveal the prince's judgment
- second-rank intelligence: wisdom can include judging others well, not only inventing alone
- self-interest test: the minister who privately serves himself fails the office
- bounded loyalty: honour, wealth, shared cares, and dependence keep service honest
- reciprocal trust structure: prince and servant trust each other only when both are rightly disposed

Specific Applications:
- reading an organization leader by the caliber and alignment of the deputies closest to them
- distinguishing brilliant but self-advancing aides from capable and faithful ones
- understanding that a leader can be wise through selection and correction even if not personally the originator of every insight
- designing incentives that make a deputy loyal without letting them imagine independence from the leader they serve
- judging whether a trusted lieutenant is carrying the mission or quietly converting it into personal advantage

Common Misreadings:
- reducing the chapter to generic hiring advice
- treating it as praise of blind obedience without judgment
- assuming the prince must always be the first class of intellect to rule well
- reading honour and enrichment as generosity alone rather than political binding
- missing that the chapter judges the prince through his ministers, not only the ministers through the prince

Counterarguments or Limits:
Modern use needs precision here. The point is not to demand servility or punish all independent thought. The point is alignment, discrimination, and trustworthiness in offices that carry another person's charge. The chapter also sets a limit on cleverness worship: brilliance that serves itself is still failure. Wisdom appears not just in finding talent but in recognizing, correcting, rewarding, and binding it properly.

Moral Complexity:
Medium-high. The chapter respects capable service and reciprocal trust, but it also treats loyalty as something deliberately structured through honours, wealth, dependence, and fear of risky separation. The servant should not be a free rival. He should be valuable and bound.
