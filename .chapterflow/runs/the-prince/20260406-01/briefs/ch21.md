Core Claim:
Chapter 21 argues that princes gain renown through visible greatness rather than through passive safety. Great enterprises, memorable internal examples, declared friendship or enmity instead of neutrality, patronage of ability, protection of productive life, and public festivals all help make a ruler esteemed. The chapter's central anti-rule is neutrality: the prince who refuses to choose is often left as the prey of the conqueror. Prudence here does not mean finding a perfectly safe course. It means choosing the lesser evil, aligning decisively, and combining public courtesy with undiminished majesty.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
This chapter is not generic advice about reputation or charisma. Renown here is political shape. Ferdinand's greatness lies not just in bold acts but in the way one enterprise follows another and keeps the minds of the people occupied. Neutrality is rejected not because risk disappears under commitment, but because refusing to take sides usually leaves the prince exposed without favor from anyone. The closing movement also matters: renown is sustained by honoring ability, rewarding peaceful enterprise, and appearing among the people without dissolving rank.

Frameworks or Terms Introduced:
- renown through sequence: greatness comes from visible enterprises that keep public attention occupied
- anti-neutrality rule: refusing sides often increases dependence rather than reducing danger
- doubtful-choice prudence: one avoids one trouble only by entering another, so prudence is choosing the lesser evil
- civic patronage: esteem grows through rewarding skill and making productive life feel protected
- courteous majesty: public accessibility matters, but only if rank is never dissolved

Specific Applications:
- distinguishing visible, directional action from passive attempts to look safe
- deciding when open alignment is less dangerous than neutrality
- rewarding talent and productive work in ways that make public life feel energized rather than preyed upon
- designing recurring public rituals that display presence without collapsing authority into familiarity
- asking whether a leader's current posture produces admiration, suspense, and shape, or only administrative quiet

Common Misreadings:
- reducing renown to branding or image polish
- treating Ferdinand's case as simple praise of expansion detached from method
- treating neutrality as the safe middle course
- turning the chapter into pure foreign-policy advice and missing the internal examples, rewards, and festivals
- turning public sociability into chumminess that erases rank
- missing that risk remains in every option and prudence means choosing among doubtful evils

Counterarguments or Limits:
Modern use must keep the chapter's anti-neutrality logic precise. The point is not reckless constant aggression or theatrical visibility for its own sake. It is that political esteem grows from memorable action and clear shape, while neutrality often leaves a ruler unsupported by both sides. The closing counsel also sets a limit: presence among the people is valuable only if it does not erode the majesty of office.
