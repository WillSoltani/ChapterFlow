Chapter Title: Concerning New Principalities Which Are Acquired Either by the Arms of Others or by Good Fortune
Position In Book: premium counterpoint to Chapter 6, asking what happens when ascent is easy because another power lifts the ruler before he has built anything durable of his own
Source Sidecar Path: .chapterflow/runs/the-prince/20260406-01/sidecars/source/ch07.source.txt

Core Claim:
Principalities gained by fortune or by the arms of others are easy to rise into and difficult to keep because the ruler arrives at the summit before he has knowledge, loyal force, or foundations. Machiavelli's main lesson is not that such a rise is hopeless, but that the missing roots must be built afterward under pressure and danger. Cesare Borgia becomes the chapter's central case because he does almost everything a prudent ruler should do to turn borrowed elevation into self-standing power: cut dependence on unstable backers, reorganize local order, win friends, destroy rivals, and prepare for succession shocks. His near-success also shows the limit of skill. Foundations built afterward may be real, yet fortune and one bad political choice can still ruin the almost-secured ruler.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
Chapter 6 admired the self-made founder who struggles upward and then stands on his own roots. Chapter 7 reverses that sequence. It is the book's first long case study, and it combines classification, counterexample, political sequence, administrative theater, and retrospective judgment inside one single figure, Cesare Borgia.

Author Logic Chain:
First the author says rulers raised solely by good fortune have little trouble in rising but much in keeping, because they fly upward without roots, knowledge, or faithful forces.
Then the author says sudden states resemble fast-growing things that lack fixed foundations and correspondencies unless ability lays afterward what was not laid before.
Then the author contrasts Francesco Sforza, who rose by his own means and kept with little trouble, with Cesare Borgia, who rose through his father's ascendancy and then had to lay roots after the rise.
Then the author says Borgia's case deserves close study because he did everything a wise and able new prince should do, and his failure came from extraordinary fortune rather than omission.
Then the author traces how Borgia broke dependence on French power, Orsini, and Colonna networks by weakening factions, winning gentlemen, and trapping enemies at Sinigalia.
Then the author turns to the Romagna, where Borgia uses Ramiro d'Orco to impose order, then regularizes justice and distances himself from the minister's odium through a public execution.
Then the author shows Borgia preparing for Alexander's death by destroying rival families, winning Roman gentlemen, shaping the college, and trying to acquire enough strength to withstand the first shock.
Then the author says Borgia would likely have stood by himself if Alexander had lived longer or if he himself had not been deathly ill at the decisive moment.
Then the author concludes that Borgia is still the liveliest example for those raised by others' arms or fortune, except for one fatal mistake: choosing Julius II, wrongly believing new benefits could erase old injuries.

Frameworks or Terms Introduced:
- easy-rise hard-keeping problem: borrowed ascent comes before the ruler has his own roots
- afterward foundations: sudden rulers must build after accession what self-made rulers build on the way up
- patron-dependence trap: the same forces that elevate the ruler can later limit, seize, or abandon him
- faction-cutting sequence: win over adherents, isolate rival houses, then remove dangerous dependence
- order then odium management: harsh restoration, formalized justice, and public redirection of blame
- succession-shock planning: build enough power to survive the patron's death and the first hostile turn
- injury-memory rule: fresh favors do not erase the fear or hatred of people previously harmed

Specific Applications:
- being elevated quickly by a sponsor, founder, family elder, or institution and discovering that title arrives before genuine support
- inheriting a disorderly unit and needing to build real order without letting the most hated instrument become the whole regime
- realizing that dependence on the patron's network makes every later decision conditional until that dependence is cut
- offering new favors to old rivals and misreading those favors as full reconciliation

Common Misreadings:
- treating the chapter as if luck-made rulers are simply doomed when Machiavelli's real point is that foundations can still be built afterward
- reading Cesare Borgia only as a villain or only as a hero and missing the analytical use of his actions
- reducing the Romagna sequence to cruelty alone instead of seeing the sequence of disorder, concentration of force, institutionalization, and blame management
- collapsing the chapter into generic networking advice instead of dependence, factions, order, succession, and injury memory
- forgetting that Borgia is blamed in the end for one strategic election choice even after almost everything else is praised

Counterarguments or Limits:
This is one of the book's most morally charged chapters. Modern examples must translate coercion, fraud, faction, and theatrical punishment into analogous authority problems without pretending the original harshness is mild. Keep distance from endorsement, especially in the Ramiro sequence. Do not import later chapters on wickedness, fear, or broken faith to pad the analysis; the source already gives enough through foundation building, faction management, public order, and injury memory.

Previous Chapter Bridge:
- Chapter 6 argued that self-made founders suffer on the ascent but stand more securely once their order is their own. Chapter 7 asks what happens when a ruler receives the summit first and has to build his roots afterward under danger.

Next Chapter Bridge:
- the next chapter turns from borrowed elevation to principalities obtained by wickedness, where the question is no longer only how roots are built but what kind of seizure morally and politically defines the regime from the start

Hard-Depth Minimum:
Keep the timing pressure alive. The ruler raised by others must build faithful force, local order, elite control, and succession insulation after power has already been granted. Hard depth must preserve why those tasks cannot all be done at once and why even excellent sequencing may still be vulnerable to fortune.

Threshold Question:
If borrowed support has lifted a ruler into place before he has roots of his own, what must he secure first so that the whole structure does not collapse when the patron weakens: loyal force, local order, or insulation against succession?

- no invented psychological motives beyond fear, hatred, loyalty, goodwill, and calculation named by the source
- no modern examples that excuse or celebrate the chapter's cruelty as mere decisiveness
