Core Claim:
Chapter 10 argues that the real measure of strength is whether a prince can support himself with his own resources or whether he always needs the assistance of others. If he can raise enough men or money to meet the enemy in the field, that is one kind of strength. If he cannot and must shelter behind walls, the prudent course is different: provision and fortify the town, avoid defending the whole countryside, and make sure the city is hard to assault and the people are not hateful toward him. In that second case, material preparation and civic management belong together. A wise prince can keep citizens steadfast even after outside property is burnt, provided he supports and defends them through the siege.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
This chapter is diagnostic rather than classificatory. It does not ask what kind of principality you have, but what kind of pressure it can survive. It also combines physical and political conditions more tightly than the label fortification suggests. Walls, provisions, work, artillery, public order, and the absence of odium are all part of one strength test.

Author Logic Chain:
First the author says strength must be measured by whether a prince can support himself with his own resources or must rely on others.
Then the author distinguishes those who can raise a sufficient army to join battle from those who must defend themselves behind walls.
Then the author says the second kind should provision and fortify their towns and not attempt to defend the whole country.
Then the author adds the anti-odium condition: a well-fortified town that is not hated by its people is hard to attack.
Then the author gives the cities of Germany as the model of visible difficulty, preparation, artillery, yearly stores, civic work, military exercises, and ordinances.
Then the author answers the objection that burnt property outside the city will make citizens abandon the prince.
Then the author says a powerful and courageous prince can manage this by hope, fear of the enemy, adroit handling of the bold, and steady support of the citizens.
Then the author closes with the paradox that people may bind themselves more strongly because their losses were incurred in the prince's defence.

Required Anchors:
- The opening measurement: can the prince support himself with his own resources, or does he need the assistance of others.
- The definition of self-support as enough men or money to raise a sufficient army to join battle.
- The definition of dependency as being forced to shelter behind walls.
- The instruction to provision and fortify the town and not defend the whole country.
- The anti-odium condition: the city must be well fortified and the prince not hated by his people.
- The German cities example: little country, walls, ditches, artillery, one year's stores, civic work, military exercises, ordinances.
- The objection that citizens with burnt property outside the city will lose patience.
- The response: hope, fear of enemy cruelty, and adroit handling of the bold.
- The line that people are bound by the benefits they confer as much as by those they receive.
- The final claim that the wise prince keeps citizens steadfast by supporting and defending them.

Frameworks or Terms Introduced:
- self-support test: whether the prince can stand on his own resources in need
- field-capable principality: enough men or money to meet the enemy in open battle
- wall-backed principality: a regime that survives by fortified withdrawal and siege endurance
- fortified-town prudence: provision the core and do not dissipate force defending everything
- anti-odium condition: walls matter differently when the people do not hate the prince
- siege steadfastness: managing morale after outside losses rather than pretending losses will not occur

Specific Applications:
- deciding whether to defend every exposed perimeter or secure a core operation that can actually hold
- understanding that stockpiles, visible difficulty, and civic work all support morale rather than standing apart from it
- recognizing that people may stay loyal through damage when they see their losses as incurred in a common defence and see the leader still supporting them

Common Misreadings:
- turning the chapter into generic resilience talk
- treating walls as sufficient without the people or provisions
- reading the German cities example as hardware only and missing work, ordinances, and morale
- assuming the chapter promises that outside losses will not matter
- flattening the final paradox about conferred benefits into simple gratitude rhetoric

Counterarguments or Limits:
Chapter 10 is written in the language of siege, town, and countryside. Modern examples should preserve the distinction between open-field defence and defensible core, rather than dissolving the chapter into broad preparedness advice. The prince's psychological management must stay tied to concrete loss and defence.

Previous Chapter Bridge:
- Chapter 9 asked what kind of civic support makes rule safer. Chapter 10 asks whether that principality is actually strong enough to stand when attacked.

Next Chapter Bridge:

Moral Complexity:
Medium. The chapter values courage, prudence, and defence, but it also includes calculated management of hope, fear, and bold subjects during siege pressure. Keep the focus on survivability and civic steadiness, not patriotic romance.

Concept Budget:
- target concept count: 5

Hard-Depth Minimum:
Hard depth must preserve that fortification is not enough by itself. A wall-backed prince still needs stores, labour, visible difficulty, anti-odium, and political management after external damage. The chapter fails if it becomes a simple bunker lesson.

Threshold Question:
If a prince cannot meet the enemy in the field, what secures him first: defending the countryside everywhere, a fortified and provisioned city that is not hated by its people, or early foreign assistance?

Unsupported Zones:
- no drift into later chapters on mercenaries, auxiliaries, or the art of war
- no reader-facing claim that fortifications cancel political hatred
- no flattening into generic emergency-preparedness language
- no invention beyond siege, provisions, morale, odium, work, and steadfastness
