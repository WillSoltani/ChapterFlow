Core Claim:
Hereditary principalities are easier to hold than new ones because long custom lowers the need for disruptive offense, subjects are already disposed toward the established line, and ordinary prudence is usually enough to preserve the state. Even if extraordinary force drives the hereditary ruler out, the usurper's later weakness often opens the way back.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
Chapter 1 built a classification map. Chapter 2 now isolates the easiest principality case to show what continuity itself buys. The pressure is not brilliance. It is the stabilizing force of custom, habit, and reduced grievance.

Author Logic Chain:
First the author leaves republics aside and keeps the promised order by turning to principalities only.
Then the author states the main comparative claim: hereditary states are easier to hold than new ones.
Then the author explains why: do not transgress ancestral customs, adapt prudently to circumstances, and average powers will often suffice.
Then the author adds the limit case: extraordinary and excessive force can still depose the ruler, but a usurper's later trouble can restore the hereditary line.
Then the author grounds the claim in the Duke of Ferrara and explains the deeper mechanism: hereditary rulers have less cause and less necessity to offend, so they are more loved, and over time memories that motivate change fade.

Required Anchors:
- The instruction not to transgress ancestral customs and to deal prudently with circumstances as they arise.
- The claim that a prince of average powers can maintain a hereditary state unless extraordinary and excessive force removes him, plus the possibility of regaining it when the usurper falters.
- The Duke of Ferrara surviving attacks by the Venetians and Pope Julius because he was long established.
- The final mechanism: less cause to offend, more love, and antiquity erasing memories and motives for change.

Frameworks or Terms Introduced:
- hereditary principality: a state long accustomed to the same ruling family.
- custom as political ballast: ancestral habits reduce disruption and comparison pressure.
- prudence with circumstances: continuity still needs judgment rather than autopilot.
- extraordinary force versus ordinary maintenance: the chapter separates routine preservation from exceptional shocks.
- restoration after usurpation: the old line may return when the replacement generates its own instability.

Specific Applications:
- inheriting a long-settled team or family business where continuity is itself part of the asset
- stepping into leadership inside an institution already used to one internal line of authority
- deciding when not to disrupt routines simply to prove that new leadership has arrived

Common Misreadings:
- treating the chapter as a claim that hereditary rulers do not need skill
- reading "more loved" as pure affection rather than reduced grievance and long habit
- assuming hereditary rule cannot fail, even though Machiavelli explicitly allows extraordinary force and extraordinary vice
- importing mixed-principality problems back into this narrow case too early

Counterarguments or Limits:
This is the easiest case, not a universal law of politics. The chapter's confidence depends on continuity already existing. Machiavelli does not say hereditary rulers can ignore prudence, nor does he say duration rescues rulers ruined by extraordinary vice. The source is still thin, so no later tactics about fear, cruelty, or deception should be smuggled in.
