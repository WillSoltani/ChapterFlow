Core Claim:
Chapter 13 argues that auxiliaries are even more dangerous than mercenaries because they come already united and obedient to another power. If they lose, the prince is undone; if they win, he becomes their captive. The wise prince therefore avoids both mercenaries and auxiliaries, preferring his own forces and even preferring to lose with them rather than to win by another's arms. The chapter then shows, through Cesare Borgia, Hiero, David, France, and the Roman Empire, that reputation, security, and durable power rise only when force is one's own and decline when strength passes outward.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
This chapter is comparative and sequential. It does not merely restate the Chapter 12 attack on mercenaries. It isolates auxiliaries as a different and sharper danger, explains why mixed arms are a compromise but not an ideal, and turns the whole issue into a rule about real victory, reputation, and dependence. The chapter's metaphor of borrowed arms is one of the cleanest compression points in the book.

Author Logic Chain:
First the author defines auxiliaries as the forces of another prince called in to aid and defend.
Then the author gives Pope Julius as the immediate case and states the core paradox: if they lose, you are undone, and if they win, you are their captive.
Then the author adds other examples, including the Florentines at Pisa and the Emperor of Constantinople with the Turks, to show how foreign help can become a deeper danger than the original threat.
Then the author compares auxiliaries with mercenaries and says auxiliaries are more hazardous because their ruin is ready made.
Then the author says the wise prince prefers his own arms and would rather lose with them than win with another's.
Then the author traces Cesare Borgia's movement from auxiliaries to mercenaries to his own men and says his reputation rose as he became master of his own forces.
Then the author adds Hiero and David to reinforce the rejection of alien arms.
Then the author says the arms of others either fall from your back, weigh you down, or bind you fast.
Then the author turns to France to show that mixed forces are better than pure foreign reliance but still inferior to one's own.
Then the author says early apparent success can hide poison that becomes visible only later.
Then the author closes by saying no principality is secure without forces made of subjects, citizens, or dependents.

Frameworks or Terms Introduced:
- auxiliary trap: united foreign force obeying another center of command
- ready-made ruin: danger that arrives complete the moment auxiliaries succeed or fail
- reputation ladder: a prince is esteemed more highly as he becomes master of his own force
- mixed-arms compromise: better than pure foreign dependence, still worse than one's own
- borrowed-armor principle: another's force does not fit, stays heavy, or binds
- own-force rule: subjects, citizens, or dependents are the only durable base of security

Specific Applications:
- spotting when another institution's whole team is being invited in to solve a problem that will then no longer be yours to govern
- distinguishing short-term relief from long-term captivity
- seeing why apparent victory can still leave the center of authority weaker than before

Common Misreadings:
- treating auxiliaries as just another name for mercenaries
- assuming that bravery in the borrowed force solves the dependence problem
- reading Borgia's sequence as simple opportunism instead of as a lesson in decreasing danger as control becomes internal
- treating mixed forces as the chapter's preferred end state
- flattening David's example into style or personality rather than suitability and command

Counterarguments or Limits:
Modern examples should preserve the central feature of auxiliaries: the force obeys another sovereign or institutional center. The chapter is not about ordinary cooperation or alliance in the abstract. It is about letting another power carry the means by which you hold your ground.

Previous Chapter Bridge:
- Chapter 12 showed why hired captains and hired soldiers are dangerous. Chapter 13 sharpens the danger by isolating the case where the force is not even yours to hire fully, but already belongs to another ruler.

Next Chapter Bridge:

Moral Complexity:
Medium. The chapter praises self-reliance in arms, but its center is not pride. It is the political cost of handing survival to another power. Keep the argument focused on dependence, captivity, and the transfer of vigour outward.

Concept Budget:

Hard-Depth Minimum:
Hard depth must preserve why auxiliaries are worse than mercenaries, not just also bad. It must also preserve the middle status of mixed forces and the reputational argument that real mastery rises as force becomes one's own.

Threshold Question:
If a foreign force is brave, disciplined, and wins for you, why does Machiavelli still treat that outcome as a near-defeat rather than a true security?

Unsupported Zones:

- no treating mixed force as a fully acceptable resting point
- no invention beyond foreign command, dependence, reputation, and own-force security
