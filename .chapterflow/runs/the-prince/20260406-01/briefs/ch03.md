Core Claim:
Mixed principalities are hard to hold because acquisition begins by disappointing those who welcomed the new ruler, injuring those displaced by him, and forcing him to govern people whose political memory still points elsewhere. If the annexed territory resembles the old state, he can often secure it by extinguishing the prior ruling line and changing neither laws nor taxes. If it differs in language, customs, or laws, he must live there or plant colonies, manage lesser and greater powers, block strong foreigners, and treat future trouble before it grows. Louis XII loses Lombardy because he violates those rules and keeps making other powers stronger.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
Chapter 2 showed continuity helping the ruler. Chapter 3 shows continuity resisting him. It is also the first chapter where Machiavelli combines a classification frame, a cluster of practical rules, Roman precedent, and a detailed anti-model in one sustained argument.

Author Logic Chain:
First the author names the basic instability of new and composite states: people welcome change hoping for improvement, then discover they have gone from bad to worse once the new ruler burdens the acquisition.
Then the author explains the double bind: the ruler injures those he seizes from, disappoints the allies who opened the gates, and still needs the goodwill of the natives.
Then the author uses Milan and Louis XII to show why first acquisition is fragile and why second acquisition may be harder to lose after rebellion reveals suspects and weak points.
Then the author splits mixed acquisitions into two kinds: similar territories and unlike territories.
Then the author says similar territories are easier to absorb if the old ruling family is extinguished and laws and taxes are left alone.
Then the author says unlike territories require residence or, better still, colonies rather than expensive roaming garrisons.
Then the author adds the broader power-management rule: support weaker neighbors without letting them become too strong, weaken the greater, and keep rival foreigners from getting a foothold.
Then the author turns to Rome as the model of early remedy and to Louis XII as the anti-model of delayed, compounding mistakes.
Then the author ends with the general rule that whoever causes another power to grow usually prepares his own ruin.

Required Anchors:
- The opening mechanism: people change rulers willingly, hoping to better themselves, then discover they have gone from bad to worse because the new prince burdens the acquisition.
- The double bind of acquisition: injured enemies, disappointed friends, and the ruler's need for the goodwill of the natives.
- Louis XII taking and losing Milan, then the distinction between first loss and second loss after rebellion exposes delinquents, suspects, and weak places.
- The same-country rule: extinguish the family of the former prince and alter neither laws nor taxes.
- The different-country rule: reside there or send colonies; do not lean on expensive garrisons as a substitute.
- The harsh maxim that men should either be well treated or crushed, plus the logic of lighter versus heavier injury.
- The Romans in Greece, future-trouble foresight, the hectic-fever analogy, and the claim that war deferred helps others.
- Louis XII's error chain in Italy, ending with the rule that causing another to become powerful ruins you.

Frameworks or Terms Introduced:
- mixed or composite principality: a territory added onto an older state rather than inherited as a stable whole
- acquisition double bind: the newcomer begins with injured enemies and disappointed promoters at the same time
- similarity split: annexed territories of like language and custom behave differently from unlike territories
- residence versus distance: living on the spot lets the ruler detect disorder early and limit official abuse
- colonies versus garrisons: small, anchored settlement points are cheaper and politically cleaner than roving occupation
- lesser-power balancing: protect weaker neighbors without letting them become substitutes for your own rule
- future-trouble discipline: cure the political fever early rather than waiting until everyone can see it
- cascading anti-model: Louis XII shows how one mixed-principality error makes the next one harder to avoid

Specific Applications:
- taking over a merged team whose members expected immediate improvement and now resent both the disruption and the unmet promises
- trying to govern a distant branch with different routines through remote oversight instead of local presence
- building a coalition by empowering one ally so much that the ally becomes the next problem

Common Misreadings:
- treating mixed principalities as one flat case instead of a split between similar and unlike acquisitions
- assuming armed strength removes the need for native goodwill
- reading colonies and the "well treated or crushed" line as detachable slogans rather than part of a cold argument about revenge and scattered injury
- collapsing the Roman example into generic decisiveness instead of early detection and prevention
- saying Louis XII failed only because he lacked force when the chapter's point is that he kept rearranging the power map against himself

Counterarguments or Limits:
The chapter assumes conquest and annexation as its frame, so modern examples must translate mechanism rather than copy the violence literally. The source is dense, but it still stays within acquisition, residence, colonies, alliance management, and early remedy. Do not import later chapters on mercenaries, cruelty, fear, or broken faith to pad the analysis.

Previous Chapter Bridge:
- Chapter 2 showed why a ruler benefits when continuity already supports his line. Chapter 3 shows what changes when the newly acquired territory still remembers another line, another law, or another political habit.

Next Chapter Bridge:
