Core Claim:
Chapter 20 argues that common expedients of rule cannot be judged in the abstract. Their value depends on the particulars of the state and, above all, on whether they convert subjects into the prince's own strength or merely expose distrust and weakness. A new prince should arm subjects in a new principality, but disarm an acquired province added to an old state. Factions are usually a sign of weakness and fail under pressure. Formerly distrusted men can become highly useful if they need the prince enough to reverse his opinion of them. Fortresses are sometimes useful, but the best fortress is not being hated by the people.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
This chapter is not a miscellaneous appendix of tricks. It is a test of devices by circumstance. Every measure here asks the same deeper question: what really secures the prince in this case, and what only feels secure in peacetime? The chapter's hardest edge is that the most visible defensive device, the fortress, can matter less than the least architectural condition, popular goodwill.

Author Logic Chain:
First the author lists familiar expedients: disarming subjects, fostering factions, fostering enmities against oneself, gaining over the distrusted, building fortresses, and destroying them.
Then the author says these things cannot receive one final judgment apart from the particulars of the state.
Then the author says a new prince in a new principality arms subjects rather than disarming them, because armed subjects can become his own dependents and adherents.
Then the author says disarming them breeds hatred because it shows distrust and pushes the prince toward mercenaries.
Then the author distinguishes a newly added province, where disarming may be necessary while keeping arms effectively in the prince's own men.
Then the author rejects the old maxim about holding cities by factions, arguing that divided cities fall quickly under outside attack and that such methods prove weakness.
Then the author says some think a wise prince may foster animosity against himself in order to crush it and rise higher by the obstacle.
Then the author says princes, especially new ones, often find more fidelity in those first distrusted than in those first trusted.
Then the author sharply distinguishes two kinds of former supporters: those once hostile but now in need of the prince can be won firmly, while those who helped only from discontent are hard to satisfy.
Then the author turns to fortresses and says they may help or harm depending on whether the prince fears the people more or foreigners more.
Then the author says the castle of Milan harmed the Sforza more than other disorders did.
Then the author gives the strongest rule: the best fortress is not to be hated by the people.
Then the author says fortresses did help the Countess of Forli once against a popular assault, but failed later when the people were joined to foreigners.
Then the author concludes by praising both those who build and those who do not, while blaming any prince who trusts fortresses and cares little about popular hatred.

Frameworks or Terms Introduced:
- particulars test: no expedient is universally good apart from the state's condition
- ownership conversion: a device is useful when it turns subjects and arms into the prince's own strength
- peacetime illusion: factions and similar tricks may seem manageable in calm times but fail in war
- loyalty reversal: distrusted men can become better servants than secure flatterers
- fortress limit: physical defenses never erase the political condition of popular hatred

Specific Applications:
- distinguishing between giving trusted people real responsibility and stripping everyone of agency out of fear
- recognizing when internal rivalries are being tolerated as a management shortcut that will collapse under real external pressure
- identifying which former skeptics can become strong allies because they need to prove themselves
- testing whether a visible security measure solves the right threat or merely advertises distrust
- asking whether a leader is leaning on control devices because the deeper work of public trust has been neglected

Common Misreadings:
- treating the chapter as a random list of tactics with no unifying logic
- universalizing one rule about arming or disarming across all political conditions
- treating factional division as clever balance instead of weakness
- assuming former supporters are always better allies than former enemies
- treating fortresses as the final answer to insecurity
- missing that popular hatred can nullify even strong physical defenses

Counterarguments or Limits:
Modern use must preserve the chapter's case-by-case discipline. The point is not that leaders should mechanically arm, disarm, divide, reconcile, or fortify. The point is that each device must be judged by what kind of state it enters and which danger is actually decisive. The final rule about the people marks the limit of technical fixes: visible systems of control cannot rescue a leader who has already made himself politically hated.
