Core Claim:
This chapter argues that OKRs create power by forcing teams to focus on a small number of priorities and commit real effort to them. The chapter's job is to explain why saying yes to everything destroys execution and why commitment matters as much as selection. It should make focus feel like a discipline of sacrifice, not a tidy organizational preference.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
It is the first chapter that names one of OKRs' superpowers directly and explains the general operating logic behind focus and commitment before the company-specific stories that follow.

Author Logic Chain:
First the author explains that focus requires choosing what matters and dropping the rest.
Then the author shows that commitment turns chosen priorities into real operating pressure.
Then the author concludes that OKRs gain power when teams resist overload and live inside a small number of meaningful commitments.

Required Anchors:
- OKRs help teams focus on what matters most
- commitment means participation and buy-in rather than passive awareness
- too many priorities weaken execution even when the goals sound worthwhile

Frameworks or Terms Introduced:
focus; commitment; priority sacrifice; buy-in; overload; execution pressure.

Specific Applications:
- cut a crowded priority list down to the few outcomes that matter most
- distinguish awareness of a goal from real commitment to it
- recognize when goal overload has turned a system into noise

Common Misreadings:
- treating focus as merely reducing workload
- treating commitment as passive agreement
- assuming every worthy idea should remain active

Counterarguments or Limits:
The chapter should preserve the value of ambition while showing that ambition without sacrifice turns into overload. It should not imply that fewer priorities always means smaller goals.
