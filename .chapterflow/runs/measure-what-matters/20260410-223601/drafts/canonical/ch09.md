The Intuit story matters because it takes connection out of principle and places it inside a company case. After the alignment chapters, the open question is no longer whether teams should share direction. The harder question is whether a company can link adjacent work visibly enough that alignment survives execution instead of breaking into separate streams.

The first lesson in the case is that connection is not communication volume. It is not the moment when teams talk more often or repeat the same vocabulary. Connection begins when dependency handoffs and linked priorities become visible enough that teams can see how one stream of work changes another. A company can sound collaborative in meetings and still execute as disconnected streams if those links remain weak.

The Intuit case works because it shows connection as linked execution rather than as surface coordination. Real linkage changes what teams can see about adjacent work, where handoffs matter, and how one stream either supports or slows another. Without that operational visibility, teams can share direction in theory and still fragment in practice.

This also explains why fragmented streams matter so much in the chapter. Teams do not need conflict to disconnect. They only need to protect their local flow while the real links between streams remain vague. That is how a company keeps the language of coordination while execution breaks apart underneath.

The chapter should not be read as a call for one centralized stream of work. Forced centralization can imitate connection on the surface while flattening the distinct work different teams still need to do. The stronger point is that separate teams have to make their linkage visible enough for execution to stay connected.

The durable lesson is that connection becomes real when a company makes dependency handoffs and linked priorities visible enough to keep adjacent work streams moving together. That is what prevents alignment from becoming a cleaner description of fragmentation. Once that is clear, the next question follows. How do teams track and own whether that connected work is actually moving?
