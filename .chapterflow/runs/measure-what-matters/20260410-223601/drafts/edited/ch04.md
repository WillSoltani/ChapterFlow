A crowded goal system feels ambitious right up to the moment it has to execute. Teams can fill a roadmap, a planning board, or a quarterly memo with worthwhile aims and still weaken all of them at once. The problem is not that the goals are foolish. The problem is that worthiness does not create priority. When too many goals stay active, attention spreads, tradeoffs stay blurry, and nobody can tell what truly deserves the next unit of effort.

Focus matters as the first OKR superpower because it forces that choice. Focus is not a neat preference for simplicity. It is the discipline of choosing what matters most and letting other attractive work lose. A team has not focused merely because it renamed a long list. It has focused only when some plausible options are visibly no longer leading. Selection becomes real when sacrifice becomes real.

Commitment is the second half of the mechanism. A chosen priority does not gain force because people have heard it. It gains force when they participate in it, buy into it, and start organizing their behavior around it. Passive awareness keeps a goal socially visible. Commitment makes it operational. That difference matters because a team can recite the same objective in a meeting and still hedge its effort once the meeting ends.

Buy-in matters because execution pressure is social before it is mechanical. People need to know not only what has been chosen, but why it outranks the alternatives and what their role becomes now that the choice has been made. Without that, the system keeps old priorities alive underground. The language of commitment stays public while the real allocation of energy stays private.

Overload is the enemy in this chapter because too many priorities do not fail one by one. They weaken one another together. Each worthy goal borrows time, attention, and credibility from the others until the whole system starts sounding serious while doing less and less directed work. Overload turns an operating system into noise because every goal is now competing for first place without permission to admit it.

The edge of the chapter is that focus is not the opposite of ambition. Focus is what keeps ambition from dissolving into clutter. Commitment is not passive agreement. It is the earned participation that makes a chosen priority costly enough to guide action. Once that logic is clear, the next question follows naturally.
