The Nuna story matters because it takes commitment out of principle and places it inside a company case. After the focus chapters, the open question is no longer only what the company chose. The harder question is whether the company stayed bound to that choice once ordinary pressure returned. A priority can be selected cleanly and still lose force if the team treats it like a public statement instead of a carried obligation.

The first lesson in the case is that commitment is not awareness. It is not the moment when people hear the priority and agree that it sounds right. Commitment begins when the chosen work starts governing calendars, tradeoffs, and effort. The chapter's narrow company frame makes that distinction useful. A company can sound aligned in a meeting and still behave as if the old stack remains equally alive.

The Nuna case works because it shows commitment as burden rather than as branding. Real buy-in changes what a team carries, what it postpones, and what it stops protecting. Without that displacement, OKRs become decorative. The priorities remain visible, but they do not rule behavior strongly enough to matter.

This also explains why hedging matters so much in the chapter. Teams rarely reject priorities openly once they have agreed to them. They hedge instead. Old obligations keep their calendar space. Side work keeps its privileges. People leave the chosen objective on the slide while quietly protecting the work they trusted before. That is how symbolic alignment drains force from real commitment.

The chapter should not be read as a sermon about obedience. Forced compliance can imitate commitment on the surface while leaving buy-in hollow underneath. The stronger point is that commitment has to be costly enough to reorganize behavior without collapsing into pressure theater. People need to carry the choice, not merely submit to its language.

The durable lesson is narrower than the company name alone suggests. Commitment becomes real when a company lets chosen priorities govern behavior strongly enough to displace hedging and decorative agreement. That is what keeps focus from becoming temporary. Once that is clear, the next question follows. How do multiple teams align and connect once they are truly committed to what matters?
