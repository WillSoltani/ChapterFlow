After the Part 2 transition explains continuous performance management and CFRs, the next step is to see that shift inside a named company story. The Adobe chapter matters because it turns the move away from annual reviews into an operational case rather than leaving it as a conceptual argument.

The chapter's claim should stay narrow and practical. Performance gets stronger when old annual-review logic gives way to more ongoing conversation, feedback, and recognition around the work. That is what makes this the first company case for Part 2. The issue is not whether formal review disappears entirely. The issue is whether the system becomes more useful while the work is still alive.

The chapter also has to preserve a key limit from Chapter 15. Leaving annual reviews behind is not the same as lowering standards or replacing discipline with positivity. A softer tone is weaker than a stronger operating system. The chapter should therefore show that continuous management changes the cadence and quality of performance support rather than erasing accountability.

That is why the story belongs here. Chapter 15 defined the larger system conceptually. The Adobe story should make that system feel practical. A static annual review can summarize a period, but it is weaker than useful conversation and feedback that help people improve while the work is still moving.

This makes the chapter a practical test of CFR-like support. If the shift is real, then ongoing conversation, feedback, and recognition become more central to how performance is managed. If the shift is only rhetorical, then the chapter would collapse into HR language without changing the actual support around the work.

The durable lesson is that continuous performance management becomes practical when a company relies less on static annual-review logic and more on useful ongoing support around performance. That is what the Adobe story should prove. Once that case is established, the next chapter can extend the same logic into another company story.
