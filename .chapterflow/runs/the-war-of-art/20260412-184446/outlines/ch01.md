Chapter Promise:
The reader will understand Resistance as the recurring inner enemy that attacks meaningful work before it begins and will feel why naming it matters.

Opening Move:
Open on a person who has prepared, organized, and postponed everything except the actual page that would commit them to the work.

Anchor Allocation:
- anchor 1 used in paragraph(s): Resistance named explicitly in P2 and sharpened in P3
- anchor 2 used in paragraph(s): blank page / unstarted project pressure in P1 and P4
- anchor 3 used in paragraph(s): Hitler anecdote and preview warning in P5

Paragraph Job Map:
- P1: anchor. A concrete scene of avoidance right before meaningful work begins.
- P2: mechanism. Name Resistance and distinguish it from ordinary inconvenience.
- P3: implication. Show how Resistance clusters fear, procrastination, and rationalization into one frame.
- P4: tension. Explain why the work that matters most attracts the strongest pushback.
- P5: limit plus anchor. Use the Hitler anecdote narrowly as a memorable warning about catastrophic evasion.
- P6: bridge. Land the consequence and hand the reader the next question about how to fight.

Required Distinction or Mechanism:
Resistance is not a random bad mood. It is a repeatable inner adversary that intensifies around real callings and disguises itself through excuses that sound reasonable.

Hard-Depth Tension:
The hard variant must keep the frame severe while admitting its limit: the enemy metaphor clarifies recurring avoidance, but it becomes false if it pretends to explain every struggle with total certainty.

Threshold Question:
How do you use the enemy-frame to recognize sabotage without turning it into melodrama or self-hatred?
