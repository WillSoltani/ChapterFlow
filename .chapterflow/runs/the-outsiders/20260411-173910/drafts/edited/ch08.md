Warren Buffett enters the book where outsider rationality becomes clearest and easiest to mythologize. Smith's chapter showed disciplined allocation surviving inside family-control continuity. Buffett keeps the allocation logic but strips away much of that ambiguity. The title itself names the challenge: the investor as CEO. That means the chapter is not mainly about celebrity investing or a list of great bets. It is about whether investor logic can become the central operating logic of a company.

That is why the Berkshire setting matters. The frozen support is narrow but enough. Berkshire Hathaway is framed as a diversified holding company with a decentralized operating structure, and Buffett is framed as its long-time leader. Thorndike's interest is not merely that Buffett allocates capital well. It is that he makes capital allocation the governing management act while leaving operating responsibility distributed. The center judges where capital belongs without trying to run every local decision directly.

This changes the scorecard in a more integrated way than earlier cases did. Murphy, Singleton, Anders, Malone, Graham, Stiritz, and Smith each showed one version of rational leadership. Buffett brings investor thinking and operator responsibility into the same frame. The chapter only works if those roles are fused rather than merely adjacent. The investor is not sitting outside the business making clever observations. The investor logic is running the firm.

That is why decentralization becomes the chapter's first mechanism rather than a side note. A broad holding company can easily turn into neglect if headquarters becomes passive. It can also turn into managerial sprawl if the center tries to micromanage everything. Buffett matters if he avoids both errors. Local operators keep real autonomy, but the center still judges capital use with enough discipline that the whole company behaves coherently.

Holding-company discipline sharpens that point. Scale and diversification do not deserve respect by themselves. Berkshire only matters if breadth is governed by rational ownership logic rather than by prestige, appetite, or headquarters theater. Thorndike's stronger claim is that investor discipline can calm a large company down instead of making it more frenetic. Capital allocation becomes the main act, and decentralization becomes credible because it is paired with central judgment rather than with abdication.

There is an obvious risk in how readers approach the case. Buffett's reputation can make the chapter feel settled before it starts. The frozen support does not justify that shortcut. The anti-celebrity rule has to stay active. Fame is not the mechanism. The mechanism is investor-operator fusion, decentralized trust, and a long-range ownership scorecard that still demands rational capital use.

That is why the chapter belongs here, just before the final synthesis. The book has tested outsider rationality under operating austerity, tactical adaptation, turnaround severity, structural complexity, reluctant succession, public-company rigor, and family-control continuity. Buffett gathers the logic into its cleanest case. The next chapter can distill the outsider mind-set only because Buffett makes the central pattern easier to see: rational capital allocation is not separate from leadership. In this case, it is leadership.
