The book's third case changes the pressure completely. Murphy showed how a lean operator compounds value from a disciplined machine. Singleton showed how a rational allocator changes tools when markets change. Bill Anders arrives in a harsher setting. General Dynamics is not a healthy platform looking for a clever edge. It is a turnaround. That matters because the usual language of growth, momentum, and confidence no longer fits. The live question is whether outsider discipline can survive the uglier work of shrinking, pruning, and resetting a company that has already become too heavy for its own economics.

That changes the scorecard at once. In a turnaround, size can stop being an asset and start becoming a burden. Legacy breadth can look impressive right up to the point where it traps capital, attention, and managerial pride inside the wrong shape. Thorndike's chapter logic, as frozen here, points toward portfolio triage and rational downsizing. That means Anders is not being asked to decorate a large enterprise with better slogans. He is being asked to decide what the company should stop being if it wants to become valuable again.

The outsider element matters for a specific reason. Anders comes in as a late-arriving executive rather than as a leader whose identity is fused with General Dynamics's inherited form. That distance does not make him automatically right. It does make colder judgment more possible. Insiders often have emotional and reputational reasons to defend old scale. Units, categories, and legacies start to feel sacred simply because they are already there. A turnaround punishes that instinct. The company does not need loyalty to its previous silhouette. It needs loyalty to recovery.

That is why portfolio triage becomes the chapter's central mechanism. Triage is uncomfortable because it forces ranking. Some activities deserve capital, attention, and time. Others do not, even if they once carried prestige. In a damaged company, refusing to rank can be a form of denial. Protecting everything usually means weakening everything. Anders's case matters because it treats pruning not as surrender but as a rational attempt to restore strength by stripping away the parts that no longer justify their claim on the whole.

The hard part is that rational shrinkage looks like failure to many observers. A leader who keeps every unit, every project, and every symbol of historical scale can still sound brave for a while. A leader who cuts, exits, or sells can look frightened, destructive, or overly financial. Thorndike's chapter pushes against that surface reading. In a turnaround, defending size can be the sentimental move. Shrinking can be the harder form of discipline if it is tied to a sharper account of value, survivability, and future strength.

That boundary matters because cutting is easy to glorify after the fact. Not every downsizing move is intelligent. A smaller company is not automatically a better one. Triage can become panic, vanity, or liquidation theater if the underlying scorecard is vague. The chapter only stays serious if the shrinkage serves recovery rather than merely producing the optics of toughness. Anders is useful here not as a mascot for slash-and-burn management, but as a test case for whether pruning can be both severe and rational.

The chapter also widens the book's moral terrain. Murphy and Singleton both operate in ways that can be admired with relatively low emotional friction. A turnaround is different. Rational decisions may wound pride, identity, and institutional memory. The chapter should not flatten that cost. Outsider discipline can look clean on a spreadsheet while feeling brutal inside an organization. Thorndike's interest is not cruelty. It is the claim that sentimentality about inherited scale can become its own kind of irresponsibility when the company is already weak.

That is why this case sits where it does. After Singleton, the reader has learned that tactics can change while principles stay fixed. Anders forces a tougher question: what if the principle now demands that the company itself become smaller? The next chapter shifts again. Malone will make complexity and leverage look potentially productive in a fast-moving industry. Anders first clears the ground by showing that outsider rationality is not married to expansion. Sometimes the disciplined move is to cut the shape down until the economics can breathe again.
