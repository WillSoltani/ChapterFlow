Teledyne had become so identified with unconventional behavior that a dividend could make news. Thorndike opens there for a reason. He wants Henry Singleton to feel strange before he feels admirable. A normal-looking corporate move lands like a surprise because the company has spent years refusing the standard script. That opening matters because it tells you this chapter is not about a tidy management doctrine. It is about a CEO who kept changing tools without losing the logic underneath them.

The logic starts with a different scorecard. Singleton is not judged here by the usual trophies of size, revenue growth, or the smooth story managers like to tell about momentum. Thorndike's frame is per-share value. That shift sounds technical, but it changes the whole pressure of the job. If value per share is the real standard, then empire building stops looking noble by default. Bigger is not automatically better. Activity is not automatically progress. A CEO can look disciplined in public while quietly diluting the thing that matters.

That is where the institutional imperative enters. Thorndike uses the phrase to name a familiar weakness in management life: leaders feel pulled toward scale, convention, and the choices that are easiest to defend in polite company. Singleton's importance is that he keeps resisting that gravity. He is willing to look odd if the odd move serves value better than the respectable one. That does not make him erratic. It makes him harder to imitate, because most managers want the protection of recognizable logic even when it creates weaker results.

The chapter's sharpest move is to show that Singleton did not worship one tactic forever. In the 1960s, Teledyne used acquisitions funded by equity when that tool made sense in the market it was facing. Later, when valuations and capital conditions changed, the company leaned hard into share repurchases instead. That shift is the heart of the chapter. Singleton is not loyal to acquisitions as identity, and he is not loyal to buybacks as ideology. He is loyal to value creation under current facts.

That makes the Keynes line more than a clever epigraph. "I change my mind when the facts change. What do you do?" sounds witty until you notice how much executive life punishes that posture. Changing your mind in public can look weak, inconsistent, or opportunistic. Holding to a stale plan can look strong because it is legible. Singleton's case shows the opposite. Under a real value standard, refusing to change can become the weaker move. Discipline is not frozen consistency. It is consistency at the level of principle while tactics keep updating.

That distinction matters because flexibility is easy to fake. Anyone can call a reversal adaptive after the fact. The chapter only works because Singleton's changes are tied to a stable yardstick. Per-share value stays constant even as the tools change. That is what separates evidence-responsive leadership from fashionable improvisation. If the standard drifts every time the tactic drifts, the manager is not adapting. He is hiding.

The risk runs the other way too. A leader can become so proud of being unconventional that contrarianism turns into vanity. Singleton does not matter because he was different. He matters because difference stayed subordinate to valuation, opportunity, and return. The unconventional choice only deserves respect when it serves a stronger discipline beneath it. Otherwise, it is just a more exotic version of executive ego.

That is why this chapter widens the book's argument. Murphy showed how outsider rationality can run a focused operating company. Singleton shows what happens when the same rationality has to keep reallocating across a changing portfolio and a changing market. The question is no longer whether a leader can stay disciplined. It is whether discipline can survive tactical change without collapsing into drift. The next case pushes the tension harder by asking what outsider judgment looks like when the company itself needs a turnaround rather than just a smarter allocation engine.
