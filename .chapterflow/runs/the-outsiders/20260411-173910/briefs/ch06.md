Core Claim:
Bill Stiritz's chapter argues that outsider rationality can make a public company behave with owner-like discipline usually associated with a leveraged buyout. The edge is not financial costume. It is forcing portfolio focus, operating rigor, and capital decisions to answer to the same hard scorecard.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
This chapter brings the book back into overtly economic discipline after Graham's succession story. Stiritz matters because he makes public ownership no excuse for softness. The chapter should teach focus, pruning, and owner-like pressure without pretending the public-company setting disappears.

Author Logic Chain:
First the author frames Ralston Purina as a company that had drifted into unfocused sprawl.
Then the author positions Stiritz as a manager willing to tighten the company rather than preserve the inherited collection.
Then the author shows that portfolio pruning and operating rigor can create buyout-like economics without taking the company private.
Finally the author warns that public-market discipline fails whenever focus becomes a slogan instead of a real scorecard.

Required Anchors:
- Ralston Purina is introduced as a company needing sharper focus; the friction is inherited sprawl, the consequence is that capital and attention are being diluted.
- Stiritz is framed around pruning, tightening, and owner-like pressure; the friction is that public companies often tolerate softer standards, the consequence is that discipline itself becomes the outsider edge.
- The chapter's supported intent centers on public-LBO logic; the friction is that the company remains public, the consequence is that management must import toughness without relying on private ownership as a crutch.
