Core Claim:
Buffett's chapter argues that outsider rationality reaches its clearest form when investor logic and operator logic become the same management craft. The edge is not stock-picking glamour. It is capital allocation, decentralized trust, and long-range ownership discipline operating as the CEO's main job.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
This chapter is the book's cleanest synthesis case before the final principle chapter. Buffett matters because he fuses the investor's scorecard with the operator's responsibility, making capital allocation the central management system rather than a side function.

Author Logic Chain:
First the author frames Berkshire Hathaway as a diversified holding structure where capital allocation is unusually central.
Then the author positions Buffett as both investor and chief executive rather than as one role borrowing language from the other.
Then the author shows that decentralization can coexist with strong central judgment about capital.
Finally the author warns that investor rhetoric becomes empty when it loses touch with operating trust and ownership discipline.

Required Anchors:
- Berkshire Hathaway is presented as a diversified holding company; the friction is that decentralization can drift into passivity without a rational center, the consequence is that capital allocation becomes the governing management act.
- Buffett is framed as a CEO whose investor logic shapes the firm; the friction is that investors and operators often think in different rhythms, the consequence is that his unusual value comes from unifying them.
- The chapter's supported intent centers on investor-operator fusion; the friction is that reputation can obscure the actual mechanism, the consequence is that the scorecard has to stay tied to allocation, trust, and long-range ownership.
