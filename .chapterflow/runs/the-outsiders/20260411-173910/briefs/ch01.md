Core Claim:
Tom Murphy built extraordinary value not by looking like a grand strategist, but by treating capital allocation, operating autonomy, and cost discipline as one integrated machine. The chapter argues that great CEOs create per-share value when they stay allergic to bureaucracy, keep local managers close to revenue, and wait patiently for a few decisive bets that fit the same disciplined logic.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
This chapter is not a generic profile of a media executive. It is about a tiny company that kept outcompeting much larger rivals because Murphy and Dan Burke paired austere operating habits with unusually rational capital allocation. The ABC acquisition matters not as a spectacle, but as the visible payoff of years of disciplined compounding.

Author Logic Chain:
First the author frames Murphy as the head of a much smaller company competing against giants.
Then the author shows that Capital Cities kept its center lean, delegated heavily, and demanded local responsibility.
Then the author links that operating style to disciplined capital allocation and patient dealmaking.
Finally the author uses the ABC acquisition as proof that the same logic could scale into a massive move without abandoning discipline.

Required Anchors:
- Capital Cities began far smaller than the dominant broadcast giants; the friction is scale disadvantage, the consequence is that management quality had to carry more weight than size.
- Tom Murphy and Dan Burke ran the company with lean headquarters, tight cost discipline, and strong local autonomy; the friction is avoiding bureaucracy while maintaining standards, the consequence is durable operating strength and capital flexibility.
- The acquisition of ABC serves as the visible payoff; the friction is that Capital Cities looked like the smaller player, the consequence is that disciplined compounding enabled a surprising takeover.
