Core Claim:
Henry Singleton's edge was not simply that he ran a conglomerate well. It was that he kept changing capital-allocation tactics when market conditions changed instead of forcing one doctrine forever. The chapter argues that rational CEOs are judged by per-share value creation, not by size, and that Singleton beat the institutional imperative by shifting from acquisition-heavy growth in one era to massive repurchases in another.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
This chapter is not a broad pro-conglomerate defense. It is about adaptive capital allocation under changing facts. Singleton matters because he did not treat acquisitions, buybacks, or dividends as identity markers. He treated them as tools whose value changed with price, capital markets, and opportunity.

Author Logic Chain:
First the author frames Teledyne as a midsize conglomerate with a reputation for unconventional behavior.
Then the author shows that Singleton should be judged by per-share value creation rather than headline growth.
Then the author traces a strategic shift: acquisitive expansion funded by equity in one market regime, then heavy repurchases when markets undervalued the company.
Finally the author uses Singleton to attack the institutional imperative and show how rationality can require changing your mind in public.

Required Anchors:
- Teledyne is introduced as a midsize conglomerate with unconventional behavior; the friction is that the public expects normal dividend behavior and standard corporate habits, the consequence is that Singleton immediately reads as an outlier.
- Singleton's record is framed around capital deployment and per-share value rather than size; the friction is conventional CEO scorekeeping, the consequence is a different standard of excellence.
- Thorndike's abstracted chapter logic shows a shift from acquisitions funded by equity in the 1960s to heavy repurchases in the 1970s and 1980s; the friction is changing market conditions, the consequence is that flexibility beats doctrinal consistency.

Frameworks or Terms Introduced:
- per-share value: the real scorecard is value per share, not empire size
- institutional imperative: managers drift toward scale, convention, and defensible imitation
- adaptive capital allocation: acquisitions, repurchases, or dividends only make sense relative to price and opportunity
- market-regime sensitivity: the right move changes when facts and valuations change

Specific Applications:
- changing strategy when the environment changes instead of defending an old plan
- judging managers by value creation per unit rather than surface growth
- refusing symbolic consistency when conditions now reward a different tool

Common Misreadings:
