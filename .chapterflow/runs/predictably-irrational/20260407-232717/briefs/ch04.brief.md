# Chapter Brief

- Chapter: 4 The Cost of Social Norms
- Chapter ID: ch04-the-cost-of-social-norms
- Source IDs: unt_catalog_toc, wikipedia_summary, supersummary_guides, duke_papers_index
- Paper Hints: Effort for Payment
- Objective: Ariely uses the split between social norms and market norms to show that money can change behavior by changing the category of the relationship itself. A favor, a shared obligation, or a gift can call up reciprocity, identity, and generosity. A price, a fee, or a small payment can call up a colder calculation about whether the exchange is worth it. The striking result is that tiny payments can perform worse than no payment at all because they crowd out the stronger social motivation that was already carrying the effort. Ariely supports this through the small-payment experiments, the AARP lawyer example, and the daycare late-pickup fines that made parents later by turning guilt into a paid option. The chapter's claim is not that money is evil or that market norms are always wrong. Market norms work when the relationship is supposed to be transactional. The danger comes from mixing the systems badly or trying to switch back after pricing an interaction that used to run on trust, care, or obligation. Keep the distinction alive, keep the examples concrete, and end with the practical discipline: choose the frame on purpose, avoid token payments and symbolic fines in social settings, and use clear market terms only when you truly want a market exchange.

## Keep Visible
- Social norms run on reciprocity, belonging, and identity rather than explicit price.
- Market norms run on wages, fees, contracts, and stated exchange rates.
- A tiny payment can reduce effort because it replaces social motivation with a weak market signal.
- Once market norms enter, they can be hard to reverse back into social trust.
- The chapter warns against mixing norms badly, not against all uses of money.
