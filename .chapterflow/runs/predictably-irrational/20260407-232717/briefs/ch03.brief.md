# Chapter Brief

- Chapter: 3 The Cost of Zero Cost
- Chapter ID: ch03-the-cost-of-zero-cost
- Source IDs: unt_catalog_toc, wikipedia_summary, supersummary_guides, duke_papers_index
- Paper Hints: Zero as a Special Price
- Objective: Ariely uses the candy experiment to show that free is psychologically different from merely cheap. When a Lindt truffle and a Hershey's Kiss both cost something, many people prefer the better chocolate. When each price drops by one cent and the Kiss becomes free, preference reverses dramatically even though the quality relationship did not change. That reversal gives the chapter its mechanism. Zero price does not simply reduce cost; it removes the felt downside and creates an emotional bonus that crowds out net-value thinking. Ariely widens the point through free shipping, trial offers, bonus add-ons, and giveaway logic that make people chase the word free while ignoring time, clutter, lock-in, and total spend. The chapter's warning is not that every free offer is a trap. It is that zero price weakens the normal comparison process people would use if both options carried even a tiny visible cost. The practical defense is to reinsert the missing comparison. Ask what you would do if each option cost one cent more, whether the free offer is solving a real problem, and what hidden costs arrive with the emotional rush of paying nothing. Keep the chapter specific to zero-price distortion rather than broad consumer temptation.

## Keep Visible
- Free is a special psychological category, not just a larger discount.
- The candy experiment matters because a one-cent shift creates a major preference reversal.
- Zero hides opportunity cost, future cost, and clutter by removing the sting of loss.
- Marketers exploit the effect with shipping thresholds, trials, bundles, and bonus items.
- The chapter's defense is to run the one-cent test and restore net-value comparison.
