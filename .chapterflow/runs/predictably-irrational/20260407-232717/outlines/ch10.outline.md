# Chapter Outline

## Opening
- Ariely uses placebo-style price effects because they show that cost can change felt effectiveness.

## Core Moves
- Price can act as an expectation signal rather than as a neutral number.
- Higher-priced identical treatments or products can feel more effective.
- The mechanism crosses medicine, supplements, consumer products, and status-labeled experiences.
- The chapter keeps an ethical tension alive because the same effect can reassure or manipulate.
- The practical lesson is to separate actual efficacy from price-driven expectation when possible.

## Reinforcement
- The Wellness Tool That Felt Smarter After the Enterprise Price Tag (decision_point, work)
- The Campus Energy Drink Trial That Seemed Stronger at Full Price (postmortem, school)
- The Conversation About Why the Expensive Pain Patch Felt Faster (dialogue, personal)
- The Training Program That Looked More Effective Once HR Called It Premium (predict_reveal, work)
- The Tutor Package Dilemma When Price Started Standing In For Quality (dilemma, school)
- Before and After the Household Hid Prices During Product Tests (before_after, personal)

## Exit
- If price and expectations can bend felt effectiveness, what happens when moral behavior itself changes with context rather than fixed character?
