# The High Price of Ownership

The High Price of Ownership says that once people own or imagine owning something, they value it more than nonowners do. Ariely uses the endowment effect, the seller-buyer gap, and Duke-ticket style examples of inflated asking prices to prove that the pattern is not just intuition.

What makes the chapter useful is the risk it isolates: ownership, effort, and imagination make exchange harder and distort fair value. Once that pressure appears, people stop noticing the real tradeoff and start obeying the frame or state that got there first.

The chapter then extends the mechanism into ordinary decisions across selling, buying, attachment. That reach matters because the distortion is not confined to the original experiment.

Ariely's practical answer is to compare from the outside and ask what you would pay if you did not already own it. The chapter's limit is equally important: the chapter does not deny attachment; it warns against mistaking attachment for market value.
