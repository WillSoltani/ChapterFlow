# The Cost of Social Norms

The Cost of Social Norms says that social norms and market norms motivate effort through different meanings. Ariely uses the moving-help example, token-pay experiments, and the daycare fine that increased lateness to prove that the pattern is not just intuition.

What makes the chapter useful is the risk it isolates: small payments can crowd out goodwill and turn moral pressure into purchasable permission. Once that pressure appears, people stop noticing the real tradeoff and start obeying the frame or state that got there first.

The chapter then extends the mechanism into ordinary decisions across favors, volunteering, fees. That reach matters because the distortion is not confined to the original experiment.

Ariely's practical answer is to decide whether an interaction should stay relational or become clearly transactional before adding money. The chapter's limit is equally important: the chapter is not anti-money; it warns that money changes the category of the relationship.
