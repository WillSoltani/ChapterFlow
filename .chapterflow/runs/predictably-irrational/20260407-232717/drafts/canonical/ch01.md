# The Truth about Relativity

The chapter opens with The Economist subscription offer because it reveals how relative choice works. Ariely shows three options: web only, print only, and web plus print. The print-only option is not there to be chosen. It is there to make the bundle look superior by creating an easy comparison. Remove that decoy and many people switch back toward the cheaper web option. That result matters because it demonstrates that preferences are not as stable as they feel. People often choose by contrast, not by intrinsic worth. Ariely expands the argument through cases involving vacation packages, consumer products, and status comparison. The brain likes options that are easy to compare, and once one option dominates another nearby option, the decision starts to feel rational. But the frame is doing more work than the chooser notices. This is why relativity is both useful and dangerous. It reduces effort, yet it also invites manipulation and breeds envy. People compare salaries, houses, and lifestyles against the nearest visible benchmark, then act as if that benchmark is naturally important. A shopper who was satisfied with a midrange item can feel deprived after a premium option appears, even if the shopper's needs never changed. Ariely's correction is not to eliminate comparison altogether. It is to choose comparisons deliberately and to ask whether the visible comparison is helping or hijacking judgment. Relevant options need to be built into the frame before the easy contrast hardens into a conclusion. If the wrong benchmark becomes the default, people can end up solving the seller's question or the neighbor's question instead of their own. The chapter therefore turns comparison into something that must itself be managed, not merely obeyed. That extra step is what keeps convenience from quietly becoming command. It also restores some authorship to the chooser. In other words, the smartest move may be frame selection, not option selection. That is where the chapter becomes practical and defensive. Small pauses before comparing are often more valuable than louder confidence afterward.

## Key Takeaways
- A decoy changes choice by making one comparison simpler than the full decision.
- Relative judgment is a shortcut for life without a stable internal value scale.
- Social comparison imports new standards and then treats them as natural.
- The clearest-looking choice can still be frame-dependent rather than value-dependent.
- Good judgment includes curating the reference set.
