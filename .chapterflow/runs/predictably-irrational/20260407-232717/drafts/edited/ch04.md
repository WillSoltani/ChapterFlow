# The Cost of Social Norms

Chapter 4 works because Ariely does not argue that money is inherently corrupting. He argues something tighter and more useful. Social norms and market norms run on different logics, and small prices can push people out of the first system into the second. A favor, a shared obligation, or a gift can invite warmth, trust, and reciprocal effort. A token wage or symbolic fine can make the same act feel like a cold transaction and shrink the motivation that used to make it work. That is why tiny payments can underperform free help and why daycare fines can make parents later instead of earlier. Once the exchange gets priced, guilt changes into calculation and generosity gets replaced by "is this worth it?" The chapter's practical lesson is to stop mixing the systems casually. If you want a social exchange, protect it with gratitude, mission, and identity. If you want a market exchange, make the terms clear and fair. What fails is the muddled middle, where a relationship that depended on trust gets offered a number too small to compensate and large enough to change the meaning of the act.

## Key Takeaway Card
- The chapter's key move is to protect social cooperation from token prices that turn shared obligation into weak market exchange.
