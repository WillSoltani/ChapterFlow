# The Fallacy of Supply and Demand

Chapter 2 is Ariely's case against the comforting idea that willingness to pay simply reveals stable demand. He opens with Tahitian black pearls because they show how value can be constructed through an opening price. Those pearls were not self-evidently precious to buyers at first. They became luxury goods after marketers placed them beside rare gems, surrounded them with elite symbolism, and attached them to strikingly high prices. The first number was not a neutral description. It was an anchor that taught buyers how to see the product.

Ariely calls the broader pattern arbitrary coherence. The first price can be arbitrary, but once it enters the system, later judgments become coherent around it. That coherence is powerful because it feels like evidence of stable preference. Buyers can compare later prices, rank alternatives, and defend their decisions in ways that sound rational. Yet the whole structure may still depend on a first number that had little claim to natural authority.

The experiments extend the argument beyond anecdotes. Ariely shows that arbitrary starting numbers can bend what people will pay for unfamiliar items. He also shows that early offers influence how much money people later demand to hear unpleasant sounds again. The reason those studies matter is that they strip away the excuse that participants were simply reporting well-known market values. When value is ambiguous, the first anchor provides borrowed judgment, and later choices recruit that judgment as if it were internally generated.

This is where the chapter confronts standard supply-and-demand theory. Classical stories treat demand as though it exists in a stable form before the market reveals it. Ariely argues that demand is often partly produced by historical anchors, framing, and earlier commitments. The path matters. People do not only ask what something is worth. They also carry forward what it cost before, what comparable items cost, and what initial range they were taught to expect.

The chapter becomes practical when it moves from markets to personal life. Salary histories, housing budgets, rates for professional services, and even the sense of what counts as a normal splurge can all become coherent around inherited anchors. Once that happens, later decisions feel disciplined because they fit the chain. Ariely's remedy is not magical independence. It is anchor inspection. Ask where the first number came from, what interests shaped it, whether the good was familiar enough for the anchor to deserve trust, and what independent estimate you would make without the inherited reference point. That move matters because coherence is not proof. A later price logic can be neat, socially accepted, and still rest on a weak beginning. Chapter 2 works as a warning against mistaking historical consistency for objective value. The first number often writes more of the story than the buyer notices. Old anchors can keep speaking long after the original market conditions are gone. That is why resetting the frame can be more useful than polishing a later comparison. The deeper issue is not price alone but inherited judgment.

## Key Takeaway Card
- The first anchor often shapes later willingness to pay more than people realize.
