# The Cost of Zero Cost

Ariely uses the chocolate experiment because it exposes a sharp preference reversal created by the word free. When the truffle and the Kiss both cost something, quality still has a fighting chance. When the cheaper option becomes free, the comparison changes its emotional structure. Zero does not feel like a normal price cut. It feels like downside disappeared. That is why the chapter is stronger than a simple lesson about bargains. Ariely is showing that people stop computing net value with the same discipline when the price crosses into zero. Shipping promotions, trial offers, free add-ons, and samples all ride that same mechanism. The free offer captures attention while opportunity cost, future commitment, clutter, and wasted time recede into the background. The chapter's limit matters too. Ariely is not arguing that free is always foolish. He is arguing that free deserves more scrutiny because it weakens the very comparison process that would normally protect the chooser. The practical move is to restore the missing contrast. Ask what you would do if the free item cost one cent, whether the paid alternative would look stronger again, and what real costs arrive once the emotional relief of paying nothing fades. That is the chapter's durable lesson. Zero feels safe, but it often disables exactly the judgment people think they are exercising.

## Key Takeaway Card
- The chapter's key move is to reinsert comparison whenever free makes a weak option feel irresistible.
