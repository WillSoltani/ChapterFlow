# The Truth about Relativity

The opening subscription offer from The Economist does heavy work because it shows that relative comparison is often mistaken for clear judgment. Ariely gives readers three options: web only, print only, and web plus print for the same price as print only. The middle option exists as a decoy. It is not supposed to win. It is supposed to make the bundle look dominant. Once that comparison becomes easy, many people stop evaluating the full decision and start rewarding the apparently smarter side of the easy comparison. That result matters because it undermines the assumption that preferences are already stable and waiting to be revealed. Often the frame helps construct the preference. Ariely's larger claim is that relativity is not an occasional error layered on top of rational choice. It is a core decision shortcut in a world where most things do not arrive with an internal value meter. People compare because absolute judgment is expensive. They compare apartments to nearby apartments, salaries to nearby salaries, and subscription plans to the options placed beside them. That shortcut is adaptive, but it is not neutral. Once a comparison set is chosen, it starts steering what feels reasonable, generous, expensive, or attractive. This is why a decoy can shift purchases and why social comparison can shift satisfaction without any underlying change in objective conditions. The reference point does the work. Ariely's correction is not to ban comparison, which would be impossible and foolish. It is to treat the reference set as part of the decision rather than as invisible background. The chapter therefore has a built-in limit. Relativity helps choice happen, yet the very mechanism that helps can also be manipulated. The same cognitive economy that keeps ordinary life moving creates openings for menus, status systems, and market designs that exploit easy comparison. The practical discipline is to interrogate the frame: Why are these the options? Which comparison is being made easy? What relevant alternative is missing? That move creates distance between the chooser and the architecture of the choice. Without it, people often confuse a clean comparison with a sound decision. It also explains why a new benchmark can make a once-satisfying option feel suddenly inadequate. The chapter becomes especially strong when it is applied outside shopping. Once a colleague's compensation, a neighbor's renovation, or a prestige option becomes the nearest visible benchmark, people can begin optimizing against a contest they never consciously accepted. They may start spending more, resenting more, or chasing a goal they never previously valued. A disciplined chooser therefore has to manage benchmarks, not just prices. That means stepping back before the choice feels urgent and asking which comparison actually deserves authority. It also means noticing when a comparison is creating pressure without adding understanding. That is the broader warning. Relative judgment is ordinary and often necessary, but uninspected relativity can quietly recruit us into values and comparisons we did not mean to adopt. That is the chapter's durable limit and lesson.

## Key Takeaway Card
- The chapter's key move is to inspect the comparison frame before trusting the choice inside it.
