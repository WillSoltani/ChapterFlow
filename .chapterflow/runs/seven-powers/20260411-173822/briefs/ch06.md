Core Claim:
Cornered resource exists when a firm gains preferential access on attractive terms to a coveted resource that independently raises value. The barrier is not merely owning something nice. It is controlling access to an input that materially matters while rivals cannot secure equivalent terms or a close substitute.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
This chapter is the cleanest warning against confusing ordinary good assets with strategic cornering. It requires the resource to matter on its own and to be meaningfully unavailable or unattractive to rivals. The power comes from constrained access, not from generic competence.

Author Logic Chain:
First the author defines cornered resource as preferential access to a coveted input.
Then he stresses that the input must independently raise value.
Then he explains that the advantage depends on rivals lacking equivalent access on comparable terms.
Then he clarifies that not every good asset or capable team counts as cornered.
Then he leaves the reader with the practical test: if rivals could get the same resource or a close substitute, would the advantage survive?

Frameworks or Terms Introduced:
Cornered resource: privileged access to an important scarce input.
Independent value: the resource matters even before the firm's surrounding system is considered.
Equivalent access test: rivals must be unable to secure comparable terms or substitutes.
False corner: an apparently special asset that rivals can match without much difficulty.

Specific Applications:
- testing whether a scarce input is truly strategic
- separating patent or talent stories with real scarcity from ordinary good execution
- asking whether a rival could secure a close substitute without much loss

Common Misreadings:
- treating any strong team or good supplier as a cornered resource
- ignoring whether the resource independently matters
- assuming scarcity without checking equivalent access or substitution

Counterarguments or Limits:
Cornered resource weakens when substitutes are plentiful, when rivals can buy similar access on comparable terms, or when the asset matters only inside a broader system rather than independently. Hard depth should preserve the test that scarcity alone is insufficient if the resource does not raise value on its own.
