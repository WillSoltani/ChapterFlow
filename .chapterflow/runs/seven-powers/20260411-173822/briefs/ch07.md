Core Claim:
Process power is rare. It appears when a deeply embedded set of interlocking activities yields lower cost or better product, and rivals would need prolonged commitment to match it. The barrier is not a single trick or best practice. It is the difficulty of reproducing the whole coordinated system.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
This chapter is the cleanest warning against mistaking ordinary operational excellence for strategic power. It requires an interlocking activity set whose parts reinforce one another and cannot be copied quickly in isolation. The power sits in the system, not in one visible move.

Author Logic Chain:
First the author defines process power as an embedded interlocking activity system.
Then he shows that the system must yield lower cost or better product.
Then he explains that rivals would need prolonged commitment to replicate the whole thing.
Then he clarifies that ordinary efficiency improvements or isolated best practices do not qualify.
Then he leaves the reader with the practical test: if a rival copied one piece, would the advantage survive because the rest of the system still matters?

Frameworks or Terms Introduced:
Process power: advantage produced by an interlocking operating system.
Interlocking activities: mutually reinforcing routines, standards, and methods that work together.
System replication burden: the difficulty of copying the whole system rather than one visible element.
False process story: ordinary operational competence mistaken for strategic process power.

Specific Applications:
- separating true system-level process power from generic efficiency
- testing whether copying one visible practice would still leave the rival far behind
- explaining why rare process power takes long commitment to replicate

Common Misreadings:
- calling any well-run operation process power
- treating one best practice as if it were the whole moat
- ignoring the prolonged commitment needed to copy the broader system

Counterarguments or Limits:
Process power weakens when the activities are loosely connected, when rivals can copy the visible routines without rebuilding a broader system, or when the advantage is really just temporary execution quality. Hard depth should preserve the rarity claim and the difference between system power and ordinary competence.
