Core Claim:
Switching costs arise when customers stay because leaving would destroy value they have built up with the incumbent. The barrier is not admiration or habit by itself. It is the loss of financial, procedural, or relational value that would be paid during the move to a rival. The incumbent benefits because the customer must compare the rival's offer against the cost of abandoning accumulated compatibility, routines, or trust.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
This chapter is the cleanest explanation of lock-in without collapsing into vague loyalty talk. It forces the reader to see that a product can remain sticky even when a rival is attractive, because changing suppliers imposes its own cost. The barrier comes from accumulated continuity value.

Author Logic Chain:
First the author defines switching costs as value loss from leaving the incumbent.
Then he distinguishes financial, procedural, and relational forms of that loss.
Then he explains that switching costs protect the incumbent by making the rival's offer look net weaker once transition pain is counted.
Then he clarifies that affection alone is not enough; the customer must actually sacrifice something by moving.
Then he leaves the reader with the practical test: what value has the customer built up that would be destroyed or recreated during a switch?

Frameworks or Terms Introduced:
Switching costs: value loss or friction paid by customers when changing suppliers.
Continuity value: the useful setup, routines, or relationships customers have already built.
Financial switching cost: money or economic concessions lost in the move.
Procedural switching cost: time, effort, retraining, migration, or workflow disruption.
Relational switching cost: trust, familiarity, or working understanding that must be rebuilt.

Specific Applications:
- Testing whether customer stickiness comes from real accumulated value or only from weak attention.
- Distinguishing lock-in from branding.
- Evaluating products, systems, or routines where migration itself is the hidden barrier.

Common Misreadings:
- treating every repeat purchase as evidence of switching costs
- confusing positive feeling with actual lock-in
- ignoring transition effort and rebuilt compatibility when comparing rivals

Counterarguments or Limits:
Switching costs weaken when migration is cheap, when standards or portability reduce continuity loss, or when customers have built little unique value with the incumbent. Hard depth should preserve the possibility that a rival can still win if the transition burden is low enough or the gain is large enough.
