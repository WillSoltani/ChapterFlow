Core Claim:
Counter-positioning appears when a newcomer adopts a superior business model that incumbents decline to copy because doing so would damage their existing business. The entrant's advantage does not come from secrecy or technical incomprehension. It comes from incumbent self-harm: the old leader sees the move and still finds imitation too costly to its current profit pool, channel structure, or operating logic. The barrier is therefore strategic unwillingness grounded in real economic pain.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
This chapter is the cleanest example of a barrier driven by incumbent conflict rather than by scale, network participation, or user lock-in. It makes the reader distinguish "cannot copy" from "will not copy because copying would hurt too much." That makes it the book's sharpest lesson about disruption without generic innovation cliches.

Author Logic Chain:
First the author defines counter-positioning as a newcomer adopting a new superior model.
Then he explains why the incumbent's response is constrained by the damage that copying would inflict on its current business.
Then he shows that the entrant's edge depends on the incumbent's embedded commitments, not on simple cleverness alone.
Then he clarifies that the barrier weakens if incumbents can copy without serious self-cannibalization.
Then he leaves the reader with the practical question: is the entrant really forcing a painful tradeoff on the incumbent, or merely offering a different version of the same game?

Frameworks or Terms Introduced:
Counter-positioning: superior entrant model resisted by incumbents because copying would harm their existing business.
Self-cannibalization: copying the entrant would damage the incumbent's current economics or structure.
Entrant crux: the newcomer's key move that changes the game rather than competing on incumbent terms.
Barrier through unwillingness: the incumbent's refusal is grounded in economic pain, not confusion.

Specific Applications:
- Distinguishing genuine model conflict from ordinary feature competition.
- Testing whether a startup is really forcing incumbent self-cannibalization.
- Recognizing when an incumbent can absorb the new move easily, which means the barrier may be weak.

Common Misreadings:
- treating every startup with a new interface as counter-positioned
- assuming incumbents are always blind or stupid when they do not respond
- confusing ordinary differentiation with a move that truly hurts incumbent economics to copy

Counterarguments or Limits:
Counter-positioning fails when incumbents can imitate without serious damage, when the entrant's model is not clearly superior, or when the incumbent can ring-fence the new model without much self-harm. Hard depth should preserve the tension that entrant cleverness alone is not enough. The trap must be real.
