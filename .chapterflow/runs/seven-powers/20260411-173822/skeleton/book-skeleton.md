## Chapter Order And Intent

1. **Scale Economies**: establish why size becomes strategy only when larger scale structurally lowers cost and produces a barrier smaller rivals cannot erase.
   - source richness: high
   - concept density: high
   - moral complexity: low
   - thin-chapter risk: low
2. **Network Economies**: show how one user's value can rise with the participation of others, while keeping the boundaries and winner-take-most logic explicit.
   - source richness: medium-high
   - concept density: high
   - moral complexity: medium
   - thin-chapter risk: low
3. **Counter-Positioning**: explain how a superior newcomer model can stay safe because incumbents would damage themselves by copying it.
   - source richness: medium-high
   - concept density: high
   - moral complexity: high
   - thin-chapter risk: medium
4. **Switching Costs**: make lock-in concrete by showing how compatibility over time creates value customers do not want to abandon.
   - source richness: medium-high
   - concept density: medium-high
   - moral complexity: medium
   - thin-chapter risk: low
5. **Branding**: distinguish real brand power from marketing fluff by grounding it in historical trust, positive association, and time-based uncertainty for challengers.
   - source richness: medium
   - concept density: medium-high
   - moral complexity: medium
   - thin-chapter risk: medium
6. **Cornered Resource**: show how preferential access to a scarce asset creates value only when the asset independently matters and rivals cannot get equivalent terms.
   - source richness: medium
   - concept density: medium-high
   - moral complexity: medium
   - thin-chapter risk: medium
7. **Process Power**: argue that process power is rare because it requires a deeply embedded interlocking system that rivals cannot quickly copy.
   - source richness: medium-high
   - concept density: high
   - moral complexity: low
   - thin-chapter risk: low
8. **The Path to Power**: pivot from statics to dynamics by showing why operational excellence and "me too" imitation do not create power.
   - source richness: medium-high
   - concept density: high
   - moral complexity: medium
   - thin-chapter risk: low
9. **The Power Progression**: close by matching different powers to origination, take-off, and stability, with timing as the central strategic pressure.
   - source richness: high
   - concept density: high
   - moral complexity: medium
   - thin-chapter risk: low

## Rotation Plan For Examples

- Keep every chapter at 2 work, 2 school, and 2 personal examples, but let the work examples carry more analytical weight because the book's examples are mostly business-facing.
- Use school examples to teach mechanism cleanly in lower-stakes settings where cost, lock-in, or imitation can still be vivid.
- Use personal examples to prevent the package from reading like a boardroom memo and to test whether the mechanism still holds outside companies.

## School-Setting Plan

- Ch01-Ch03: campus clubs, student newspapers, startup competitions, student marketplaces, and rival campus platforms.
- Ch04-Ch06: software adoption in labs, team workflows, faculty reputation, scholarships, and scarce mentorship or research access.
- Ch07-Ch09: capstone teams, student organizations, and project timing decisions where path dependence and stage-matching matter.

## Thin-Chapter Risk Notes

- Ch03 can drift into generic disruption talk if the incumbent damage logic is not kept explicit.
