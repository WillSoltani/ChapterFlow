Lots of firms are well run. Far fewer have what Helmer calls process power. The difference is that process power does not sit in one smart routine or one admired practice. It appears when a deeply embedded set of interlocking activities yields lower cost or better product, and rivals would need prolonged commitment to match the whole system.

That interlocking quality is the chapter's center. One routine supports another. Standards, handoffs, incentives, and habits reinforce each other until the operation works like a coordinated machine. The benefit can show up as lower cost, better output, or both. What matters is that the result does not come from one isolated move. It comes from the system those moves form together.

This is why process power is rare. A rival may copy one visible practice quickly and still fail to reproduce the broader effect. The copied routine worked partly because it was nested inside a larger operating logic. Without the surrounding structure, imitation produces a weaker result or no durable result at all.

That distinction keeps the chapter away from generic excellence talk. Helmer is not praising efficiency in general. Operational excellence alone does not qualify. A firm may be competent and disciplined without having a process so embedded and mutually reinforcing that rivals need sustained commitment to replicate it.

The barrier is therefore the burden of system replication. Copying the advantage requires time, coherence, and discipline across many linked activities. If the rival borrows only the visible trick, the moat can survive because the reinforcing structure underneath remains intact.

Process power is a static description of a machine that already works. The next chapter turns from that static picture to dynamics and asks how firms actually build power in the first place.
