The final chapter gives the earlier powers a timetable. Helmer calls it the Power Progression: different powers fit different stages, and timing determines which of them are still realistically available. The frozen notes organize the stages as origination, take-off, and stability.

That stage logic matters because not every power is equally buildable at every moment. The typical sequence in the frozen notes places counter-positioning and cornered resource in origination, where early directional moves and scarce advantages matter most. Scale economies, network economies, and switching costs fit take-off, where growth and reinforcement begin to thicken. Process power and branding fit stability, where maturity and accumulated history can finally support them.

The chapter's force comes from timing. A power is not only something desirable in the abstract. It is something that may or may not still be buildable at the current stage. If a firm misses the stage when a certain power can be established, later options can narrow permanently. Wanting the power later does not reopen the window by itself.

That is why the chapter is more than a summary list. It changes how strategic choice should be made. The question is no longer only which power sounds attractive. It becomes which power fits the current stage and which opportunities are already slipping away. Stage mismatch turns ambition into wishful thinking.

The sequence still has to be handled with discipline rather than rigidity. The frozen notes present a typical alignment, not a magical law. But the alignment is strong enough to force a harder conversation about timing and sequence. A mature firm cannot casually backfill an origination power it never built. An early firm cannot simply declare itself ready for a stability power that requires history.

The book closes by reconnecting power to time. Strategy is not only about understanding moats or imagining them. It is about building the right kind of moat at the right moment, before the window closes.
