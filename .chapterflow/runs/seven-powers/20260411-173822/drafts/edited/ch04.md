Sticky products are often explained as though customers stay because they feel attached. Helmer's chapter on switching costs is narrower and more useful. Customers can see a rival clearly, even prefer parts of it, and still remain with the incumbent because leaving would destroy value they have already built up.

That accumulated value is the heart of the chapter. A switch can forfeit money already committed. It can force a team to rebuild routines, retrain people, reconnect files, or relearn a workflow. It can also break a relationship where trust and shared history have reduced friction over time. The frozen notes group these burdens into financial, procedural, and relational forms.

This is what makes the challenger's job so hard. The rival is not competing only against the incumbent's current offer. It is competing against the cost of movement itself. A lower price or sharper feature list may still lose once the customer counts migration time, compatibility loss, retraining effort, or the need to rebuild a working relationship.

That is why switching costs are different from branding. Branding shapes expectation and feeling. Switching costs make departure expensive because useful continuity value has accumulated inside the current choice. The customer is not staying only out of affection. The customer is staying because leaving would destroy something real.

The barrier is not automatic. Standards, portability, or simple setup can make transition light enough that the power thins out. If customers have built little unique value with the incumbent, the challenger faces less exit pain and has a cleaner path in. Lock-in exists only when continuity value is strong enough that moving becomes its own penalty.

Switching costs therefore turn the customer's past effort into present resistance. The next chapter keeps the idea of staying but changes the reason. Instead of continuity value making exit costly, branding makes one option feel safer and more desirable over time.
