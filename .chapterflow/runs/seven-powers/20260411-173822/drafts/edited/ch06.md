Scarcity alone does not create power. Helmer's chapter on cornered resource is narrower than that. A firm needs preferential access on attractive terms to a resource that independently raises value, while rivals cannot secure equivalent access or a close substitute on comparable terms.

That independent-value test is the first discipline. The resource has to matter even before the firm's broader system is considered. A patent, rare talent pool, or scarce asset counts only if it materially improves the offer or the economics on its own. Owning something impressive is not enough if the resource does not change value in a meaningful way.

The second discipline is rival access. A resource is not truly cornered if capable rivals can buy the same input, hire a close substitute, or route around the scarcity without much loss. The barrier comes from constrained access, not from self-congratulation. If the rival can match the terms or find an alternative with similar value, the moat is thinner than it first appears.

This is why the chapter pushes back against loose asset worship. A strong team, useful supplier, or nice patent portfolio does not automatically create power. The resource must matter independently and remain meaningfully hard to match. Otherwise the firm simply has a good asset rather than a cornered one.

The chapter also keeps substitution in view. Some resources look scarce until the market reveals another path. If rivals can substitute without much value loss, then the scarcity was overstated. Cornered resource is strongest when both the input and the access terms are genuinely hard to replicate.

Cornered resource is therefore a power built on constrained possession of something that materially matters. The next chapter leaves the single-input logic and turns to process power, where the barrier comes from an embedded interlocking system instead of one scarce asset.
