People often tell disruption stories as though incumbents lose because they are slow, arrogant, or blind. Hamilton Helmer's chapter on counter-positioning is sharper than that. The incumbent may see the entrant's move clearly. The reason it still fails to copy is not confusion. It is pain.

Counter-positioning begins when a newcomer adopts a superior business model. That superiority matters. The entrant is not just repainting the same offer or adding a clever feature. It is changing the logic of the business in a way that better serves the market or improves the economics. The chapter's real focus is the incumbent's response. The old leader does not mimic the move because doing so would damage the business it already has.

That is the barrier. The entrant is protected not by secrecy, but by incumbent self-harm. If copying the new model would cannibalize current margins, undermine existing channels, or fracture the operating logic that still pays the bills, the incumbent faces a grim choice. Keep the old model and concede ground, or copy the new one and wound the business that made it powerful in the first place. The chapter subtitle, *Scylla and Charybdis*, captures that trapped position.

This is why counter-positioning is harder than generic innovation talk. Plenty of new ideas irritate incumbents without truly threatening them. A new interface, a cheaper feature, or a sharper brand can matter and still remain easy to absorb. Helmer's standard is tougher. The entrant's model has to be good enough that the incumbent should want to imitate it, while also being painful enough that imitation damages the incumbent's current position.

That distinction keeps the chapter away from startup mythology. The entrant does not win simply by being young, bold, or inventive. Invention matters because it creates a move the incumbent cannot follow cleanly. If the old leader can ring-fence the new model, launch it beside the old one, or copy it without serious self-cannibalization, then the barrier is weaker than the disruption story suggests.

Seen that way, counter-positioning is a chapter about trapped incentives. The incumbent's weakness is not stupidity. It is commitment. The very structure that made the older business successful can make the superior new move hard to adopt. That is why the entrant's edge can persist long enough to matter. The incumbent is not merely behind. It is stuck negotiating with its own success.
