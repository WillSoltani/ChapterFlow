The earlier chapters describe what power looks like once it exists. Helmer's chapter on the path to power changes the question. It asks how a firm begins moving toward a protected position in the first place, and it starts by rejecting a common confusion: operational excellence is not strategy.

That claim is not an insult to good execution. Running well matters. But execution inside the same exposed game does not by itself create a barrier. A firm can become better, faster, and more disciplined than before and still remain easy to match if it is only playing the existing terms more cleanly than rivals.

This is why the chapter turns to invention and crux choice. Firms often need a directional move that changes the path they are on. The move does not matter because it is flashy or novel. It matters because it opens a route toward a barrier that did not exist before. That is the difference between a strategic move and a better version of "me too."

The subtitle captures the warning. Me-too positioning is insufficient because it leaves no barrier. If the firm's offer is only an imitation with cleaner execution, rivals can often answer in kind. The company may improve its current standing for a while, but it has not changed the deeper exposure of the business.

So strategy dynamics asks a different question from strategy statics. Instead of asking which moat exists already, it asks what move could begin building one. That is where invention and crux choices matter most. They redirect the firm toward a position that might later become protected.

Seen that way, the chapter is a disciplined bridge, not a motivational speech. It narrows the reader's attention to moves that change the route to power rather than polishing the same vulnerable game. The final chapter will organize those routes by stage and timing in the Power Progression.
