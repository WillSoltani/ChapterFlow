Scarcity by itself does not create power. Helmer's chapter on cornered resource is stricter than that. A firm needs preferential access on attractive terms to a resource that independently raises value, while rivals cannot secure equivalent access or a close substitute on comparable terms.

That independent value test matters. The resource has to be useful even before the firm's broader system is considered. A patent, rare talent pool, or scarce asset can qualify only if it materially improves the offering or economics on its own. Owning something impressive is not enough if the resource does not actually move value in a meaningful way.

The second test is access. A resource is not truly cornered if capable rivals can buy the same input, hire an equivalent substitute, or route around the scarcity without much loss. The advantage comes from constrained access, not from the firm's self-description. If the rival can match the terms or find a close replacement, the barrier is thinner than it first appears.

This is why the chapter pushes back against asset worship. A strong team, useful supplier, or nice patent portfolio does not automatically create power. The resource must matter independently and remain meaningfully hard to match. Otherwise the firm may simply have a good asset rather than a cornered one.

The chapter also preserves a limit around substitution. Some resources look scarce until the market reveals an alternate path. If rivals can substitute without much value loss, then the scarcity was overstated. Cornered resource is strongest when both the input and the access terms are genuinely hard to replicate.

Seen that way, cornered resource is a chapter about strategic possession under constraint. The next chapter changes the barrier again. Instead of one privileged input, process power comes from a deeply embedded system of interlocking activities that rivals struggle to copy as a whole.
