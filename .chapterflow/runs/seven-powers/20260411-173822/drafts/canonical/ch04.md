People often describe sticky products as though customers stay because they are in love. Helmer's chapter on switching costs is more exact. Customers can see a rival clearly, even like the rival, and still remain with the incumbent because leaving destroys value they have already built up.

That built-up value is the center of the chapter. A customer may have invested money that would be lost in a change. The customer may have shaped routines, trained a team, connected files, or learned a workflow that would have to be recreated elsewhere. The customer may also have built a relationship with the incumbent, where trust, shared history, and working understanding reduce daily friction. Helmer's notes group these into financial, procedural, and relational forms of switching cost.

This makes the rival's problem harder than it first appears. The challenger is not only competing against the incumbent's current offer. It is competing against the cost of movement itself. A better-looking price, feature, or interface may still lose once the customer counts migration time, broken compatibility, retraining, or the loss of an established relationship.

That is why switching costs differ from branding. Branding can make a customer feel better about choosing one option over another. Switching costs make leaving expensive because useful value has already accumulated inside the current choice. The customer is not staying only out of affection. The customer is staying because departure would destroy something real.

The barrier also has limits. If standards, portability, or simple setup make the transition light, the power weakens. If the customer has built little unique value with the incumbent, the challenger has less transition pain to overcome. The chapter's logic is disciplined: lock-in exists when continuity value is strong enough that moving becomes its own penalty.

Seen that way, switching costs are a power built from memory in the system. The customer's past effort keeps shaping the present choice. The next chapter keeps the idea of staying but changes the reason. Instead of continuity value making exit costly, branding makes one option feel safer or more desirable over time.
