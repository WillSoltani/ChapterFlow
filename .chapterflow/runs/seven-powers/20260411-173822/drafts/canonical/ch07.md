Some companies are run well. Far fewer have what Helmer calls process power. The difference is that process power does not sit in one clever routine or one admired practice. It appears when a deeply embedded set of interlocking activities yields lower cost or better product, and rivals would need prolonged commitment to match the whole thing.

That interlocking quality matters. One routine supports another. Standards, handoffs, incentives, and habits reinforce each other until the system works as an integrated machine. The benefit can show up as lower cost, better output, or both. But the key is that the result does not come from one isolated move. It comes from the coordination among many moves.

This is why process power is rare. A rival may copy one visible practice quickly and still fail to reproduce the broader effect. The visible routine worked partly because it sat inside a larger operating logic. Without the rest of the system, imitation produces a weaker result or no durable result at all.

That distinction keeps the chapter away from generic excellence talk. Helmer is not praising efficient management in general. Operational excellence alone does not qualify. A company may run competently without having a process so embedded and mutually reinforcing that rivals need sustained commitment to replicate it.

The barrier therefore comes from replication burden. Copying the system requires time, coherence, and discipline across many linked activities. If the rival is only borrowing a visible trick, the moat can survive because the hidden reinforcing structure remains intact.

Seen that way, process power is a static description of a machine that already works. The next chapter turns from that static picture to dynamics and asks how firms actually build power in the first place.
