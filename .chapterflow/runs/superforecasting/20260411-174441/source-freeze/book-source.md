## Early-chapter support notes

### Chapter 1

- The opening contrast pits famous expert commentary against little-known high performers in forecasting tournaments.
- Tetlock's long-run expert-prediction study is framed as evidence that average expert judgment often performed no better than chance.
- The chapter uses Mohamed Bouazizi and the Arab Spring to show that real limits on predictability exist.
- The key stance is neither naive confidence nor fatalism. The chapter argues for an "optimistic skeptic" posture: accept limits, then test where better judgment is still possible.
- The Good Judgment Project is introduced as the place where forecast-measure-revise becomes an operational discipline.

### Chapter 2

- The chapter turns from prediction's limits to the mental habits that make bad forecasting feel smart.
- Overconfidence and the illusion of explanatory depth are core pressure points.
- Archie Cochrane functions as a reality-check anchor for evidence, feedback, and intellectual humility.
- The chapter's practical turn is that strong forecasting depends on replacing confident stories with testable judgments.
