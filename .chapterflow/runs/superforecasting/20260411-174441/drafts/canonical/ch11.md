By the time Tetlock and Gardner reach this chapter, they have spent most of the book building the case for superforecasting. That makes the title important. The question is no longer how these people think or why they outperform others in a tournament. The question is whether the whole story has been oversold. Are superforecasters really so super, or are they just impressive until the world becomes too strange for their tools to matter? The chapter earns its place by refusing easy triumphalism.

That pressure comes partly from serious critics. Daniel Kahneman and Nassim Taleb matter because both push against the idea that the future can be domesticated by confident prediction. Tetlock and Gardner do not treat that criticism as something to wave away. They use it to force a narrower and more honest claim about what forecasting can do.

Black swans are the chapter's hardest limit. Some events are rare, high-impact, and hard to anticipate in the ordinary forecasting frame. That matters because it keeps the book from pretending that a better probability estimate can abolish surprise. Long-range prediction becomes especially vulnerable here. The farther out the forecast stretches, the more room there is for surprise, complexity, and structural change to overrun it.

Yet the chapter does not collapse into defeat. Tetlock and Gardner insist that limits do not make forecasting useless. The practical value of forecasting can survive even when prediction remains incomplete. A forecast can still improve planning, sharpen preparation, and force better thinking about what conditions would change the odds. The point is no longer total control over the future. The point is better judgment before the future arrives.

This is why planning for surprise matters so much. A good forecaster does not only ask what is most likely. A good forecaster also asks what would be costly if it arrived unexpectedly. That does not defeat black swans, but it makes the mind and the institution less fragile when reality refuses to cooperate. The chapter's deeper defense is that forecasting is useful partly because it disciplines preparation, not because it guarantees mastery.

That makes the authors' position narrower but stronger. Superforecasters are not magical seers standing outside uncertainty. They are disciplined thinkers who improve the odds of better judgment. Kahneman and Taleb matter because they prevent the book from drifting into self-congratulation. Tetlock and Gardner matter because they refuse the opposite mistake of concluding that surprise nullifies all effort.

By the end, the chapter sounds less like a retreat than a correction of scope. Forecasting remains valuable, but only when its promise is stated honestly. It helps people prepare, compare possibilities, and think more rigorously under uncertainty. It does not license fantasies of final control. That answer points directly to the last chapter. If forecasting is limited but useful, what kind of future should people try to build for it?
