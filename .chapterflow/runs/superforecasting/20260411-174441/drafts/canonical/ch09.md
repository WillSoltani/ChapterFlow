A smooth meeting can look like intelligence when it is really just social success. Tetlock and Gardner use this chapter to break that illusion. By the time the book reaches superteams, it has already shown what strong forecasters look like alone. Now the question changes. What happens when several of those minds work together? The answer is not that more people automatically produce better forecasts. The answer is that teams become powerful only when they preserve the disagreement that individuals might otherwise hide.

That matters because the risk changes at team scale. A capable forecaster working alone can be stubborn, but a capable team can become conformist. Groupthink is the chapter's central danger. It does not require a weak room. It requires a room where dissent becomes socially expensive. Once that happens, the appearance of agreement starts crowding out the quality of judgment. People stop offering the small objections that would have sharpened the estimate because calm starts feeling more valuable than truth.

This is why the chapter refuses to define superteams as merely harmonious teams. Harmony may be a byproduct, but it is not the engine. The engine is aggregated difference. A strong team does not flatten several views into one bland consensus too early. It lets conflicting interpretations stay visible long enough for the forecast to improve. The point is not argument for its own sake. The point is to keep the group from becoming a machine that rewards social ease over evidentiary challenge.

That is where psychologically safe pushback matters. The phrase is easy to cheapen, but Tetlock and Gardner are after something narrower and harder. People inside the team need enough trust to challenge one another honestly without treating disagreement as disloyalty. If the first objection gets punished, the room learns quickly. If the first objection is examined, the room learns something else. It learns that correction is part of the job.

The chapter therefore makes dissent look less like a threat to teamwork and more like a condition of real teamwork. A team that cannot surface conflict early will meet conflict later in the form of a worse forecast. The quiet member who raises a reservation, the teammate who asks whether the evidence really earned the latest move, the colleague who keeps alternative interpretations alive a little longer than feels comfortable, all become part of the team's forecasting edge. They protect the group from flattering itself with premature closure.

This also explains why aggregation matters. The team advantage does not come from putting several people in one room and waiting for wisdom to appear. It comes from preserving enough separation among views that the final estimate contains more information than any one person had at the start. Once the room collapses too quickly into one narrative, the potential gain starts leaking away. The team becomes louder than its members instead of smarter than its members.

By the end, superteams look like a disciplined extension of the earlier chapters rather than a break from them. Chapter 8 asked whether a forecaster could stay unfinished and revisable. Chapter 9 asks whether a group can do the same thing together. Tetlock and Gardner think it can, but only if the team makes challenge operational. The next question follows naturally. If dissent is this valuable, what should a leader actually do with it while still making decisions under pressure?
