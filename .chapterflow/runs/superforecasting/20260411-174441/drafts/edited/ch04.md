The Iraq WMD failure sits in this chapter like a warning flare. Tetlock and Gardner do not use it for cheap hindsight or to pretend the evidence was simple at the time. They use it to show how judgment can go wrong inside elite institutions when a hard forecasting question gets quietly replaced by an easier one. The real question was whether Saddam Hussein actually had weapons of mass destruction. The easier question was whether he looked like someone hiding something. Once the second question took over, confidence could rise around a conclusion the evidence had never truly locked down.

That substitution matters because it shows why prestige systems can still fail badly. The intelligence case could be reasonable and still wrong. Robert Jervis's framing makes the point sharper. If the institutions involved had expressed less confidence in the same conclusion, the downstream political result might have changed. The chapter is not only about accuracy. It is about the cost of confident miscalibration when stakes are high.

Out of that wreckage came IARPA's effort to improve forecasting inside the intelligence world. Tetlock pushed for something medicine had already learned: methods should be tested in environments close to the real thing, and claims about better judgment should answer to actual results. That is why the Good Judgment Project matters. It turned forecasting into a competition where volunteers and professionals could be compared under pressure instead of under theory alone.

The surprise was not that some ordinary people did well. The surprise was how well they did, and how consistently. Good Judgment Project forecasters updated their estimates as new events came in, worked with probabilities instead of slogans, and benefited from aggregation that weighted stronger performers more heavily. They beat intelligence professionals, prediction markets, and other benchmarks often enough to make the claim impossible to shrug off as a lucky weekend.

Doug Lorch gives the chapter a human scale. He was not introduced as a genius from central casting. He became important because he made enormous numbers of forecasts and kept performing at an unusually high level. The chapter needs a figure like that because superforecasting would sound mystical without one. Lorch makes it look procedural instead. Strong judgment here is not magic. It is repeated estimation, revision, and exposure to scorekeeping.

Still, the authors refuse to let the success story become a fairy tale. Luck always sits near performance, especially in streaks. That is where regression to the mean becomes crucial. Many standout runs fade once randomness cools off. If superforecasters were merely people on hot streaks, their edge should have collapsed toward average performance over time. Yet Tetlock found that many of the strongest forecasters held up well enough to suggest something more durable than fortune alone. Not perfect immunity to luck. Not a superhuman gift. A real skill component that kept surviving the check.

That balance is what makes the chapter persuasive. It does not ask the reader to believe in infallible seers. It asks the reader to believe that some habits of thinking, updating, and collaborating perform better than others once the scoreboard is real. That is a narrower claim, but also a stronger one. It survives because it is willing to keep skill and luck in the same frame instead of letting either one swallow the other.

By the end, superforecasters look less like a special species and more like a disciplined minority inside ordinary human judgment. They ask harder questions. They update faster. They let aggregation and feedback correct them. They do not win because they see the future in a blaze. They win because they keep turning vague uncertainty into a sequence of exposed, revisable estimates. That leaves the next question hanging in the right place. If these people are not magicians, what habits actually make them different from the rest of us?
