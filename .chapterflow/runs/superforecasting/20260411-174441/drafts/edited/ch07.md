Superforecasters do pay obsessive attention to incoming information, but Tetlock and Gardner spend this chapter warning against a lazy explanation. The edge is not simply that these people read more news than everyone else. If that were the whole story, the book would shrink into an argument for information gluttony. Instead, the chapter insists that update discipline matters more than information volume. Superforecasters were already outperforming others before later updates arrived. That means the stronger starting forecast still counts.

That point matters because it keeps the book from romanticizing information flow. A weak prior does not become strong just because it is revised often. Tetlock and Gardner want the reader to see updating as a separate skill layered onto an already better initial judgment. The question is no longer who can consume the most signals. It is who can tell how much a particular signal deserves to move the forecast.

Bill Flack gives the chapter its cleanest example of that skill. In the Arafat case, he did not react to new evidence as though every development should overturn the whole estimate. He moved from 60 percent to 65 percent. That small revision matters because it shows respect both for the evidence and for proportion. The update was real, but it was not dramatic. The signal mattered, yet it was not enough to justify a theatrical swing.

This is one of the chapter's hardest lessons. Many forecasters fail by underreacting because they are attached to the old story and do not want to disturb a forecast they have already inhabited. Others fail by overreacting because every new report feels urgent enough to deserve a large revision. Tetlock and Gardner treat both as distortions. Good updating is not stubbornness, and it is not twitchiness. It is the ability to move by the amount the evidence earns.

That is where the dilution effect matters. New information can look important simply because it is new, even when it does not really sharpen the forecast. A forecaster can wind up revising because the inbox got fuller, not because the case got clearer. The chapter wants a harsher filter than that. Meaningful evidence should change the estimate. Irrelevant or weakly diagnostic detail should not be allowed to impersonate a signal.

Tim Minto sharpens the chapter's behavioral model by showing how many small updates can outperform rare dramatic revisions. Forecasting novices often freeze for too long, then lurch once reality becomes too loud to ignore. Superforecasters tend to revise more often, but with better sizing. The forecast stays alive to evidence because it is always being adjusted in smaller, more defensible steps instead of being abandoned and rebuilt after each shock.

This gives the chapter a useful tension. Staying informed is necessary, but it is not a virtue by itself. News can improve judgment only if the forecaster resists both attachment and noise. Too little movement leaves the forecast stale. Too much movement turns it into a hostage of the latest headline. Tetlock and Gardner are after the narrower discipline in between: proportionate revision by a mind strong enough to keep score on itself while the world keeps changing.

By the end, the chapter has turned updating into something less glamorous than omniscience and more demanding than news addiction. The best forecasters are not merely information junkies. They are people who start better, revise carefully, and know that every new fact comes with a harder question attached: does this really change the odds, and if so, by how much? That question points naturally to the next chapter. If strong forecasters keep handling new information well, how do they keep improving their own process instead of just repeating it?
