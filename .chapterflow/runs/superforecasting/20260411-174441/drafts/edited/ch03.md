Most forecasts fail before reality ever gets a chance to disagree with them. They fail in the wording. Someone says an outcome is likely, significant, or a serious possibility, and everyone in the room walks away hearing something slightly different. The language feels useful because it sounds precise enough to guide action while staying blurry enough to avoid being pinned down later. Tetlock and Gardner attack that blur because a forecast that cannot be measured cannot really be improved.

Sherman Kent saw the problem early inside intelligence work. After watching ambiguity create confusion where precision mattered, he tried to force probability language into numerical ranges. The move was awkward because numbers feel colder than words. They also feel riskier. Once you attach a percentage to a claim, you have stopped hinting and started exposing yourself to a future check. That discomfort is why the chapter values the move. Vague forecasting protects status. Numerical forecasting invites accountability.

That shift matters for more than style. A forecast expressed in percentages changes the thinker's own mind. It forces finer distinctions. "Maybe" can hide laziness. A 62 percent estimate has to compete with 55 or 70, and that pressure makes a person inspect the claim more carefully. Tetlock and Gardner like the comparison to artists learning finer shades of gray. Better forecasters do not merely know more. They get better at seeing the difference between kinds of uncertainty.

Once forecasts become explicit, scorekeeping can begin. That is where calibration and resolution enter. Calibration asks whether confidence and reality line up over time. If you say 70 percent often, do those events happen around 70 percent of the time? Resolution asks whether you are willing to move away from mushy middle numbers when the evidence deserves it. A forecaster who always hides near 50 percent may avoid embarrassment, but that caution can be disguised ignorance. Good judgment is not only about avoiding overconfidence. It is also about being bold enough to make supportable distinctions.

The Brier score gives the chapter its hardest edge because it turns forecasting into a record. A perfect score is zero. A worse forecast lands farther away. Chance-level guessing sits in embarrassingly familiar territory. But Tetlock and Gardner stay careful here too. A score does not mean much without context. Hitting a given number in a wild environment is more impressive than hitting the same number in an easy one. Precision only becomes meaningful when difficulty stays in view.

That is where the contrast between hedgehogs and foxes starts to matter. Hedgehogs love one big idea. They can be magnetic because a single organizing theory makes the world sound clean and legible. Foxes gather many small pieces, tolerate contradiction, and revise more freely. Under media incentives, hedgehogs often look stronger. Under scorekeeping, foxes tend to do better. The scoreboard punishes the pleasure of a grand story if that story keeps bending evidence back toward its favorite conclusion.

Aggregation pushes the same lesson one step further. A single forecaster, even a careful one, is still trapped inside personal blind spots. Combining judgments from people who see different parts of the problem can produce a stronger estimate than any lone performance. That is counterintuitive because it feels less elegant than following one decisive mind. The chapter keeps siding with methods that survive contact with results over methods that merely feel sharper while the future is still open.

By now the book's argument is changing tone. Chapter 1 stripped prestige away from forecasting. Chapter 2 stripped certainty away from explanation. Chapter 3 strips vagueness away from judgment. Once forecasts are measurable, the room changes. Some styles start looking weaker. Some minds start looking stronger. That leads to the obvious next question. If scorekeeping exposes real differences, who keeps performing well once the numbers are public?
