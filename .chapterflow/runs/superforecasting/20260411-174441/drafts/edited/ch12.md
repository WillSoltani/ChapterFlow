Tetlock and Gardner end the book by refusing to treat superforecasting as a finished achievement. That is the first important move. The future of forecasting is not something that arrives automatically once a few talented people prove what is possible. It depends on whether institutions, leaders, and citizens demand better habits of accountability than they usually do. Chapter 12 is therefore less a victory lap than a reform argument.

Scorekeeping sits at the center of that argument. Forecasting improves when people keep track of what was predicted, what happened, and how good the judgment really was. Without that discipline, persuasive forecasting and accurate forecasting blur together too easily. A forecaster can sound impressive, move a crowd, or flatter a leader without ever being forced to confront the actual record. Tetlock and Gardner want a world where the record fights back.

This is where institutional incentives become the real problem. Political and organizational systems often reward confidence, loyalty, and rhetorical usefulness more than accuracy. Forecasts that energize allies or defend prior commitments can beat forecasts that are simply more correct. The chapter matters because it makes forecasting reform sound less like a technical tweak and more like an incentive fight.

Ernest Amory Codman gives the chapter a historical anchor for that fight. What matters is not the biographical detail but the structural lesson. Institutions resist being measured when measurement threatens prestige, comfort, or hierarchy. Codman matters because he represents the stubborn demand that outcomes be tracked anyway. Tetlock and Gardner borrow that spirit for forecasting: if you want improvement, you need visible accountability even when institutions dislike it.

The chapter then turns from incentives to question design. Big future questions are often too large, vague, or tangled to answer well in one pass. The better move is to break them into smaller component questions that can be judged, scored, and revised. This keeps the forecasting problem workable. It turns grand uncertainty into a series of narrower decisions that can actually learn from feedback.

That is why superquestioners matter. The future of forecasting does not belong only to people who answer well. It also belongs to people who frame the right questions in the first place. Tetlock and Gardner suggest that a stronger forecasting culture will need both: superforecasters who can reason carefully once the question is set, and superquestioners who know how to carve the world into tractable pieces.

The ending mood is optimistic, but only conditionally. Forecasting can get better. Institutions can learn. People can demand more honest scorekeeping and more disciplined questions. But none of that happens by default. The same systems that reward bad incentives now will keep doing so unless someone insists on a different standard. The future is open, but not self-correcting.

That gives the book a clean final stance. Superforecasting is not a promise of mastery. It is a demand for better habits: better scorekeeping, better questions, better incentives, and better humility about what remains uncertain. The book ends by lowering the fantasy and raising the standard. That is a stronger ending than certainty would have been.
