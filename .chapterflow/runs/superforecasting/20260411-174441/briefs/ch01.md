Core Claim:
The chapter establishes the book's central posture by refusing two bad extremes. Blind confidence in expert punditry fails because experts are rarely scored and often perform around chance over long horizons. Fatalism fails too because some pockets of the future are still forecastable when people work with probabilities, feedback, and revision. The right stance is disciplined skepticism: respect real limits while still trying to improve judgment where effort pays off.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
This chapter is the gateway because it sets the emotional and intellectual tone for the whole book. It lives on the contrast between celebrity pundits and accountable forecasters, then keeps that contrast honest by bringing in chaos, black-swan limits, and the Arab Spring.

Author Logic Chain:
First the authors puncture expert prestige with the long-run prediction record.
Then they show that unpredictability is real, not a straw man.
Then they refuse the slide from limits to nihilism and point toward a measured practice of better forecasting.

Required Anchors:
- Tom Friedman versus Bill Flack, with fame contrasted against forecasting accuracy and accountability.
- Tetlock's long expert-prediction study from 1984 to 2004, ending in roughly chance-level average expert accuracy.
- Mohamed Bouazizi and the Arab Spring as a reminder that some shocks outrun confident long-range storytelling.
