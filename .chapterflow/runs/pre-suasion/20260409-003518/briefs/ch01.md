Core Claim:
Chapter 1 argues that persuasive advantage is often secured before the formal appeal begins. Cialdini's opening move is to show that top persuaders prepare receptivity, attention, and associative context instead of relying only on the quality of the request itself. The chapter's job is to make that claim feel concrete through vivid examples rather than through abstract theory alone.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
This is the book's threshold chapter. It is not yet the deep-association chapter and not yet the attention-taxonomy chapter. It has to prove that "before the ask" is a real leverage point by using the undercover-training story, the pricing-anchor consultant, and Jim's trust trick as load-bearing anchors.

Author Logic Chain:
First the author describes infiltrating persuasion training programs to learn what high performers actually do.
Then he reports a surprising pattern: the best performers spend extra effort on what happens before the request.
Then he defines pre-suasion as arranging receptivity in advance.
Then he shows concrete examples of anchors and trust cues changing later outcomes.
Then he widens the promise from sales settings to a broader science of influence while preserving an ethical warning.

Common Misreadings:

Counterarguments or Limits:
The chapter argues that setup matters enormously, not that setup makes substance irrelevant. It also hints that some pre-suasive moves can be ethically perilous. The prose should preserve both boundaries so the chapter does not sound like a license for manipulation or a promise that weak offers can be rescued by framing alone.
