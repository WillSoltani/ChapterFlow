The chapter opens by pushing the book from attention into linkage. Chapter 6 argued that some cues keep attention from drifting away. Chapter 7 asks what begins happening around a cue once attention keeps returning to it. Cialdini's answer is that the focal concept does not sit alone in the mind. It begins collecting associations, and those associations help determine how the concept feels before the main request or judgment fully arrives.

That is the force of the title: *The Primacy of Associations: I Link, Therefore I Think*. The chapter's narrow claim is not that every judgment is fake or that people never evaluate anything directly. It is that attended concepts can become more important and more attractive because of what they are linked to. Once a concept is focal, surrounding meanings and feelings can start attaching themselves to it. What is already warm, favorable, or trusted in the audience can lend some of that value to the target now sitting in view.

This is why the chapter follows the magnetizers chapter so naturally. Magnetizers explain why a cue stays present. Association explains what that continued presence enables. A held cue has more chances to gather meaning. It can pick up positive coloration from neighboring words, images, or ideas. It can begin to feel better not because its own merits have changed, but because the mind is not processing it in isolation. The target enters a field of linkage.

That point matters for persuasion because the audience often experiences the result as if it were native to the concept itself. If a proposal is introduced beside favorable signals, or if an idea is made focal in a context already carrying positive meaning, the proposal can feel more attractive before its case has been fully weighed. The target borrows some of the value already living in the surrounding associations. Pre-suasion works here not by changing the argument first, but by changing the associative company the argument keeps.

The chapter therefore sharpens an important warning. Borrowed positivity is not proof. A concept may deserve the warmer response it receives, but the warmth itself does not settle that question. The audience still has to ask whether the favorable feeling belongs to the concept on its own merits or whether the concept is simply benefiting from the associations now clustered around it. If that distinction is ignored, linked goodness starts masquerading as earned goodness.

That is also why the mechanism can be helpful or manipulative. Helpful use means linking an idea to associations that genuinely belong with it, making it easier for people to notice real value they might otherwise miss. Manipulative use means giving a weaker idea borrowed shine from a context that flatters it without justifying it. The mechanism is the same even when the ethics differ. The question is whether the association clarifies what is true or merely upgrades what is already strategically placed in front of the audience.

Seen this way, Chapter 7 turns pre-suasion into a chapter about inherited meaning. A focal cue is not only seen. It is interpreted inside a web of neighboring cues that change how it is received. This chapter does not need broad claims about every form of priming to make that point. It only needs the narrow frozen support that attended concepts can become more important and attractive because of favorable associations already present in the audience. That is enough to explain why linkage matters before the request.

The bridge to Chapter 8 follows directly. If favorable associations matter so much, then where do those associations come from in practice? Not only from ideas and words, but from places and states as well. Once the book has shown that a focal concept borrows value from what surrounds it, the next question is how physical and psychological settings become part of that surrounding field.
