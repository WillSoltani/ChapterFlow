Not every moment before a request carries the same weight. That is the move Robert Cialdini makes after proving that the before-the-ask stage matters at all. Once you accept that setup shapes outcomes, the next question is obvious. When does that setup matter most? Chapter 2 answers by pointing to what he calls privileged moments, the points when another person's mind is especially ready to receive what comes next.

That idea sounds small until you notice what it changes. If all earlier moments were equal, then good timing would just mean luck, patience, or social instinct. But the chapter treats timing as more structured than that. Some moments give a communicator unusual leverage because attention is unusually concentrated, self-relevance is unusually alive, or the mind is already turned toward the kind of answer the speaker hopes to hear. In those moments, the next request does not merely arrive. It arrives with a head start.

The chapter's real claim is not that people become blank or defenseless in those windows. It is that what is active in attention at the moment of decision gains special privilege. A thought, value, or identity cue that is freshly elevated can shape how the following request is interpreted. That is why Chapter 2 matters so much for the rest of the book. It narrows pre-suasion from a broad insight about setup into a sharper insight about timing. Setup is not only a matter of what you place in front of someone. It is also a matter of when that placement will matter most.

You can feel the logic in ordinary life. Two identical requests can land differently depending on what the listener has just been thinking about. Ask for help in a moment when helpfulness is abstract and distant, and the request may feel optional. Ask after a person has just been nudged to think of themselves as the kind of person who helps, and the same request can feel much closer to their own self-understanding. The content of the request may be unchanged. The readiness surrounding it is not.

That is why self-relevant openers matter in the frozen support around this chapter. A small question about whether someone sees themselves as helpful or adventurous does not look like the main event. But it may be doing the real work of the main event by making a particular identity more available just before the request appears. In that sense, the opener is not an innocent preface. It is a selective light. It brightens one part of the listener's self-image so the next proposal enters through that illuminated door.

This is what makes the chapter more precise than generic advice about catching people at a good time. A privileged moment is not merely when the listener is relaxed or polite. It is when the listener's attention has been organized in a way that gives some thought, value, or self-description unusual weight. A speaker who understands that is no longer asking only, Is this a convenient moment? The better question is, What is mentally elevated right now, and what will my request mean if it enters through that point of elevation?

The chapter also adds a subtle warning. Once you realize that self-relevance can prepare a person to respond consistently with an activated identity, it becomes tempting to treat identity as a lever to be pulled whenever you want agreement. That is where the idea darkens. There is a difference between helping someone hear a worthy request in a moment of genuine openness and steering their self-concept so the request feels harder to refuse than it should. The chapter's power depends on keeping that difference visible.

Seen this way, privileged moments are not magical moments. They are moments when attention, readiness, and identity line up tightly enough that the next message enters a mind already organized around a nearby answer. That can be useful when the request deserves a fair hearing and the listener's attention would otherwise be scattered or indifferent. It can also become suspect when the whole point is to narrow the listener's judgment before fuller reflection can catch up. The method is powerful for the same reason it needs restraint.

Chapter 2 therefore does two jobs at once. It gives the reader a more exact view of timing, and it raises the stakes of timing. You are not merely looking for a friendly pause in the conversation. You are looking for the moment when focus is live and meaning is ready to stick. And because that moment can be recognized or shaped, the ethical question gets harder, not easier. Are you making it easier for someone to see what matters, or are you making it harder for them to see anything else?

That question is what pushes the chapter forward. If privileged moments matter because what is elevated in attention gains special weight, then attention itself cannot be a neutral background feature. It has to be one of the engines of importance. What people notice most intensely in the instant before a decision may not just influence the choice. It may start to feel like the most important thing about the choice.
