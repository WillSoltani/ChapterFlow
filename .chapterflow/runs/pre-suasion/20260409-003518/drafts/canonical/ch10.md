After Chapter 9 explains how pre-suasion works, Chapter 10 asks the practical optimization question: if a communicator can ready later response by foregrounding the right concept, which concepts are the smartest broad roads to use? Cialdini answers with the chapter title itself, *Six Main Roads to Change: Broad Boulevards as Smart Shortcuts*. The emphasis shifts from mechanism to selection.

The chapter's frozen support keeps the answer narrow. The strongest broad pre-suasive focal concepts are the six classic influence principles from *Influence*: reciprocity, liking, authority, social proof, scarcity, and consistency. Chapter 10 does not present these as a random list or as six interchangeable buzzwords. It presents them as the broadest and most reliable principle paths to frontload before an ask.

Why these six? The authorized support says they are broad shortcuts because they typically steer people when deciding what to do. That makes them especially useful for pre-suasion. A pre-suasive move is not only about putting anything into focus. It is about putting something into focus that has a high chance of guiding later choice. The six principles qualify because they already function as wide decision guides. When attention is directed toward one of them in advance, later requests arrive in a mind that is already oriented along a powerful route.

This is why the chapter belongs in the book's best-practices section. Chapter 9 established that readying aligned concepts changes later ease of activation. Chapter 10 now says that some aligned concepts are better than others because they are broad boulevards rather than side streets. Reciprocity can make giving back feel appropriate. Liking can make agreement feel warmer. Authority can make deference feel reasonable. Social proof can make following others feel safer. Scarcity can make action feel more urgent. Consistency can make follow-through feel self-respecting. The chapter's claim is not that each principle always wins, but that each one is a dependable high-level route through which later requests may be received.

That practical value explains the phrase smart shortcuts. A shortcut is smart when it helps people orient themselves quickly in a decision field. These principles often do that. They give audiences a familiar way to sort what matters before the full case is complete. From a pre-suasive standpoint, this is strategic efficiency. Instead of trying to prepare every possible idea, the communicator can frontload one of the broad principles that already steers many ordinary decisions.

But the chapter keeps a necessary restraint in place. A broad road is not a license. The same feature that makes these principles useful also makes them risky. If a principle is broad and reliable, it can guide response even when the merits of the specific request remain underexamined. Chapter 10 therefore cannot be read as permission to treat reciprocity, liking, authority, social proof, scarcity, or consistency as proof on their own. They are shortcuts into judgment, not substitutes for judgment.

That distinction matters because optimization can easily become oversteering. A well-chosen principle cue may help a listener organize a decision. It may also make a weak request feel stronger than it deserves. The chapter's narrow value is therefore double-edged. It tells communicators where to aim their preparatory attention, but it also tells readers what to watch for when a request seems to arrive already riding on one of these broad roads.

The bridge forward is clean. Once the six classic principles have been named as the main roads, the book is ready to expand the map. The next chapter turns to unity, asking how being together extends the set of pre-suasive routes beyond the original six.
