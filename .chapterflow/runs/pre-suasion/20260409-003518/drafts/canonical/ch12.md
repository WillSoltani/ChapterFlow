Chapter 12 takes the unity principle one step further. Chapter 11 argued that people can become a We through being together in identity, especially through family and place. Chapter 12 asks what happens when the feeling of We is built through action instead. The title names the shift directly: *Unity 2: Acting Together*. The chapter's claim is that a shared sense of us can arise not only from who people are, but from what they do together.

The frozen support keeps the mechanism narrow. We relationships can result from acting together synchronously or collaboratively. The point is not that any shared activity matters equally. The chapter is interested in coordinated or joint action that makes separate people feel like a unit. A shared rhythm, a coordinated effort, or a collaborative task can turn a collection of individuals into a more unified We before a later request arrives.

That matters because the official support also ties this unitary activity to consequences. Arranged pre-suasively, acting together can increase mutual liking and support. A later request therefore does not arrive in the same field it would have entered before the shared action. It arrives after people have already moved, worked, or aligned with each other in a way that makes further agreement feel easier and more natural.

This is the action-based version of unity. Chapter 11 emphasized identity markers that say we belong together. Chapter 12 emphasizes experiences that make people feel they are functioning together. In pre-suasive terms, that is powerful because support can be prepared through participation itself. The audience does not only hear the later message. It hears it after a We relationship has been strengthened by joint movement or joint effort.

The frozen support also notes a wide illustration range: strangers, teammates, children, students, and even consumer-brand loyalty. That list matters less as a set of details than as a signal of breadth. The chapter's narrow claim is that acting together can create or intensify a We frame across very different contexts. The common factor is not the domain. It is the unitizing effect of synchrony or collaboration.

The chapter therefore sharpens a useful distinction. Shared action can create real mutual liking and support, but those effects still do not prove that the later request deserves assent. A group warm-up, coordinated task, or collaborative exercise may prepare a more favorable field, yet the later proposal still has to answer to its own merits. Action-based unity can guide receptivity; it cannot settle judgment.

That is why the chapter carries both promise and risk. Real collaboration can build authentic support. Staged togetherness can also lend a weak request more communal force than it has earned. If people mistake the cohesion created by acting together for evidence that the later request is good, unity starts doing illegitimate work again, this time through motion rather than shared identity.

The chapter's value lies in naming that second route to We. Chapter 11 said people can become one through who they are together. Chapter 12 says they can also become one through what they do together. From there the book is ready to close by drawing its final implications.
