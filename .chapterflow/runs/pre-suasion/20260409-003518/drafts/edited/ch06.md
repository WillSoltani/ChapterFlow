Winning the first look is powerful, but it is not the whole game. Some cues do more than attract attention. They keep the mind coming back. That is the force of Chapter 6, "Commanders of Attention 2: The Magnetizers." Cialdini is no longer asking what seizes the spotlight first. He is asking what gives the spotlight staying power once it has landed.

The chapter's answer is that certain cues have a holding effect. They keep attention engaged because they feel personally tied to the self, incomplete, or mysterious. These cues do not merely interrupt the field. They linger inside it. The mind keeps revisiting them because they seem unfinished, self-implicated, or still not fully understood.

That is why magnetizers matter. A self-relevant cue can hold attention because people remain alert to what reflects on them, concerns them, or touches their own goals and identity. An unfinished cue can hold attention because incompleteness creates mental tension that asks to be resolved. A mysterious cue can hold attention because uncertainty keeps inviting another look. The common thread is persistence. These cues do not just win the opening. They keep reopening the case.

This chapter advances the logic of the last one. Chapter 5 explained how some cues capture attention early. Chapter 6 explains why some cues do not release attention easily after that capture. The distinction matters because sustained attention gives later persuasion more time, more mental space, and often more emotional grip than a brief glance ever could.

That has direct implications for persuasion. A self-relevant frame can keep a listener engaged because the message continues to feel connected to the listener's own concerns. An incomplete preview can keep a room mentally hooked because people want closure. A mysterious detail can keep curiosity alive long enough for the next message to arrive inside an active search for meaning. The persuader is no longer benefiting only from opening capture. The persuader is benefiting from continued return.

The chapter's limit matters as much as its mechanism. A cue can hold attention without deserving belief or importance. A personally relevant point can stay mentally active and still be misleading. An unfinished issue can keep the room hooked and still be less important than a quieter finished one. A mysterious detail can feel worthy of extended thought simply because it withholds completion well. Staying power is not proof.

That distinction guards against a common mistake. People often confuse what keeps returning to mind with what deserves the most weight. But recurrence can come from incompleteness, self-involvement, or unresolved uncertainty, not only from genuine importance. Magnetizers explain why the mind keeps circling something. They do not automatically explain why the thing being circled should govern judgment.

Seen this way, magnetizers are not ornaments added after attraction. They are the second layer of attentional leverage. Attractors take the first look. Magnetizers make the look last. Once that staying power exists, later persuasion can build inside a field where attention is not only captured but maintained.

The practical question becomes sharper here. Is the cue staying active because it is helping the audience keep hold of what genuinely matters, or because it is skillfully using self-reference, incompleteness, or mystery to remain onstage longer than it deserves? That is the difference between useful engagement and strategic overholding.

The bridge into the next part of the book is clear. If a cue keeps the mind returning, then the next issue is what happens while the mind keeps returning. What links begin forming? What associations start attaching themselves to the held cue? That is why Chapter 7 turns toward association. Held attention becomes especially powerful once it starts connecting ideas together.
