The easiest way to make something feel important is not always to improve it. Sometimes it is simply to aim attention at it and hold it there. That is the sharper claim underneath Chapter 3's strange title. Robert Cialdini is not saying attention merely helps us notice what matters. He is saying attention can help create the feeling that something matters more than it otherwise would.

That distinction changes how the earlier chapters read. Privileged moments are not powerful only because they happen near a decision. They are powerful because what sits in the foreground at that instant starts to carry extra weight. A feature, risk, benefit, or identity cue can feel more significant simply because it is the thing the mind is currently organized around. The object may be unchanged. Its felt importance is not.

This is why attention is more than a spotlight. A spotlight sounds passive, like a beam that simply lands on whatever deserves to be seen. Chapter 3 suggests something stronger. Once the light is there, the thing inside it starts to look like the main thing. Attention is doing evaluative work. It is not only helping information arrive. It is helping importance form.

You can feel the mechanism in small decisions. Put one concern at the front of the room and it can begin to feel like the decisive concern. Repeat one benefit and it can start to feel like the real benefit. Highlight one risk and the rest of the field can lose weight even when the underlying facts have not changed. The shift is not that people become irrational in some dramatic sense. The shift is that what is most attended becomes easier to treat as what matters most.

That is why the chapter matters for persuasion. A pre-suader does not always need to change the object of judgment. Often it is enough to change what the audience is attending to when the judgment arrives. If one feature has been elevated, the audience can honestly report that it felt more important, because in that moment it did. The importance was not discovered in a neutral survey of all factors. It was built inside a field where one factor had already been privileged.

This is also where the chapter needs restraint. Attention can guide people toward what genuinely deserves weight. If a neglected but critical detail is brought to the front, directed attention can improve judgment. But the same mechanism can distort judgment when the highlighted feature is merely vivid, emotionally charged, or convenient for the persuader's goal. The chapter's value depends on keeping that difference visible. Attention does not create truth. It changes felt significance.

That is the real warning embedded in the title. If attention starts becoming importance, then a listener cannot assume that what feels most pressing is necessarily what deserves the most weight. The foreground can be a guide, but it can also be a trap. A room can slowly confuse the most repeated issue with the most consequential issue, the most visible cost with the most serious cost, or the most emotionally available feature with the most meaningful feature.

Once you see that, pre-suasion looks less like ornament and more like field design. The persuader is not only trying to make the message memorable. The persuader is also shaping the ranking system through which later information will be judged. A foregrounded point does not merely sit higher in awareness. It can begin to sit higher in importance. That is a quieter form of leverage than argument, but not a weaker one.

The chapter also sharpens the risk of mental laziness. If people treat whatever is attended as what matters, then careful judgment requires a harder question: Is this issue important because it deserves that status, or because it has been made hard to ignore? That question does not cancel attention. It disciplines it. It asks whether the spotlight is revealing significance or manufacturing it.

That pressure leads directly into the next chapter. If attended things feel more important, then the next shortcut is easy to imagine. What is most prominent may also start to feel like the explanation for what is happening. Once the mind gives the foreground extra importance, it may be only one step away from giving the foreground extra causal credit as well.
