Chapter 10 asks the optimization question that follows Chapter 9's mechanism. If pre-suasion works by readying later response, which broad concepts are the smartest ones to frontload before an ask? Cialdini's answer is in the chapter title itself: *Six Main Roads to Change: Broad Boulevards as Smart Shortcuts*. The issue is no longer whether pre-suasion can work, but which wide routes are most worth preparing.

The frozen support keeps the answer narrow and specific. The strongest broad focal concepts are the six classic influence principles from *Influence*: reciprocity, liking, authority, social proof, scarcity, and consistency. Chapter 10 treats these not as a generic persuasion summary but as the broad roads most likely to shape later choice. The claim is about optimization. If a communicator wants to ready a later response efficiently, these six principles are the most reliable high-level paths to choose from.

The official support also explains why these principles matter here. They are smart shortcuts because they typically steer people when deciding what to do. That makes them especially suited to pre-suasion. A pre-suasive move works best when it prepares a concept that already helps organize judgment. Directing attention toward reciprocity, liking, authority, social proof, scarcity, or consistency means the later request enters a field already oriented around one of the strongest practical guides people use in ordinary decisions.

That is why the chapter belongs in the best-practices section. After Chapter 9 explained that readying aligned concepts changes later ease of response, Chapter 10 says some concepts are better aligned than others because they are broad boulevards rather than narrow side paths. Reciprocity can frame action as giving back. Liking can make agreement feel more congenial. Authority can make compliance seem sensible. Social proof can make following others feel prudent. Scarcity can increase felt urgency. Consistency can make follow-through feel faithful to prior commitments. The chapter's point is not that these cues always deserve victory, only that they often function as wide decision roads.

That is the sense in which they are smart shortcuts. They help people orient quickly before the full case has been weighed in detail. For a communicator, this creates efficiency. Rather than trying to prepare every possible thought, the communicator can foreground one principle that already carries strong guidance value. Pre-suasion becomes more targeted because the field is being prepared with one of the most dependable broad concepts available.

The chapter also preserves the limit that keeps shortcut value from collapsing into proof. A broad road is useful because it guides response, but that same usefulness can oversteer judgment when the request itself is weak. Reciprocity, liking, authority, social proof, scarcity, and consistency may help people decide what to do, yet none of them automatically settles what they should do. A shortcut can clarify, but it can also pre-bias.

That is why Chapter 10 has to be read with discipline. The six principles are the main roads to change, not automatic entitlements to assent. Their power lies in their breadth and familiarity, which is exactly why they require auditing. A request riding one of these roads may be easier to accept for good reason or for insufficient reason. The audience still has to separate guidance from justification.

The bridge to the next chapter is straightforward. Once the six classic principles have been identified as the broad roads most worth frontloading, the book is ready to ask whether another route belongs on the map. Chapter 11 turns to unity and the influence of being together.
