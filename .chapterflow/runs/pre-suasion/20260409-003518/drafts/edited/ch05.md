Attention does not land on the world evenly. Some cues win the race to the foreground before anyone has decided whether they deserve to. That is the force of Chapter 5, "Commanders of Attention 1: The Attractors." Cialdini is asking a prior question now. Before attention can change importance or causal judgment, what makes one feature seize attention in the first place?

The chapter's answer is that certain cues are built to attract the mind. They stand out because they signal threat, difference, or relevance to an active goal. Those properties do not make the cue true, important, or morally worthy. They make it hard to ignore. The mind treats them as early priorities because, under many conditions, missing them could be costly.

That is why attractors matter. A threatening cue can seize attention because danger is expensive to overlook. A different cue can seize attention because deviation interrupts expectation. A goal-fitting cue can seize attention because it seems immediately useful to whatever the person currently wants, fears, or is trying to solve. The common thread is not merit. It is pull. Some features come with more attentional gravity than others.

This chapter sits one step earlier than the last two. Chapter 3 explained how attention can increase felt importance. Chapter 4 explained how focality can start receiving causal credit. Chapter 5 backs up and asks how focality begins. Why is this issue the one the room keeps looking at? Often the answer is not that the issue has already been judged best. The answer is that it has the properties of an attractor.

That has direct implications for persuasion. If a communicator can place an attractor cue near a later message, the message enters a field already oriented toward what pulled attention first. A standout comparison, a threatening headline, or a frame linked to the audience's current goal can claim the opening seconds of mental life. Once that opening is occupied, everything that follows enters on terms partly set by the cue that won the first look.

The chapter's limit is what keeps it useful. Attractors do not deserve automatic trust. A cue can be highly attention-worthy in the narrow sense of capture and still be low in real importance. A noisy issue can dominate a meeting because it is vivid or unusual, not because it is the most consequential issue in the room. A goal-fitting message can feel immediately compelling because it brushes against a live desire, not because it is the wisest message available.

That distinction matters because the mind is tempted to reward capture with weight. If something wins attention fast, people often start treating it as though it has already earned more than a first look. But Chapter 5 is asking for discipline at the gateway. The first question is not whether the attractor worked. The first question is what property of the cue made it work, and whether that property tracks what should matter next.

Seen this way, attractors are not tricks layered on top of persuasion. They are part of the environment that persuasion inherits. Before an argument is judged, before a request is weighed, before a cause story is formed, some feature has already gotten there first. That feature may be a useful warning, a revealing contrast, or a self-relevant signal. It may also just be the loudest object in view.

The chapter sharpens a practical question. Which cues deserve to be front-loaded because they point toward what matters, and which cues merely front-load themselves because they are built to grab the mind? That is the difference between using attention intelligently and simply following whatever seizes it.

The bridge into the next chapter is natural. Winning attention is not the same as keeping it. Once a cue has claimed the first look, the next question is what gives it staying power. Why do some cues keep the mind returning after the initial pull has passed? That is where the magnetizers chapter begins.
