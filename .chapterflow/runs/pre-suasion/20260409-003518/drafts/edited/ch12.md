Chapter 12 shifts unity from identity to action. Chapter 11 argued that people can become a We through family, place, and other belonging cues. Chapter 12 asks how that We relationship changes when people act together instead. Its title states the move clearly: *Unity 2: Acting Together*. The chapter's claim is that unity can arise not only from shared identity, but also from shared synchronized or collaborative activity.

The frozen support keeps the mechanism narrow. We relationships can result from acting together synchronously or collaboratively. The chapter is not making a broad claim about all group activity. It is focused on unitizing action, the kind of shared movement or joint effort that makes separate people feel more like a single group. A coordinated rhythm, a shared task, or a collaborative effort can prepare that field before the later request arrives.

That matters because the official support also gives the chapter's main consequence. Unitary activity, arranged pre-suasively, can increase mutual liking and support. The later request therefore enters a changed relationship frame. It arrives after people have already moved or worked together in a way that makes additional cooperation feel easier and more natural.

This is the action-based version of unity. Chapter 11 was about being together. Chapter 12 is about functioning together. The persuasive force comes from participation itself. The audience is not only hearing a message after a shared experience. It is hearing that message after a We relationship has been strengthened through synchrony or collaboration.

The frozen support also signals that this effect reaches across many settings. The chapter gestures toward strangers, teammates, children, students, and consumer-brand loyalty. The important point is not the specific illustrations. It is that acting together can create the same kind of We frame in very different domains. The common feature is not the audience type. It is the unitizing effect of shared action.

The chapter still keeps its limit intact. Acting together can increase liking and support, but it cannot prove that the later request deserves assent. A shared warm-up, coordinated task, or collaborative exercise may make the audience more receptive, yet the proposal still has to stand on its merits. Support created through action is still support, not proof.

That is why Chapter 12 carries both promise and risk. Real collaboration can build authentic cohesion. Staged togetherness can also make a weak request feel more communal and more justified than it really is. If people mistake the feeling of We for evidence that the later ask is good, unity starts oversteering judgment again, this time through action instead of identity.

The chapter's final contribution is to name that second route to We clearly. People can become one through who they are together, and they can become one through what they do together. From there the book is ready to turn toward its closing reflections.
