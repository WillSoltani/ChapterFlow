A persuasive move can be clever long before it is fair. That is the pressure Chapter 13 brings into view. After spending many chapters on how attention, association, unity, and timing can prepare receptivity, the book steps back to an earlier question. Before choosing what to place in front of an audience, a pre-suader has to decide whether the approach should be used on ethical grounds at all.

The title states that move directly: *Ethical Use: A Pre-Pre-Suasive Consideration*. The chapter is not adding a warning label after the tactic has already been chosen. It places ethics before tactic design. A communicator may know how to arrange a setting, load a cue, or ready a relationship frame. Chapter 13 asks whether that power is being used to inform people fairly or to press them toward a result they might reject under clearer conditions.

The frozen support keeps the argument narrow. It says commercial communicators often place profit above ethics. That pressure matters because it tempts people to treat effectiveness as the only serious test. If a setup works, it can start to feel justified by success alone. The chapter pushes back by saying the earliest persuasive decision is not simply what will work. It is whether the method deserves to be used.

The chapter also gives a practical reason, not only a moral one, for that restraint. It argues against unethical use by citing studies indicating that such tactics undermine organizational profits in three potent ways. The frozen support does not spell out those three ways, and the chapter should not invent them. But the direction is clear enough. A communicator who puts ethics second in the name of gain may end up damaging the very organizational outcomes the tactic was supposed to serve.

That makes Chapter 13 a checkpoint inside the book's optimization section. Earlier chapters asked how to increase receptivity. This one asks what kind of receptivity should never be engineered in the first place. The question is not whether pre-suasion can move people. The question is whether the move respects the audience enough to deserve its influence.

The chapter therefore keeps one distinction firm. A tactic can succeed in the short run and still fail the ethical test. It can even look like a profit-smart shortcut while quietly working against longer-term organizational interests. Chapter 13 treats ethics as an earlier gate, not a later apology. From there the book can turn to its final concern: not only how influence begins, but whether the change it creates can last.
