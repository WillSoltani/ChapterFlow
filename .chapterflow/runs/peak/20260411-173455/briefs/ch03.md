Core Claim:
This chapter argues that expert performance depends on rich mental representations: internal models that let a person notice what matters, predict what comes next, remember meaningful structure, and correct errors faster than a novice can. Practice does not only strengthen effort. It builds better representations.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
Chapter 2 established that humans adapt. Chapter 3 explains what high-level adaptation is often building toward. Its job is to make expertise feel less mystical by showing that experts perceive and organize a domain differently from novices.

Author Logic Chain:
First the chapter explains that experts do not merely possess more effort or more confidence.
Then it shows that experts carry richer internal models that change what they see and recall.
Then it argues that those representations support prediction and self-correction.
Then it preserves the limit that representations are domain-specific and cannot be borrowed instantly by motivation alone.

Required Anchors:
- experts notice meaningful structure that novices miss
- mental representations improve memory because they organize information rather than merely storing more pieces
- prediction and self-correction depend on better internal models
- the limit that representations are built slowly inside a specific field

Counterarguments or Limits:
The frozen bundle supports the concept strongly at the mechanism level, but it does not justify grand claims that representations transfer cleanly across unrelated domains. Keep the argument field-specific. Preserve the limit that better representations are built through long contact with the right problems and feedback.
