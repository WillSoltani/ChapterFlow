The next threshold in Frankl's memoir is not insight. It is arrival. The transport reaches Auschwitz, and ordinary scale collapses almost at once. The first thing that matters is not a developed philosophy of suffering but the fact that people are being sorted under conditions they do not understand and cannot control. The chapter's emotional weather comes from that sudden collision between the old mind and a system already moving toward death.

Selection gives the shock its shape. Frankl makes clear that many new arrivals are sent directly toward death, while the few who remain are left to understand only in fragments what has happened. That fragmentation matters. Terror does not begin as full comprehension. It begins as a broken reading of signs, glances, motions, and rumors. The body knows danger before the mind has made a clean picture of it. That is why the chapter feels so unstable. Reality is arriving faster than understanding can organize it.

One of the chapter's sharpest ideas is the delusion of reprieve. The mind reaches for a safer story even when the evidence is turning against it. This is not stupidity. It is one of the first psychological reflexes available under unbearable pressure. If the truth is too large to absorb all at once, the self bargains for a smaller version. The delusion buys a few more moments of internal continuity. It also shows how badly the first phase of camp life distorts ordinary judgment.

Frankl treats shock as the first inmate phase because the human system is not built to absorb this kind of transition cleanly. The first days are crowded with filth, beatings, fear, and the immediate nearness of death. In that atmosphere, suicidal thinking appears not as melodrama but as part of the psychic collapse of old assumptions. The point is not that everyone reacts in exactly the same way. The point is that camp induction attacks the very frame by which a person had previously measured danger, dignity, and possibility.

That attack produces a rough kind of adjustment, but the chapter is careful about what that means. Adjustment here is not wisdom. It is the beginning of survival narrowing. Perception contracts. Emotional range changes. The mind starts shedding what it cannot carry if it is going to make it through the next hour. There is something adaptive in that contraction. A person who remained fully open to the whole horror at every second might not last. Yet the adaptation already carries a cost, because the same narrowing that protects survival can also begin deadening older moral and emotional reflexes.

This is why the chapter matters as more than a record of terror. It shows the first compromise forced by extremity. The self does not simply stand upright and noble at the threshold of horror. It bargains, misreads, narrows, and adjusts. That does not make the response contemptible. It makes it human. Frankl's early camp psychology is powerful because it refuses both romantic heroism and detached judgment. The first phase is shock because the old world has been broken too quickly for anything calmer to be honest.

So the chapter leaves a hard question behind. If shock is the first shield against complete psychic collapse, when does that shield begin turning into something more dangerous? The next movement in the memoir has to answer that. Once terror stops being a fresh blow and starts becoming routine, what kind of inner life remains, and what parts of the older self survive the narrowing?
