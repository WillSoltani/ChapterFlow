Core Claim:
This chapter shows that the first phase of camp life is not yet philosophy but psychological impact. Arrival, selection, filth, crowding, and immediate proximity to death shatter ordinary assumptions and create shock, delusion, and suicidal thinking. The distinct move is to show how a human being does not become noble at the threshold of horror. The mind first narrows, bargains, misreads, and tries to survive the first collision.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
It is the induction chapter. It should feel impossible to confuse with the later apathy/freedom chapter because it is governed by arrival pressure, selection, delusion of reprieve, and the stunned first adaptation to camp reality.

Author Logic Chain:
First Frankl shows the transition from transport to camp selection and the immediate nearness of death.
Then he shows the psychology of shock, including delusion, numbness, and the collapse of ordinary moral scale.
Then he concludes that the first survival posture is not insight but a brutal adjustment to conditions no normal life had prepared a person to meet.

Counterarguments or Limits:
The chapter can describe first-phase psychology, but it should not overstate it as universal law for every prisoner. Keep the claims close to Frankl's witness and the frozen summary support. Do not invent scenes or dialogue to make the chapter more vivid than the source allows.
