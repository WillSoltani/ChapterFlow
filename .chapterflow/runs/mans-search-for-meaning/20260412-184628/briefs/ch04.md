Core Claim:
After the memoir establishes that response freedom and meaning can survive extremity, Frankl turns to explicit theory. The chapter's central claim is that the primary human drive is the will to meaning, not pleasure alone or power alone, and that many modern disorders grow from frustrated meaning rather than instinct frustration. The distinct move is to explain existential vacuum, meaning through work/love/suffering, and therapeutic tools such as paradoxical intention without letting the theory float free of the memoir's pressure-tested seriousness.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
It is the theory chapter. It should feel impossible to confuse with the memoir chapters because it introduces logotherapy, existential vacuum, noogenic neurosis, self-transcendence, and therapeutic method directly. At the same time, it must still feel tethered to the witness that gave these ideas weight.

Author Logic Chain:
First Frankl distinguishes the will to meaning from rival motivational explanations.
Then he explains how meaning frustration creates emptiness and specific forms of suffering.
Then he outlines routes to meaning and therapeutic tools that address meaning-centered distress.

Common Misreadings:

Counterarguments or Limits:
The theory chapter can sound broader than the memoir allows. Keep it disciplined. Do not overclaim scientific certainty, and do not import unsupported case details. Preserve the line between a meaning-centered framework and a promise that all suffering becomes meaningful automatically.

Previous Chapter Bridge: the memoir showed constrained freedom and meaning under pressure; this chapter explains the framework Frankl thinks can account for that.
Next Chapter Bridge: the postscript will test whether tragic optimism can hold pain, guilt, and death without sentimentality.
