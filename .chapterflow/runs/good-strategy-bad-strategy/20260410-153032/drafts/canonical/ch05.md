The earlier chapters spent time clearing ground. They separated strategy from slogans, showed that leverage matters, identified the habits of bad strategy, and explained why institutions keep producing it. Chapter 5 changes direction. It gives the reader the compact positive structure that makes strategy real. Rumelt calls it the kernel. The point of the word is discipline. A strategy does not need to begin as a thick document. It needs to contain the few parts without which the rest becomes decoration.

The first part is diagnosis. Diagnosis names the governing obstacle or challenge. That sounds basic, but the earlier chapters already showed why it is difficult. A diagnosis is not a slogan about the future. It is an explanation of what pressure actually controls the situation. Until that pressure is named, a leader cannot tell which facts matter most, which tradeoffs are necessary, or what kind of response the strategy should prefer.

The second part is guiding policy. Once the obstacle is identified, the strategist needs an overall approach for dealing with it. This is where many weak plans drift into aspiration. A guiding policy is not a wish, a value statement, or a promise to improve everything at once. It is the chosen direction for dealing with the diagnosed challenge. It narrows the field. It tells people what sort of response will count as fitting and what sort will not.

The third part is coherent action. Policy without action remains decorative, but action without diagnosis and policy becomes the kind of initiative pile the book has already criticized. Coherent action means that the concrete moves reinforce one another because they serve one guiding approach to one diagnosed problem. The actions may be varied, but they stop pulling in unrelated directions. That is why coherence matters more than mere activity. The force comes from coordination around a center.

The strength of the kernel is that its parts depend on one another. Diagnosis without guiding policy becomes observation without direction. Guiding policy without coherent action becomes rhetoric. Coherent action without diagnosis becomes motion without logic. The kernel therefore works as a compact integrity test. If one part is missing, the whole strategy weakens quickly and begins to resemble the failures described earlier in the book.

This is also why the kernel is the positive answer to bad strategy. Decorative ambition tried to skip diagnosis. Broad consensus language tried to avoid a real policy choice. Initiative piles looked like action without coherence. The kernel closes those escape routes. It demands a named obstacle, a chosen directional response, and a set of reinforcing actions tied to that choice.

The chapter matters because it turns strategy from a mood into a structure. Once a leader understands the kernel, the question changes again. If diagnosis, guiding policy, and coherent action define the form of good strategy, then the next question is where the power for that strategy comes from. That is what sends the book forward into sources of power.
