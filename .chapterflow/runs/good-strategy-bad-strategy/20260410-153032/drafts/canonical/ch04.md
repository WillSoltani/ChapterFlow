Bad strategy matters partly because it keeps being produced. The failure is not only that some plans are empty. The deeper problem is that institutions can keep generating the same emptiness year after year while calling it unity, momentum, or alignment. That is the chapter's opening move. Bad strategy is not merely a mistake that smart people occasionally make. It can become the normal output of a group that has learned how to sound serious without forcing the decisions that would make seriousness real.

That recurrence makes sense once diagnosis is understood as a social act rather than only an analytical one. A real diagnosis does not simply name a problem. It ranks problems. It tells people which bottleneck matters most and which projects are now secondary. That is why clarity can threaten consensus. The moment a governing obstacle is named, someone loses priority, some existing work looks less important, and some attractive story about broad progress stops being available.

Exclusion is where the cost becomes visible. Good strategy gains force by refusing attractive alternatives, but refusal is rarely popular inside an institution. A project has an owner. A slogan has admirers. A program has people whose status is tied to its survival. When diagnosis begins to cut, the resistance is not always irrational. People are often protecting morale, turf, reputation, or the hope that everything important can still remain included. The problem is that those understandable protections can still keep strategy weak.

That is why broad ambition can be rewarded so easily. A plan that promises growth, innovation, trust, capability, and speed gives something to almost everyone. It sounds positive. It lowers the immediate temperature in the room. It also delays the moment when somebody has to say which problem governs the rest. Consensus language can therefore create a false appearance of alignment. The room feels calm because no real exclusion has yet been tested.

Chapter 1 already provided the positive contrast. Good strategy looks like coherent action tied to a real obstacle. Chapter 3 showed the negative version: decorative ambition, initiative piles, and leverage blindness. Chapter 4 adds the production logic underneath both. Strategy-free activity persists because institutions can reward motion and optimism more quickly than they reward painful clarity. A team gets credit for energy now. The strategic bill for vagueness arrives later.

This makes the test sharper. When a room appears unusually united, it is worth asking whether the unity comes from actual strategic agreement or from the fact that nobody has yet forced a hard choice. Real agreement often becomes possible only after conflict has passed through the room and the group has accepted what will not be protected. Before that point, harmony may simply be ambiguity doing its social work.

The chapter therefore carries a mixed moral tone. It is easy to mock bad strategy as stupidity, but that explanation is too thin. Some of the forces producing it are recognizably human and institutional. People want cohesion. Leaders want momentum. Groups want plans that do not immediately split the room. The strategic cost is that comforting language can become a substitute for diagnosis.

Once that causal logic is visible, the next chapter can move in a different direction. It is no longer enough to diagnose bad strategy or explain why it persists. The reader now needs the positive structure that can survive those pressures. That is why the book turns next to the kernel of good strategy.
