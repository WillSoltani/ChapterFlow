Once the kernel is in place, another question appears immediately. Diagnosis, guiding policy, and coherent action tell a leader how strategy is structured, but they do not yet explain why one strategy hits hard and another stays respectable but weak. Chapter 6 turns to that missing issue by asking where strategic power comes from.

The answer is not effort by itself. Teams can work intensely and still remain trapped inside the same terms that favor the obstacle. Power has to do with conditions that make some moves more potent than others. It sits in an asymmetry, a position, a fit, a coordination pattern, or some other feature of the situation that changes what one action is worth. That is why the chapter belongs after the kernel. Form alone does not create force.

This also means not every apparent advantage deserves to be called a source of power. Some successes are only tricks that work once. Some tactics look impressive only because competitors have not answered yet. Some leaders copy the visible surface of a winning move without possessing the underlying condition that made it powerful in the first place. Chapter 6 presses the reader to ask what makes an advantage durable rather than merely dramatic.

That distinction sharpens an idea already visible in Chapter 2. Discovering power means finding the hidden leverage in a situation, not just pushing harder inside the obvious contest. The difference now is that Chapter 6 treats leverage as a downstream move. Before force can be applied well, the strategist has to understand where force will actually multiply. A leader who confuses activity with power spends energy in the wrong lane and then mistakes exhaustion for seriousness.

The chapter therefore adds a harder standard to the kernel. A strategy can have diagnosis, policy, and action and still be weaker than it appears if it has not located a real source of power. The structure may be orderly. The actions may even reinforce one another. But if they do not connect to a condition that changes the odds, the strategy can remain coherent without becoming strong.

That is why surface imitation is so dangerous. A rival may copy a visible practice, a target, or a sequence of moves and still fail because the real power sat underneath in some less obvious advantage. Strategic strength is not usually hiding in the most theatrical part of a plan. It is more often hiding in the condition that lets ordinary-looking moves land with unusual effect.

The practical lesson is to ask a different question before adding effort. What in this situation makes one line of action stronger than another? If the answer is only intensity, optimism, or volume, the strategy may still be missing its power source. Once that source is visible, the next chapter can ask how leverage should be used against it.
