Once strategy can see movement over time, another hard question appears. Why do some systems keep following the same path even when change is clearly needed, and why do others slowly lose sharpness without anyone obviously trying to ruin them? Chapter 14 answers with inertia and entropy.

The chapter matters because it shifts attention away from simple blame. Sometimes a group resists change not because people are especially malicious or lazy, but because the structure keeps rewarding the old path. Existing routines, handoffs, incentives, and habits can make redirection expensive even when the need is visible.

That is the logic of inertia. Inertia means built-in resistance to change. A system has weight. It has established processes, expectations, and investments. Those features make the current direction easier to continue and make a new direction harder to adopt.

Entropy names a different but related problem. Even when a system is not actively resisting a new direction, it can still lose coherence over time. Priorities blur, coordination weakens, standards drift, and the effort that once held the whole together starts leaking away.

This matters strategically because good intention is not enough to overcome either force. A leader can ask for change and still fail if the structure keeps reproducing the old behavior. A team can care about quality and still decay if nothing keeps discipline, focus, and coordination aligned over time.

That is why the chapter pushes toward structural diagnosis instead of moral complaint. If inertia is the problem, strategy has to ask what in the system makes the old path easy and the new path costly. If entropy is the problem, strategy has to ask where coherence is eroding and what counterforce can keep the whole from drifting apart.

The practical lesson is to treat drag and decay as part of strategic reality. A strategist has to see where the system resists redirection, where energy leaks away, and where maintenance of coherence requires more than reminders or speeches.

This extends the prior chapter directly. Chapter 13 showed that movement over time creates openings and traps. Chapter 14 explains why some of those trajectories harden and why some systems degrade unless active effort keeps them coherent.

Seen this way, strategy is not only about choosing a direction. It is also about understanding the built environment that carries the current direction forward and the slow weakening that appears when coherence is not continuously renewed.

That perspective prepares the final synthesis chapter. Once inertia and entropy are visible, the strategist can integrate diagnosis, counterforce, and coherent action into one larger practice instead of treating resistance and decay as surprising accidents.
