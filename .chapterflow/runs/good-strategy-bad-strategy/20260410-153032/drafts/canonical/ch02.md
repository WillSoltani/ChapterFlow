Most people remember David and Goliath as a story about courage. Rumelt treats it as a story about strategy. The point is not that the weaker side simply believed harder. The point is that the fight stopped being a strength contest once David refused the terms Goliath wanted. A close, heavy clash favored size, armor, and brute force. Distance, mobility, and range changed the problem. What looked like mismatch under one description became a different contest under another. That is the chapter's first lesson about power. Power in strategy often hides inside the way the situation is framed.

That is a harder idea than it first appears. Most struggling organizations already know they want more power. They want more budget, more people, more speed, or more market share. Rumelt's move is different. He asks where effective power is actually sitting in the situation. Sometimes it is not in having more of everything. Sometimes it is in finding the asymmetry that makes one kind of move count more than another. A strategist who sees that hidden advantage does not merely work harder. They work on different terms.

Wal-Mart gives the chapter a business version of the same logic. Its advantage was not just one efficient store or one low-price tactic. The power sat in the network design. Distribution, coordination, and system-level management reinforced the stores from underneath. That matters because rivals could copy surface features without copying the engine. They could imitate isolated practices and still miss the source of force. Rumelt's point is that visible success often rests on a less visible architecture.

That distinction matters well beyond retail. When people talk loosely about best practices, they often copy the thing they can see first. They copy the product feature, the meeting format, the pricing move, the slogan, or the staffing ratio. But if the power came from how those pieces fit together in a larger system, imitation stays shallow. The rival looks similar from a distance and weaker up close. Discovering power means finding the part of the design that actually carries the advantage.

Marshall and Roche give the chapter a still sharper version of this logic. Their Cold War strategy did not depend on matching Soviet strength weapon for weapon. Instead, they looked for ways to use American technological strengths to impose higher costs on Soviet responses. That is a different kind of competition. It asks how one side can make the other spend more, strain more, or adapt on worse terms. The hidden power is not only in having capability. It is in shaping the contest so the other side pays a heavier price to keep up.

This is why strategy and force are not the same thing. A larger opponent can still be strategically vulnerable if the contest can be reframed around mobility, network design, timing, or asymmetric cost. A smaller player can still waste its chance if it fights on the strongest player's favorite terms. Rumelt is teaching the reader to look for where the situation bends. The surface facts remain the same, but their strategic meaning changes once one hidden leverage point becomes visible.

There is also a limit inside this lesson. Not every clever angle is real power. Some are only tricks that work once. A durable source of power changes the contest in a way that can be reinforced. David's range advantage mattered because it truly changed the kind of fight taking place. Wal-Mart's advantage mattered because the network kept strengthening the stores. Marshall and Roche's logic mattered because it connected real strengths to the opponent's real weaknesses. Cleverness without that deeper fit can create surprise without creating staying power.

That is why Chapter 2 belongs right after the opening distinction between good and bad strategy. Chapter 1 taught that strategy must answer a real obstacle. Chapter 2 adds the next demand: find the hidden leverage inside the situation before you decide how to answer. Without that second step, leaders drift toward imitation, head-on matching, or gestures that feel bold but never touch the real source of strength. Once hidden power becomes visible, bad strategy also becomes easier to spot, because one mark of bad strategy is that it ignores or never discovers the leverage already present.
