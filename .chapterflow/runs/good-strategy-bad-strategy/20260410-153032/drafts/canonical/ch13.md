Selective growth solves only part of the strategic problem. Once a strategy chooses the right kind of expansion, another question appears. How will the situation change over time, and what new openings or traps will that movement create? Chapter 13 turns to dynamics by asking the reader to stop thinking only in still pictures.

A still picture can be misleading because strategy does not live in frozen positions. People react. Conditions shift. Small moves alter what later moves become possible. What looks balanced at one moment may become vulnerable in the next because the sequence has started to change the field.

That is why the chapter treats dynamics as more than speed. Moving quickly is not the same as moving strategically. Dynamics means understanding sequence, timing, reaction, and the way one action changes the menu of later possibilities.

This matters because timing can create openings that static analysis misses. A move that looks modest now may become powerful because it arrives before resistance gathers, before alternatives close, or before the other side recognizes what is changing. The opportunity appears not only from the move itself but from when it enters the sequence.

The reverse is also true. Motion can create traps. A strategy can gain momentum in a direction that looks promising early, only to discover that the next steps become more expensive, more constrained, or more exposed because the sequence was misread. The problem is not merely that the move was active. The problem is that it changed the future path badly.

This is why dynamics has to include reaction. The strategist is not moving through empty space. Other actors, internal systems, and external pressures answer back. Every important move changes what others notice, how they adjust, and which options become easier or harder afterward.

The practical lesson is to ask not only whether a move improves the current position, but also what path it sets in motion. Does it open room, sharpen timing, and make later steps stronger? Or does it create burden, predictability, and a trap that only becomes obvious once the sequence is already underway?

Chapter 13 therefore extends Chapter 12 in a precise way. Growth had to be chosen selectively because larger scale could either strengthen the strategy or dilute it. Dynamics now asks how chosen motion changes the later field, and why a strategy needs to reason about time if it wants to avoid building its own problems.

Seen this way, the strategist has to think in trajectories rather than snapshots. A sound move is not judged only by what it wins today. It is also judged by the shape of the next position it creates, the reactions it invites, and the openings or closures it generates over time.

That change in perspective prepares the next chapter well. Once movement over time is visible, it becomes easier to see why systems resist change, accumulate drag, and decay in patterned ways. Dynamics explains why the future is shaped by sequence. Inertia and entropy will explain why some of those sequences become hard to reverse.
