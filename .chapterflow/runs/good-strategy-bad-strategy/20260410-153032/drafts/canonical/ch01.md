By September 1997, Apple was close enough to collapse that ordinary management language had stopped meaning much. The company had too many products, too many channels, too many moving parts, and not enough time. Steve Jobs did not answer that pressure with a grand promise about innovation. He answered it by cutting. He cut desktop models from a cluttered menu down toward one. He cut portable and handheld lines back to a single laptop. He cut peripherals. He cut retailers. He simplified the supply chain and pushed manufacturing outward. He talked Microsoft into an investment that bought the company room to breathe. The move looked plain. That was the point. The problem was not a shortage of aspiration. The problem was that Apple had become too spread out to survive.

That opening matters because most people use the word strategy for something much softer. They mean ambition. They mean vision statements, growth targets, or energetic plans with many parts. Rumelt starts in a harsher place. A strategy is not everything you would like to do. It is a focused response to the obstacle actually in front of you. Jobs's turnaround qualifies because the pieces fit. The cuts, the channel changes, the manufacturing decision, and the Microsoft money all served one governing purpose: make Apple small and coherent enough to live long enough for better opportunities to matter. The moves were coordinated. They were also painful. That is one reason they looked less glamorous than the strategies people prefer to talk about.

The chapter's first sharp insight is that good strategy often looks obvious after someone else has done the hard part of naming the real problem. Of course Apple needed fewer models if customers could not even tell which machine to buy. Of course it needed fewer retailers if supporting all of them multiplied complexity and markup. Of course it needed a simpler operating logic if the company was bleeding cash. Yet the obviousness arrives after the diagnosis. Before that, organizations usually protect every initiative, every constituency, and every hopeful storyline. They call that flexibility. Often it is just refusal to choose.

This is why Rumelt's contrast with bad strategy lands so cleanly. Bad strategy is not merely a weak version of the real thing. It is a different kind of object. It often comes wrapped in serious language, but when you look closely, it contains goals without a path or activity without a center. An organization says it will grow, innovate, transform, align, expand, and improve. None of those words tells you what obstacle matters most. None tells you what tradeoff leadership is prepared to make. None explains why these actions belong together instead of sitting side by side like a hopeful shopping list.

Rumelt drives the contrast home with the executives he interviewed in the electronics and telecommunications world. When he asked them about the leading competitor in their sector, they could usually tell a coherent story. A change in technology or demand opened a window. The leader recognized it, moved early enough, and got the response right. Those executives could describe strategy when it belonged to someone else. But when Rumelt asked about their own companies' strategies, the answers turned into motion without logic: alliances, feedback systems, foreign market pushes, software shifts, firmware updates, one initiative stacked on top of another. The replies sounded busy. They did not sound governed.

That gap is the chapter's real lesson. People are often better at recognizing strategy from the outside than at demanding it from themselves. From the outside, the winner's choices look connected. From the inside, every unfinished project and every political interest argues for survival. So bad strategy does not always arrive as stupidity. It often arrives as accumulation. Leaders keep adding goals because goals offend fewer people than tradeoffs do. They keep announcing initiatives because activity is easier to defend than exclusion. The result is not a cruelly simple mistake. It is a pile of respectable evasions.

The Apple example helps because it strips away the romance. Jobs did not save the company by sounding more strategic than everyone else. He saved it by making the company less contradictory. He reduced what Apple was trying to be so that the remaining pieces could reinforce one another. That is what coherence looks like in practice. It is not abstract brilliance. It is a set of choices that stop pulling against each other. Once that standard is visible, a lot of strategy talk becomes easier to judge. If a plan names no obstacle, asks for no sacrifice, and coordinates nothing, it may be ambitious. It is not strategy.

There is a harder edge inside that claim. Simplification can look timid from the outside, especially in cultures that reward expansion, slogans, and visible energy. Cutting back can feel like surrender. Rumelt's point is not that shrinking is always wise. It is that a narrowing move becomes strategic when it concentrates effort against the real bottleneck. If the company is dying from complexity, more offerings are not courage. They are drift. If the obstacle is incoherence, then simplification is not retreat from the fight. It is the first move that makes the fight winnable.

That is why the chapter matters before the rest of the book can do its work. It gives you a test. When someone says strategy, ask what obstacle governs the answer. Ask what choice has been made that excludes attractive alternatives. Ask how the actions reinforce one another instead of merely coexisting. If those questions produce nothing but goals, values, or motion, you are probably looking at bad strategy in polite clothing. Once that distinction lands, the next problem gets sharper. If real strategy is a focused response to a real obstacle, where does the power in that response come from? What hidden leverage or asymmetry turns one coherent move into a decisive one?
