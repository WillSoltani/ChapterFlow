Once strategy has concentrated force, another question becomes unavoidable. What should growth mean now? Chapter 12 answers by treating growth not as an automatic virtue, but as a strategic outcome that has to be judged by fit, cost, and consequence.

The chapter matters because expansion can look impressive while quietly weakening the strategy that made progress possible in the first place. A plan can gain scale, add activity, or widen reach and still lose force if that growth carries burdens the strategy cannot absorb.

That is why the chapter pushes against automatic-growth thinking. Bigger is not automatically stronger. Growth can strengthen a strategy, but it can also dilute focus, add cost, and create new constraints that weaken the whole.

This makes selective logic essential. The strategist has to ask which growth moves preserve concentrated force and which ones spread it too thin. Growth should be chosen, not worshiped.

Costs and constraints sit inside this judgment. Expansion can demand more coordination, more resources, more attention, and more tolerance for complexity. If those burdens outrun the strategy's capacity, growth stops being a benefit and becomes a drag.

The chapter therefore reframes growth as a consequence of good strategy rather than the definition of it. The right question is not whether the organization is getting bigger. The right question is whether expansion is strengthening the strategic position or quietly dissolving it.

The practical lesson is that growth should be judged selectively, with force and fit in mind. Once that logic is clear, later chapters can look at dynamics, timing, and the ways motion over time creates openings as well as traps.
