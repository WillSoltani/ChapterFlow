Once a strategy has a credible next objective, another question appears. Will the system actually move if action advances broadly, or is there one weak necessary link that caps the whole result? Chapter 9 answers by turning to chain-link systems, where overall performance is limited by the weakest indispensable link.

The chapter matters because some systems do not reward average strength. A team can improve many parts, celebrate strong areas, and still fail if one necessary step keeps breaking. In that kind of system, progress is not determined by the average quality of the links. It is determined by the weakest one that the whole chain depends on.

That is why the chapter pushes against compensation thinking. Strong links elsewhere do not cancel the failure of an indispensable weak link. A system can have excellence in many places and still underperform because one necessary connection keeps capping what the whole can do.

This also distinguishes chain-link systems from systems where strengths can add up or trade off more loosely. In a chain-link system, one broken or weak required part can dominate the result. The strategist therefore has to ask whether broad improvement is missing the real limit.

The practical implication is that repair has to focus on the limiting link. Effort spread across the whole system may feel balanced, but it may leave the true bottleneck untouched. If one indispensable link is capping performance, the right move is often to strengthen that link first rather than polish strengths that are already good enough.

The chapter also warns against the comfort of local success. Teams often prefer visible wins in stronger areas because those wins feel rewarding and easier to claim. But if the weak indispensable link remains weak, the whole system can continue to fail despite those local gains.

The practical lesson is to identify whether the system behaves like a chain and, if it does, find the limiting link that the whole result depends on. Once that logic is clear, later chapters can move deeper into design, constraints, and the structures strategy has to respect.
