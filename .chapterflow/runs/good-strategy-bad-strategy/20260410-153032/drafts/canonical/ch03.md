Bad strategy is dangerous because it rarely announces itself as failure. It arrives in the tone of seriousness. The plan has goals, pillars, values, initiatives, targets, and urgency. It may even have slides full of arrows and deadlines. What it often does not have is a governing obstacle. The language sounds strategic because it gestures toward importance, but nothing in it tells you what problem actually controls the situation. That is the chapter's opening pressure. A plan can feel substantial and still have no center.

That matters because weak execution and bad strategy are not the same problem. Weak execution starts with a real strategy and then stumbles in carrying it out. Bad strategy starts earlier. It never faces the difficulty clearly enough to deserve the word strategy in the first place. Chapter 1 already established the test in positive form: coherent action tied to a real obstacle. Chapter 3 turns that test around. If the obstacle stays unnamed, if the actions do not fit together, and if no hard choice has been made, the plan is not struggling strategy. It is strategy-shaped avoidance.

One reason this failure survives is that broad ambition is politically easier than diagnosis. Diagnosis threatens projects, status, and optimism. It tells people that not everything important can remain equally protected. Initiative piles avoid that pain. A leadership group can promise growth, innovation, alignment, capability, trust, and speed all at once because each word pleases some constituency and excludes almost nobody. The result sounds energetic. But energy is not the same as logic. When everything remains a priority, nothing has been given the force that comes from concentration.

This is why bad strategy often hides inside accumulation rather than laziness. The team is not doing nothing. It is doing too many things that do not reinforce one another. One project answers one concern, another project answers a different concern, and a third survives because nobody wants the conflict of cutting it. Soon the plan becomes a storage unit for unresolved tradeoffs. The document grows. The center disappears. A strategy earns coherence by refusing attractive alternatives. Bad strategy protects them side by side and calls the pile balance.

Chapter 2 adds another way to recognize the failure. Good strategy looks for hidden leverage in the situation. Bad strategy often never gets that far. It stays at the level of visible aspiration. It asks how to work harder in the existing contest instead of asking where the contest bends. A group may pour effort into the wrong lane even when an asymmetry is available, simply because finding leverage requires the same uncomfortable discipline that diagnosis requires. It means admitting that the obvious contest may be the wrong contest.

That gives the reader a sharper test than style or confidence. Ask what obstacle governs the answer. Ask what source of leverage the plan intends to use. Ask what attractive action has been cut so the remaining moves can reinforce one another. If those questions produce only mood, motion, or a list of desirable outcomes, you are not looking at unfinished strategy. You are looking at decorative ambition. The decoration may be polished, expensive, and emotionally satisfying. It is still decoration.

The chapter also carries a harder implication. Bad strategy is often easier to defend than good strategy because it spreads discomfort so widely that nobody feels singled out by the truth. A real diagnosis creates winners and losers inside the organization. It kills projects, rejects language people love, and exposes places where effort has been wasted. Bad strategy preserves hope by preserving ambiguity. That is why it persists even in experienced institutions. The next chapter can therefore ask the question that now becomes unavoidable: if the symptoms are so visible, why do people keep producing them?
