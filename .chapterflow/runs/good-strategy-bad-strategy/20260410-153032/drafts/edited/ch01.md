By September 1997, Apple was close enough to collapse that ordinary management language had stopped helping. The company had too many products, too many channels, too many moving parts, and not enough time. Steve Jobs did not answer that pressure with a grand promise about innovation. He answered it by cutting. He reduced desktop models, shrank the portable line, cut peripherals, cut retailers, simplified manufacturing, and secured Microsoft's investment to buy the company room to breathe. The response looked plain. That was exactly why it worked. Apple did not suffer from a lack of aspiration. It suffered from sprawl.

Rumelt starts here because most people use the word strategy for something softer than this. They mean ambition, vision, or a list of things the organization hopes to improve. His standard is tougher. Strategy is a focused response to the obstacle actually in front of you. Jobs's turnaround fits because the moves were coordinated around one problem: Apple had become too spread out to survive. The cuts, channel changes, and supply-chain simplification all served that diagnosis. They were painful, but they belonged together.

One reason good strategy looks unusual is that it often appears obvious only after someone has accepted the tradeoffs. Of course Apple needed fewer models if customers could not tell which machine to buy. Of course it needed fewer retailers if every channel multiplied complexity and markup. Of course it needed a simpler operating logic if the company was bleeding cash. The hard part was not inventing flashy moves. The hard part was naming the real obstacle and then refusing to protect everything at once.

That is where the chapter's contrast becomes useful. Bad strategy is not just weak strategy. It is goals without a path or activity without a center. An organization says it will grow, innovate, align, expand, and improve. None of that tells you what obstacle matters most. None of it tells you what leadership is willing to give up. None of it explains why the actions reinforce one another instead of sitting together in a respectable pile.

Rumelt sharpens the point with the executives he interviewed in electronics and telecommunications. Asked how the leaders in their sectors became leaders, they could tell a coherent story. A change in technology or demand opened a window, and the winning company responded clearly enough to take it. But when Rumelt asked about their own companies' strategies, the answers turned into motion without logic: alliances, feedback systems, foreign expansion, firmware changes, internet updates, one initiative stacked on another. The replies sounded active. They did not sound governed.

That gap matters because people are often better at recognizing strategy from the outside than demanding it from themselves. From the outside, the winner's choices look connected. From the inside, every project and every constituency argues for survival. So bad strategy often arrives as accumulation rather than stupidity. Goals offend fewer people than tradeoffs do. Initiatives are easier to defend than exclusions. A company keeps adding programs and calls the pile momentum.

The Apple example strips away that comfort. Jobs did not save the company by sounding more strategic than everyone else. He made Apple less contradictory. He reduced what it was trying to be so the remaining pieces could reinforce one another. That is what coherence looks like. It is not a mood or a slogan. It is a set of choices that stop pulling against each other.

There is still a live tension inside that claim. Simplification can look timid from the outside, especially in cultures that reward expansion and visible energy. Cutting back is not automatically wise. It becomes strategic when it concentrates effort against the real bottleneck. If the company is dying from complexity, more offerings are not courage. They are drift. If the obstacle is incoherence, simplification is not retreat. It is the move that makes a later recovery possible.

The chapter leaves the reader with a test worth keeping. When someone says strategy, ask what obstacle governs the answer. Ask what attractive alternative has been rejected. Ask how the actions reinforce one another instead of merely coexisting. If those questions produce only goals, values, and motion, you are probably looking at bad strategy in polite clothing. Once that distinction is visible, the next question changes. If strategy is a focused response to a real obstacle, where does the power in that response come from?
