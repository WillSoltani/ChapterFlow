The earlier chapters cleared ground. They separated strategy from slogans, showed that leverage matters, identified bad strategy, and explained why institutions keep producing it. Chapter 5 turns positive. It gives the compact structure that makes strategy real. Rumelt calls it the kernel. The point of that word is discipline. A strategy does not need to begin as a thick document. It needs the few parts without which the rest becomes decoration.

The first part is diagnosis. Diagnosis names the governing obstacle or challenge. That sounds simple, but the earlier chapters already showed why it is hard. A diagnosis is not a future-state slogan. It is an explanation of what pressure actually controls the situation. Until that pressure is named, a leader cannot tell which facts matter most, which tradeoffs are necessary, or what kind of response the strategy should prefer.

The second part is guiding policy. Once the obstacle is identified, the strategist needs an overall approach for dealing with it. This is where weak plans often drift into aspiration. A guiding policy is not a wish, a value statement, or a promise to improve everything at once. It is the chosen direction for dealing with the diagnosed challenge. It narrows the field and tells people what kind of response fits.

The third part is coherent action. Policy without action remains decorative, but action without diagnosis and policy becomes the initiative pile the book has already criticized. Coherent action means the concrete moves reinforce one another because they serve one guiding approach to one diagnosed problem. The actions may differ, but they stop pulling in unrelated directions. The force comes from coordination around a center.

The strength of the kernel is that its parts depend on one another. Diagnosis without guiding policy becomes observation without direction. Guiding policy without coherent action becomes rhetoric. Coherent action without diagnosis becomes motion without logic. The kernel therefore works as a compact integrity test. If one part is missing, the whole strategy quickly starts resembling the failures described earlier in the book.

That is why the kernel is the positive answer to bad strategy. Decorative ambition skipped diagnosis. Broad consensus language avoided real policy choice. Initiative piles looked active without coherence. The kernel closes those escape routes. It demands a named obstacle, a chosen directional response, and a set of reinforcing actions tied to that choice.

The chapter matters because it turns strategy from a mood into a structure. Once the kernel is clear, the next question changes again. If diagnosis, guiding policy, and coherent action define the form of good strategy, where does the power for that strategy come from? That is what sends the book forward into sources of power.
