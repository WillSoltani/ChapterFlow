Chapter 16 argued that strategy becomes more serious when its reasoning is explicit enough to challenge. Chapter 17 asks what kind of mind can actually do that work well. The answer is that good strategy depends not only on concepts or rigor, but also on disciplined thinking habits.

That shift matters because strategic failure often begins in the mind before it appears in the plan. A familiar analogy feels persuasive. An easy story feels complete. A hopeful interpretation feels more attractive than a hard diagnosis. Chapter 17 closes the book by warning that strategy weakens when the strategist lets those mental shortcuts do the thinking.

Seen this way, using your head means more than being intelligent in the abstract. It means staying active in judgment. The strategist has to keep asking whether the current story actually fits the problem, whether a comparison is clarifying or distorting, and whether confidence is outrunning evidence. Good strategy needs that mental discipline because diagnosis can be corrupted long before action begins.

This is why skepticism matters. Skepticism here is not cynicism or paralysis. It is the refusal to accept the first comfortable interpretation as if it were already insight. The strategist has to notice when a neat analogy is standing in for real diagnosis, or when a desirable outcome is quietly shaping the reading of the facts.

Analogy is especially risky because it feels like understanding. A strategist may see one situation and quickly map it onto another. Sometimes that helps. But Chapter 17 suggests that careless analogy can smuggle in the wrong diagnosis by making a current problem look more familiar than it really is. The mind relaxes too early, and the strategy inherits that mistake.

Wishful interpretation creates a similar danger. People do not only misread problems because they are careless. They also misread them because some readings are emotionally easier to accept than others. A strategist can prefer the version of reality that preserves momentum, avoids embarrassment, or supports a favored policy. The chapter pushes against that habit by tying good strategy to the discipline of seeing what is actually there.

This does not mean disciplined thinking eliminates uncertainty. It means uncertainty has to be handled by better judgment instead of by autopilot. The strategist still has to decide without perfect knowledge. But better thinking habits make those decisions less vulnerable to easy story, mental shortcut, and flattering analogy.

That is how the chapter completes Chapter 16. Strategic science required diagnosis, causal judgment, and consequences that could challenge the reasoning. Chapter 17 explains that none of those standards can hold for long if the strategist's own thinking is lazy, eager, or unexamined. Rigor depends on the quality of mind applying it.

The book closes, then, not with a trick but with a responsibility. Good strategy is partly a matter of method, but it is also a matter of how a person thinks when reality is unclear and pressure is real. The strategist has to keep attention active, question the easy story, and resist the comfort of familiar but misleading interpretations. That is what it means to use your head.
