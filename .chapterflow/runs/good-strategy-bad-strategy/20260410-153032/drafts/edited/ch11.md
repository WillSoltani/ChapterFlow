Once a strategy has been designed as a coherent whole, another question becomes decisive. Where will effort actually be concentrated so the whole can matter? Chapter 11 answers by turning to focus, treating strategy as selective intensity rather than equal support for everything that seems worthwhile.

The chapter matters because a coherent design can still become weak if effort is spread too broadly. A plan may fit together well and still underperform if every priority receives enough attention to feel included but not enough force to matter under real constraints.

This is why the chapter pushes concentration over diffusion. Focus does not mean caring about only one thing in a simplistic way. It means deciding where additional effort will create the strongest effect and where thinner support will only dilute force.

Constraints and competition make this necessary. Resources are limited, attention is limited, and obstacles do not wait politely while an organization tries to progress everywhere at once. Spreading effort across too many fronts can leave the whole strategy too weak on each one.

The positive standard is selective intensity. A strategist chooses the few places where the designed whole should hit hardest, then gives those places enough emphasis to matter. Focus turns coherence into force.

The chapter also warns against equal support for every worthy priority. Fair distribution may feel responsible, but it can thin the strategy until none of its aims receives enough strength. Some priorities have to carry heavier weight than others.

The practical lesson is that strategy gains power when effort is concentrated where it can create the biggest effect under real limits. Once that logic is clear, later chapters can ask what growth or expansion should mean without dissolving that force into broad but weaker coverage.
