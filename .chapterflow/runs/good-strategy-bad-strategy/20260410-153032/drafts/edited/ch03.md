Bad strategy rarely presents itself as failure. It usually arrives in a serious tone. The plan has goals, values, initiatives, targets, and urgency. What it often lacks is a governing obstacle. The language sounds strategic because it points at importance, but nothing in it tells you what problem actually controls the situation. A plan can feel substantial and still have no center.

That is why weak execution and bad strategy are different problems. Weak execution begins with a real strategy and then fails in delivery. Bad strategy fails earlier. It never faces the difficulty clearly enough to earn the name. Chapter 1 gave the positive test: coherent action tied to a real obstacle. Chapter 3 flips that test around. If the obstacle stays unnamed, if the actions do not fit together, and if no hard choice has been made, the plan is strategy-shaped avoidance.

One reason this survives is that broad ambition is politically easier than diagnosis. Diagnosis threatens projects, status, and optimism. It tells people that not everything can stay protected. Initiative piles avoid that pain. A leadership group can promise growth, innovation, capability, trust, and speed all at once because each word pleases someone and excludes almost nobody. The result sounds energetic. It still has no logic.

That is why bad strategy often looks like accumulation instead of laziness. The team is busy. It is just busy in too many directions. One project answers one concern, another survives because nobody wants the fight of cutting it, and the plan slowly becomes a storage unit for unresolved tradeoffs. The document grows while the center disappears. Good strategy gains force by refusing attractive alternatives. Bad strategy keeps them all.

Chapter 2 adds another signal. Good strategy looks for hidden leverage in the situation. Bad strategy often never gets that far. It stays with visible aspiration and asks only how to work harder inside the existing contest. It misses the asymmetry that could change the terms altogether. That blindness matters because a plan can fail strategically even while effort remains high.

The chapter leaves the reader with a harder test than style or confidence. Ask what obstacle governs the answer. Ask what leverage the plan intends to use. Ask what attractive action has been cut so the rest can reinforce one another. If those questions produce only mood, motion, and desirable outcomes, you are not looking at unfinished strategy. You are looking at decoration.

That implication is uncomfortable for a reason. Bad strategy is often easier to defend than good strategy because it protects ambiguity. A real diagnosis kills projects, rejects favorite language, and exposes wasted effort. Decorative ambition spreads the discomfort so widely that nobody has to face the truth directly. That is why the next question matters. If the symptoms are so visible, why do institutions keep producing them?
