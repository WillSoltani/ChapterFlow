After a source of power is found, the next question is how to use it. Chapter 6 located the force-generating condition in a situation. Chapter 7 turns to leverage, which is the disciplined concentration of force where that condition will change the outcome most.

A discovered edge does not help much if effort keeps getting spread across too many fronts. A team can know where the multiplier sits and still dilute its own strength by trying to protect every project, answer every pressure, or advance on every line at once. Leverage starts when the strategist chooses where force should actually land.

That is why leverage is not just another name for hard work. More effort can still be weak if it is diffused. Leverage means that the same amount of force produces more effect because it is aimed at the point where the situation bends. The chapter's contrast is between broad activity and concentrated effect.

This sharpens the idea of a pivot point. Not every move deserves equal force. Some actions matter because they unlock or reshape the rest. Others absorb energy without changing much. The strategist therefore has to ask not only where power exists, but where action against that power will carry the greatest return.

The chapter also pushes against the comfort of even distribution. Broad effort can look fair, energetic, and safe because everyone keeps something. But when force is spread too widely, each move lands below the level needed to matter. What looks like balance is often weakness disguised as coverage.

That is why leverage depends on disciplined exclusion. To strike harder at the pivot point, a leader has to accept that other fronts will receive less attention for a time. This can feel risky, but the alternative is a plan full of motion that never changes the contest enough to justify the cost.

The practical lesson is simple to say and hard to do. Find the point where concentrated action changes the odds most, then put real force there instead of admiring a broad front. Once that logic is clear, the next chapter can turn to proximate objectives as a way to focus action without pretending the whole future path is already known.
