Bad strategy matters partly because it keeps being produced. The failure is not only that some plans are empty. Institutions can generate the same emptiness year after year while calling it unity, momentum, or alignment. That is the chapter's opening pressure. Bad strategy is not just an occasional mistake. It can become the normal output of a group that has learned how to sound serious without forcing the choices that would make seriousness real.

That recurrence makes sense once diagnosis is treated as a social act rather than only an analytical one. A real diagnosis does not merely name a problem. It ranks problems. It tells people which bottleneck matters most and which projects are now secondary. That is why clarity can threaten consensus. The moment a governing obstacle is named, some existing work loses status and some broad story about shared progress becomes harder to keep.

Exclusion is where the cost becomes visible. Good strategy gains force by refusing attractive alternatives, but refusal is rarely popular inside an institution. A project has an owner. A slogan has admirers. A program has people whose reputation is tied to its survival. The resistance is not always irrational. People are often protecting morale, turf, or the hope that everything important can still remain included. Those protections are understandable. They still weaken strategy.

That is why broad ambition is so easy to reward. A plan that promises growth, innovation, trust, capability, and speed gives something to almost everyone. It sounds positive and lowers the immediate temperature in the room. It also delays the harder moment when somebody must say which problem governs the rest. Consensus language can therefore create a false appearance of alignment. The room feels calm because no real exclusion has yet been tested.

Chapter 1 already supplied the positive contrast. Good strategy looks like coherent action tied to a real obstacle. Chapter 3 supplied the negative version: decorative ambition, initiative piles, and leverage blindness. Chapter 4 adds the production logic underneath both. Strategy-free activity persists because institutions can reward motion and optimism more quickly than they reward painful clarity. The credit for energy arrives now. The strategic bill for vagueness arrives later.

That makes the chapter's test sharper. When a room appears unusually united, ask whether the unity comes from real strategic agreement or from the fact that nobody has yet forced a hard choice. Real agreement often becomes possible only after conflict has passed through the room and the group has accepted what will not be protected. Before that, harmony may simply be ambiguity doing its work.

The chapter therefore avoids a cheap explanation. Bad strategy does not persist only because leaders are foolish. It persists because some of the forces producing it are recognizably human and institutional. People want cohesion. Leaders want momentum. Groups want plans that do not split the room immediately. The strategic cost is that comforting language can become a substitute for diagnosis. That is why the next chapter must turn to the kernel of good strategy.
