After the kernel is clear, the next question is force. Diagnosis, guiding policy, and coherent action tell a leader how strategy is formed, but they do not yet explain why one strategy bites and another stays tidy but weak. Chapter 6 asks where strategic power comes from.

It does not come from effort alone. Teams can work hard and still fight on terms that favor the obstacle. Power comes from conditions that make some moves more potent than others. It can sit in an asymmetry, a position, a fit, a coordination pattern, or another feature of the situation that changes what one action is worth. Form by itself does not create force.

That is why not every apparent advantage is a true source of power. Some wins are only tricks that work once. Some tactics look strong only because nobody has answered them yet. Some leaders copy the visible surface of success without possessing the underlying condition that made it powerful. The chapter pushes the reader to ask what makes an advantage durable rather than merely dramatic.

This sharpens an idea already introduced in Chapter 2. Discovering power means finding the hidden leverage in a situation instead of pushing harder inside the obvious contest. Chapter 6 adds one step of precision: leverage comes after power is found. Before force can be applied well, the strategist has to know where force will multiply.

That creates a harder test for the kernel. A strategy can have diagnosis, policy, and action and still be weaker than it looks if it has not located a real source of power. The structure may be orderly and the actions may reinforce one another. Without a condition that changes the odds, though, coherence does not become strength.

Surface imitation is therefore dangerous. A rival may copy the visible practice, target, or sequence of moves and still fail because the real power sat underneath in a less obvious advantage. Strategic strength usually is not hiding in the loudest part of a plan. It is hiding in the condition that lets ordinary-looking moves land with unusual effect.

The practical lesson is to ask a different question before adding effort. What in this situation makes one line of action stronger than another? If the answer is only intensity, optimism, or volume, the strategy may still be missing its power source. Once that source is visible, the next chapter can turn to using leverage.
