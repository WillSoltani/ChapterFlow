Once a strategist sees that a system is constrained by structure, another question takes over. How should the parts be arranged so the whole actually works? Chapter 10 answers by turning to design, treating strategy not as isolated moves but as the deliberate construction of a mutually reinforcing whole.

The chapter matters because a list of individually attractive actions is not yet a strategy. Separate moves can each look sensible and still interfere with one another, dilute one another, or pull the organization in conflicting directions. Design asks whether choices fit together strongly enough that the whole becomes more powerful than the pieces viewed one by one.

This is why the chapter presses beyond isolated strength. One initiative may be impressive, one policy may be clever, and one investment may be promising. But if they do not reinforce one another, they remain disconnected efforts rather than a designed strategy.

The positive standard is mutual reinforcement. In a designed whole, one choice supports the next, one action increases the value of another, and structure gives force to intention. The strategist is not merely collecting good ideas. The strategist is arranging them so they work together.

This is also where fit becomes decisive. Choices must fit the real obstacle, the operating constraints, and each other. If they do not, activity expands while coherence weakens. A design can therefore fail not because each part is poor, but because the parts do not align into a working whole.

The chapter also implies exclusion. If strategy is a designed whole, then not every appealing move belongs inside it. Some options have to be refused because they distract effort, introduce conflict, or weaken reinforcement among the parts that matter most.

The practical lesson is that strategy gains force when it is designed as a coherent whole rather than assembled as a pile of attractive fragments. Once that logic is clear, the next step is to ask where effort should be concentrated so the designed whole does not dissolve into too many priorities at once.
