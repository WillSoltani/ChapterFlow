Most people remember David and Goliath as a story about courage. Rumelt uses it as a strategy story. The point is not that the weaker side believed harder. The point is that the fight changed once David refused the terms Goliath wanted. A close clash favored size, armor, and brute force. Distance and mobility changed the contest. What looked hopeless under one description became winnable under another. The chapter's first lesson is that power often hides inside the way a situation is framed.

That is a tougher idea than it sounds. Struggling organizations usually ask how to get more power in the obvious sense: more budget, more people, more speed, more scale. Rumelt asks a different question. Where is the effective leverage already sitting in the situation? Sometimes advantage does not come from having more of everything. Sometimes it comes from finding the asymmetry that makes one kind of move count far more than another.

Wal-Mart gives the chapter a business version of that logic. Its advantage did not mainly live in one excellent store. The power sat in the network design underneath the stores. Distribution, coordination, and system management reinforced local performance from below. That matters because rivals could copy visible store practices and still miss the source of force. They imitated the surface and left the engine intact.

That is why copying best practices so often disappoints. People copy the product feature, the meeting format, the slogan, or the staffing pattern they can see first. But if the advantage came from how those parts fit together in a reinforcing system, imitation stays shallow. It looks similar at the edge and weaker at the center. Discovering power means finding the architecture that actually carries the result.

Marshall and Roche push the idea further. Their Cold War strategy did not depend on matching Soviet strength weapon for weapon. It relied on using American technological strengths to impose higher costs on Soviet responses. That is a different style of competition. Instead of asking how to win the same race faster, it asks how to shape the race so the other side pays more to stay in it.

This is why strategy and force are not the same thing. A larger rival can still be strategically exposed if the contest can be shifted toward mobility, position, system design, or asymmetric cost. A smaller player can waste its chance by agreeing to fight on the strongest player's favorite terms. Rumelt is teaching the reader to look for where the situation bends.

There is also a real limit inside this lesson. Not every clever angle is durable power. Some moves are only tricks that work once. A true source of power changes the contest in a way that can be reinforced. David's advantage mattered because it changed the kind of fight. Wal-Mart's advantage mattered because the network kept strengthening the stores. Marshall and Roche's logic mattered because it tied one side's strengths to the other side's weaknesses. Cleverness without that deeper fit creates surprise without staying power.

That is why this chapter belongs immediately after the opening distinction between good and bad strategy. Chapter 1 said strategy must answer a real obstacle. Chapter 2 adds that the answer gets stronger when it discovers the hidden leverage in the situation first. Without that step, leaders drift toward imitation, head-on matching, or bold gestures that never touch the real source of power. Once leverage becomes visible, bad strategy becomes easier to see too, because one mark of bad strategy is that it never finds the force already hiding in the field.
