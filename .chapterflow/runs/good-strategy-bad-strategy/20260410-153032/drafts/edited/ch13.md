Selective growth solves only part of the strategic problem. Once a strategy chooses the right kind of expansion, another question appears. How will the situation change over time, and what openings or traps will that movement create? Chapter 13 turns to dynamics by asking the reader to stop thinking only in still pictures.

Still pictures mislead because strategy does not live in frozen positions. People react. Conditions shift. Small moves change what later moves become possible. What looks stable at one moment may become vulnerable in the next because the sequence has already started changing the field.

That is why the chapter treats dynamics as more than speed. Moving quickly is not the same as moving strategically. Dynamics means understanding sequence, timing, reaction, and the way one action changes the menu of later possibilities.

Timing matters because it can create openings that static analysis misses. A move that looks modest now may become powerful because it arrives before resistance gathers, before alternatives close, or before the other side understands what is changing. The opening comes not only from the move itself but from when it enters the sequence.

The reverse is just as important. Motion can create traps. A strategy can gain momentum in a direction that looks promising early, then discover that the next steps become more expensive, more constrained, or more exposed because the sequence was misread. The trouble is not mere activity. The trouble is a future path that gets worse as it unfolds.

Dynamics therefore has to include reaction. The strategist is not moving through empty space. Other actors, internal systems, and outside pressures answer back. Each important move changes what others notice, how they adjust, and which options become easier or harder afterward.

The practical lesson is to ask not only whether a move improves the current position, but also what path it sets in motion. Does it open room, sharpen timing, and make later steps stronger? Or does it create burden, predictability, and a trap that becomes obvious only after the sequence is already underway?

Chapter 13 extends Chapter 12 in a precise way. Growth had to be chosen selectively because larger scale could either strengthen the strategy or dilute it. Dynamics now asks how chosen motion changes the later field, and why strategy has to reason about time if it wants to avoid building its own problems.

Seen this way, the strategist has to think in trajectories rather than snapshots. A sound move is not judged only by what it wins today. It is also judged by the next position it creates, the reactions it invites, and the openings or closures it generates over time.

That perspective prepares the next chapter well. Once movement over time is visible, it becomes easier to see why systems resist change, accumulate drag, and decay in patterned ways. Dynamics explains why sequence shapes the future. Inertia and entropy will explain why some of those sequences become hard to reverse.
