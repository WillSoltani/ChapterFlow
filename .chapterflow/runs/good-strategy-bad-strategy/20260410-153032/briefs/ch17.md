Core Claim:
After Chapter 16 defines strategic rigor as disciplined inquiry, Chapter 17 should explain that good strategy also depends on disciplined thinking habits. The strategist has to notice weak analogies, question easy stories, hold competing possibilities in mind, and keep judgment active under pressure.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
This chapter is distinct because it turns from the logic of strategy to the mind that uses that logic. It is the book's final move from concepts and rigor into judgment habits.

Author Logic Chain:
First the author shifts from strategic science to strategist thinking habits.
Then he frames good strategy as depending on attention, skepticism, and disciplined judgment.
Then he contrasts clear thinking with lazy analogy, wishful interpretation, and mental shortcuts.
Then he shows that better thinking habits improve diagnosis and policy choice.
Then he closes the book by tying strategic success to the quality of mind brought to real problems.

Frameworks or Terms Introduced:
Thinking discipline: habits of attention and judgment that keep strategy from collapsing into lazy story-making.
Mental shortcut risk: the tendency to overuse analogy, optimism, or familiar narratives when diagnosis should stay active.
Judgment under pressure: the ability to keep thinking clearly when uncertainty and urgency tempt simplification.

Specific Applications:
- challenging an analogy before treating it as diagnosis
- slowing down a confident interpretation to test whether it fits the real problem
- improving policy choice by sharpening the quality of thought behind it
