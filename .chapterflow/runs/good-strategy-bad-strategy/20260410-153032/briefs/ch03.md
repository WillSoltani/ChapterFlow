Core Claim:
Bad strategy is not simply weak execution. It is strategy-shaped language that never faces the governing problem, never chooses a coherent response, and often mistakes ambition, slogans, or initiative piles for real strategy. On the support available in this run, the chapter should stay focused on visible failure patterns: vague aspiration, activity without a center, refusal to make tradeoffs, and blindness to leverage already present in the situation.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
This chapter is distinct because it names the impostor. Chapter 1 separated coherent action from noise, and Chapter 2 showed that leverage gives strategy force. Chapter 3 turns those standards around and asks what failure looks like when an organization talks like a strategist but never diagnoses, never chooses, and never exploits power.

Author Logic Chain:
First the author returns to the opening distinction and sharpens it into recognizable failure patterns.
Then he shows that bad strategy often sounds energetic because goals and initiatives are easier to announce than hard choices.
Then he connects the failure to blindness: leaders either never face the obstacle clearly or never discover the leverage that would make action matter.
Then he leaves the reader with a practical test for spotting plans that are decorative rather than strategic.

Required Anchors:
- strategy-shaped language that names ambition but not the governing obstacle
- initiative piles or activity lists that do not reinforce one another
- refusal to choose or simplify even after the bottleneck becomes visible
- failure to notice hidden leverage or asymmetry already available in the situation

Frameworks or Terms Introduced:
Bad strategy: strategy-shaped language without diagnosis, leverage, or coherent action.
Decorative ambition: goals or vision language standing in for a governing response.
Initiative pile: actions collected side by side without shared logic.
Blindness to leverage: failure to see or use the asymmetry that could change the contest.

Specific Applications:
- diagnosing whether a school, team, or manager has a strategy or only a slogan
- testing whether a plan rejects attractive alternatives or simply accumulates them
- checking whether a group has named the obstacle and the leverage point together

Common Misreadings:
- thinking bad strategy means low effort
- treating any long plan as proof of strategic depth
- assuming energy and optimism compensate for missing diagnosis
