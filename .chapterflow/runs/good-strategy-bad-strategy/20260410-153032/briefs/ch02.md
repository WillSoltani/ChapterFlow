Core Claim:
Good strategy is not only about naming the obstacle. It is also about finding where hidden power sits in the situation. Rumelt uses David and Goliath, Wal-Mart's network logic, and Marshall and Roche's asymmetric-cost thinking against the Soviet Union to show that strong strategy comes from seeing a favorable asymmetry that others overlook. The chapter's practical lesson is that raw effort matters less than discovering the leverage point that changes the contest.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
This chapter is distinct because it is about hidden power, not simply focus. The reader should come away with the sense that strategy strengthens when a leader sees an asymmetry, system design, or cost dynamic that other people treat as background.

Author Logic Chain:
First the author reframes strategy as the discovery of hidden power in a situation.
Then he shows with David and Goliath that the contest changes when the fight is redefined around mobility and range instead of brute strength.
Then he uses Wal-Mart to show that advantage can sit in the network design, not in one store.
Then he uses Marshall and Roche to show that competition can be won by imposing asymmetric costs rather than matching strength head-on.

- David and Goliath as a strategy story about changing the terms of the contest.
- Wal-Mart's advantage as a network/distribution design rather than a single-store best practice.
- Marshall and Roche's Cold War strategy of exploiting U.S. technological strengths to impose higher costs on the Soviet Union.

Asymmetric competition: avoiding direct strength-against-strength matching.
System advantage: power created by network design and coordination, not isolated excellence.

Specific Applications:

- reframing a contest away from the opponent's strongest terms
- using design or cost asymmetry instead of head-on matching
