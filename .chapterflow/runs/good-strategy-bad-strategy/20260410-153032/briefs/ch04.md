Core Claim:
Bad strategy persists not only because people make intellectual mistakes, but because institutions often reward ambiguity, optimism, and inclusion more than exclusion, diagnosis, and painful choice. On the support available in this run, the chapter should stay with organizational and cognitive causes at a pattern level: avoidance of conflict, preference for slogans over diagnosis, and the social comfort of initiative piles.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
This chapter is distinct because it moves from symptoms to causes. Chapter 3 identified what bad strategy looks like. Chapter 4 asks why that failure pattern keeps surviving in rooms full of smart and experienced people.

Author Logic Chain:
First the author treats bad strategy as a recurring production problem rather than a rare mistake.
Then he shows that clarity is socially expensive because it forces exclusion, bad news, and visible tradeoffs.
Then he argues that organizations often protect morale, turf, and ambiguity instead of rewarding diagnosis.
Then he leaves the reader with a sharper suspicion toward institutions that sound united only because they have not chosen.

Required Anchors:
- bad strategy is repeatedly produced, not merely stumbled into
- ambiguity and broad ambition are often socially safer than diagnosis
- exclusion and hard choice create political resistance
- institutions can reward activity, consensus language, and optimism over strategic clarity
