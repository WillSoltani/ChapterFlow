Core Claim:
Good strategy is not a slogan, a revenue target, or a bundle of initiatives. It is a focused response to a real obstacle. Rumelt opens by showing that Steve Jobs's Apple turnaround worked because it diagnosed the real problem and simplified the company around a coordinated answer. He contrasts that with executives who could describe other firms' strategies but answered questions about their own plans with activity lists and management fashion. The chapter's practical distinction is between coherent action tied to a central problem and busy-looking motion with no governing logic.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
This chapter is impossible to confuse with later chapters because it opens the book's core contrast in the clearest possible form: focused response versus strategy theater. Apple is not one example among many here. It is the load-bearing proof that real strategy can look plain, even obvious, once someone finally faces the actual obstacle.

Author Logic Chain:
First the author shows Apple's near-collapse and the simplification moves Jobs made after returning.
Then the author explains why those moves worked: they directly attacked the company's real problem with coordinated choices rather than ambition language.
Then the author contrasts that coherence with executives who answered a strategy question by listing generic programs and initiatives.
Then the author leaves the reader with a sharper definition of strategy as a coherent response to an important challenge.

Required Anchors:
- Apple in 1997: near-bankruptcy pressure, oversized product line, channel complexity, and Jobs's cut-back-to-core response.
- Jobs's coordinating moves: fewer models, fewer retailers, supply-chain simplification, and Microsoft investment as stabilizer.
- The worldwide electronics interviews: executives describe competitors' success clearly but answer about their own strategy with activity lists and fashionable programs.

Frameworks or Terms Introduced:
Good strategy: a specific and coherent response to an important obstacle.
Bad strategy: goals, slogans, or initiatives that create motion without a governing logic.
Coherent action: coordinated moves that fit the diagnosis of the real problem.

Specific Applications:
- Distinguishing a real strategy from a list of goals.
- Evaluating whether a turnaround plan addresses the actual bottleneck.
- Checking whether a manager's plan is coordinated or merely full of activity.

Common Misreadings:
- thinking strategy means ambition plus energy
- treating any detailed plan as strategy
- mistaking visible busyness for coherent action
