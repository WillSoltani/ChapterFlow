Many teams think they have answered how to win when they have only described how hard they intend to compete. They say they will be better, faster, more innovative, more customer-focused, and more disciplined than the alternatives. The language sounds strong because it piles up virtues. The problem is that it still does not explain why customers should prefer the offer on the chosen field. Effort is not a how-to-win choice. It is only useful after the company has decided what kind of advantage it is actually trying to create.

That is why how to win comes right after where to play. Once the field is chosen, the company has to define the value equation that will beat the alternatives there. What will make this offer more attractive to the customer than the other available options? That answer might involve better performance, lower delivered cost, a more compelling combination of benefits, or a route that changes the basis of comparison altogether. The chapter's pressure point is that many teams want to win without naming the actual reason they should win.

Glad ForceFlex gives the chapter its clearest anchor. The useful lesson is not a generic one about product improvement. It is that the business found a more compelling value logic rather than just promising to compete harder in the same old way. The case matters because it helps the reader see how-to-win as a strategic choice about the offer itself. A chosen field still leaves many ways to lose. The company has to identify what customers will value enough to prefer and what rivals will have difficulty neutralizing quickly.

That is also why head-to-head competition is not the only route. Leaders often assume that winning means taking the rival on directly, feature for feature or claim for claim. Sometimes that is necessary. Often it is lazy. If the rival is strongest on those terms, then matching the comparison can trap the company inside someone else's advantage. The chapter pushes the reader to consider alternate routes to win: a different basis of value, a better fit to the chosen customer, or a combination of benefits that changes what matters in the comparison.

Still, the chapter is not a license for empty cleverness. A new route only counts if it creates a believable customer preference on the field the company chose. Novelty alone is not enough. Branding language alone is not enough. The how-to-win choice has to make the offer genuinely more compelling to the people the strategy is trying to serve. Otherwise the company has only renamed its effort instead of improving its position.

That limit matters because strategy language is full of fake edges. Teams say they will deliver a superior experience, a stronger brand, or a better platform, and sometimes those phrases are just elegant ways to avoid precision. The chapter's correction is sharper: pick the reason the offer should win, then make sure the rest of the strategy can support that reason. Once that edge is named, the next question becomes unavoidable. What capabilities would the organization need to make that how-to-win choice real instead of merely well phrased?
