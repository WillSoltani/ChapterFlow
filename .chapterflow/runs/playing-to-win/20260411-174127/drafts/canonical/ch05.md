Teams often say they are playing to their strengths when they really mean they are admiring what they already know how to do. That sounds prudent, but it can still weaken the strategy. A strength matters strategically only if it supports the chosen field and the chosen edge. If it does not, then the company may be protecting an impressive capability that pulls effort away from the very advantage it claims to want.

This is why capabilities come after how to win in the cascade. Once a company has chosen its field and its edge, it has to ask what it must be distinctly good at to deliver that edge consistently. Capabilities are not a general inventory of talents. They are the critical things the organization must do well for the strategy to hold. The chapter's pressure point is that many leaders want to keep favorite strengths even when those strengths no longer fit the game they say they are playing.

Gillette gives the chapter its clearest anchor. The useful lesson is not about acquisition drama. It is about capability fit. The case matters because it helps the reader see that a strategic edge depends on what the organization can repeatedly do, not just on what it hopes the market will notice. A chosen way to win without matching capabilities is still only a promise.

The chapter also refuses to treat capabilities as isolated skills. A single strong function rarely rescues a weak system. What matters is an activity system: a set of reinforcing actions and abilities that strengthen one another in service of the edge. When the system fits, the strategy starts to feel coherent under pressure. When it does not, one strong node ends up compensating for several weak ones and the organization mistakes strain for strategy.

That is why the chapter can be misread if it turns into generic strengths rhetoric. Existing strengths do not deserve loyalty just because they were expensive to build or once made the company proud. If they do not support the chosen field and edge, then they become drag. The useful question is not 'what are we good at?' in the abstract. It is 'what do we need to be good at for this strategy to work?'

The limit matters because a company can become very capable in the wrong direction. It can build admired skills, disciplined teams, and sophisticated processes that reinforce one another beautifully, and still be supporting a weak edge or the wrong field. The chapter's correction is precise: capabilities only count strategically when they fit the choice cascade. Once that fit is named, the next pressure arrives naturally. What systems will keep those capabilities reinforced instead of letting them decay back into isolated effort?
