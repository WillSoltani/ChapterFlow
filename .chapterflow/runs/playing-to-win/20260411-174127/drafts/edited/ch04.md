Many teams think they have answered how to win when they have only described how hard they intend to compete. They say they will be better, faster, more innovative, more customer-focused, and more disciplined than the alternatives. The language sounds strong because it piles up virtues. The problem is that it still does not explain why customers should prefer the offer on the chosen field. Effort is not a how-to-win choice. It only matters after the company decides what kind of advantage it is actually trying to create.

How to win follows where to play because a chosen field still does not tell the company why it should prevail there. The missing answer is the value equation. What will make this offer more attractive than the alternatives for the customer the strategy chose to serve? The answer may involve stronger performance, lower delivered cost, a more useful mix of benefits, or a route that changes the basis of comparison altogether. The chapter keeps pressing on one point: a team has not chosen how to win until it can name the reason the offer should be preferred.

Glad ForceFlex gives the chapter its clearest anchor. The useful lesson is not generic product improvement. It is that the business found a more compelling offer logic instead of promising to compete harder in the same old comparison. The case matters because it turns how-to-win into a practical strategic choice. A chosen field still leaves many ways to lose. The company has to identify what customers will value enough to prefer and what rivals will not neutralize easily.

That is also why head-to-head competition is only one possible route. Leaders often assume that winning means taking the rival on directly, feature for feature or claim for claim. Sometimes that is necessary. Often it is lazy. If the rival is strongest on those terms, then matching the comparison can trap the company inside someone else's advantage. The chapter pushes the reader to consider alternate routes to win: a different basis of value, a tighter fit to the chosen customer, or a combination of benefits that changes what matters in the comparison.

Still, the chapter is not giving permission for empty cleverness. A new route only counts if it creates a believable customer preference on the chosen field. Novelty alone is not enough. Branding language alone is not enough. The how-to-win choice has to make the offer genuinely more compelling to the people the strategy is trying to serve. Otherwise the company has only renamed its effort instead of improving its position.

That limit matters because strategy language is full of fake edges. Teams say they will deliver a superior experience, a stronger brand, or a better platform, and sometimes those phrases are elegant ways to avoid precision. The chapter's correction is stricter: pick the reason the offer should win, then make sure the rest of the strategy can support that reason. Once that edge is named, the next pressure arrives naturally. What capabilities would the organization need to make that how-to-win choice real instead of merely well phrased?
