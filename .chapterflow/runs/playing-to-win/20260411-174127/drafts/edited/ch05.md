Teams often say they are playing to their strengths when they really mean they are admiring what they already know how to do. That sounds prudent, but it can still weaken the strategy. A strength matters strategically only if it supports the chosen field and the chosen edge. If it does not, then the company may be protecting an impressive capability that pulls effort away from the very advantage it claims to want.

Capabilities follow how to win because a chosen edge is still only a promise until the organization can deliver it. The next question is not what the company happens to be proud of. It is what the company must be distinctly good at for the strategy to hold. The chapter keeps pressing that distinction. Capabilities are not a general inventory of talents. They are the critical things the organization must do well for its field and edge to stay real.

Gillette gives the chapter its clearest anchor. The useful lesson is not acquisition drama. It is capability fit. The case matters because it shows that a strategic edge depends on what the organization can repeatedly do, not just on what it hopes the market will notice. A chosen way to win without matching capabilities remains only a promise with better wording.

The chapter also refuses to treat capabilities as isolated skills. One strong function rarely rescues a weak system. What matters is an activity system: a set of reinforcing actions and abilities that strengthen one another in service of the edge. When the system fits, the strategy starts to feel coherent under pressure. When it does not, one strong node ends up compensating for several weak ones and the organization mistakes strain for strategy.

This is why the chapter can go wrong if it turns into generic strengths rhetoric. Existing strengths do not deserve loyalty just because they were expensive to build or once made the company proud. If they do not support the chosen field and edge, then they become drag. The useful question is not what the company is good at in the abstract. It is what the company needs to be good at for this strategy to work.

The limit matters because a company can become very capable in the wrong direction. It can build admired skills, disciplined teams, and reinforcing processes that all work together, yet still be supporting a weak edge or the wrong field. The chapter's correction is narrower than motivational advice: capabilities only count strategically when they fit the choice cascade. Once that fit is clear, the next pressure follows naturally. What systems will keep those capabilities reinforced instead of letting them decay back into isolated effort?
