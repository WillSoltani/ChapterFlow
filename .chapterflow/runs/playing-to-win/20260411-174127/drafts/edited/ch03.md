Most teams say they want growth in a way that leaves the field completely blurred. They want more customers, more channels, more relevance, and more reach. The language sounds ambitious, but it usually avoids the most important question: where exactly are we going to play? Until that question gets answered, the strategy keeps pretending that every customer, channel, geography, and category deserves equal protection. That is not breadth as strength. It is breadth as refusal to choose.

That is why where to play comes immediately after winning aspiration. Once a company decides it intends to win, it has to decide where that intention will actually live. The field is not a backdrop. It shapes everything that comes after it. A broad field makes the later strategy mushy because the company is trying to satisfy too many logics at once. A chosen field sharpens the later decisions because the business no longer has to serve every possible customer or route with equal seriousness. Where to play is the move that turns aspiration into a real arena.

Bounty gives the chapter its clearest proof case. The business had spread attention into places where the structure was less attractive and the chance to build a strong position was weaker. The better move was not more generalized effort. It was a stronger field choice. Geography mattered. Customer segments mattered. The company had to decide where a winning position was actually possible and then organize around that decision. The case matters because it shows focus as a field judgment, not as a motivational slogan.

That judgment is more dimensional than leaders often admit. Where to play can mean geography, but it can also mean customer segment, channel, product category, price tier, and sometimes even which stage of the value chain the company wants to occupy. That is why the chapter refuses to reduce the question to market size or country choice. A business may be in the right geography and still have the wrong segment. It may have the right customers and the wrong channel. It may define the category too narrowly or too lazily. Field choice is strategic because it sets the shape of the game.

The chapter also keeps exposing the traps that make field choice easier to postpone. One trap is trying to serve everyone, which sounds customer-friendly but usually leaves the offer too diluted to win anywhere strongly. Another is trying to buy your way into a better game rather than choosing and building one. A third is treating the current field as fixed, as though the business inherited its map from the past and must remain there forever. These traps share one habit: they protect leaders from the discomfort of real field choice.

Still, narrower is not automatically smarter. That is the limit the chapter has to keep alive. A company can narrow itself into a field where it still has no believable route to win. In that case the focus is disciplined, but the discipline is pointed at the wrong target. The chapter works best when it holds both ideas at once: breadth often hides weakness, and narrowing only helps when the chosen field can support a real advantage later. Once that field is chosen, the next move becomes unavoidable: decide how to beat the alternatives on that ground.
