Core Claim:
How to win is the choice about what kind of advantage or value equation will beat the alternatives on the chosen field. The chapter argues that a company cannot win by promising to compete harder in general. It has to define why customers should prefer its offer, and it may need to avoid fighting head-to-head on the rival's easiest terms. The Glad ForceFlex case gives the chapter its anchor.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
This is not a generic competition chapter. Its distinct mechanism is that winning depends on a defensible value equation on a chosen field, not on effort alone. The chapter specifically distinguishes between taking a rival head-on and finding an alternate route to winning value.

Author Logic Chain:
First the authors show that where-to-play choice is incomplete until the company decides how it will beat the alternatives there.
Then they use Glad ForceFlex to show how a better offer logic can change the game.
Then they explain that head-to-head competition is only one route and may be the weakest one.
Then they press the reader toward a clearer value equation instead of generic superiority language.

Specific Applications:
- checking whether a strategy names a real reason customers should prefer the offer
- spotting when a team is competing on someone else's strongest terms
- testing whether an alternate route to value is clearer than a direct feature war

Common Misreadings:
- how to win just means try harder than competitors
- differentiation language is enough without a real customer value logic
- choosing an alternate route means avoiding competition altogether

Counterarguments or Limits:
The chapter can be misread as if any clever alternate route counts as a win. Hard depth must preserve that the route still has to matter to customers on the chosen field and has to be credible enough to sustain. The limit is that novelty without a real value equation is still weak strategy.
