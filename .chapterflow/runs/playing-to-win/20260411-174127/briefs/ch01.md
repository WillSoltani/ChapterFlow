Core Claim:
Strategy is not a mission statement, a budget, or a polished plan. It is an integrated set of choices that defines how an organization intends to win. The chapter uses the Olay turnaround to show that winning comes from choosing a clear aspiration, a field to play on, a way to win there, and the capabilities and systems needed to support those choices. Its pressure point is that many leaders think they already have strategy when they only have ambition plus activity.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
This is the book's definitional chapter. Its distinct mechanism is not generic focus or discipline, but the claim that strategy only exists when a set of linked choices creates a specific path to winning. Without that integrated choice logic, leaders are only describing intentions.

Author Logic Chain:
First the authors reject the common habit of treating strategy as vision, planning, or status-quo optimization.
Then they introduce the five-choice cascade as the real shape of strategy.
Then they anchor the argument in the Olay turnaround, where coherent choices created a winning position rather than incremental improvement.
Then they establish that the choices must fit and reinforce one another rather than sit as disconnected answers.

Required Anchors:
- Olay as the central case for turning a tired brand into a stronger strategic position through explicit choices.
- The five strategic choices: winning aspiration, where to play, how to win, capabilities, and management systems.
- The contrast between real strategy and softer substitutes such as mission statements, long-range plans, or do-more-of-the-same optimization.

Frameworks or Terms Introduced:

- winning aspiration: the ambition that defines what winning means rather than merely participating.

Specific Applications:
- testing whether a strategy deck contains real choices or just priorities
- checking whether a team's target market and value proposition actually fit each other
- spotting when an organization says it wants growth without choosing a way to win

Common Misreadings:
- strategy is just long-term planning
- strategy means having bold ambition regardless of the field
- the five choices can be answered independently without reinforcement

Counterarguments or Limits:
The chapter can be misread as if naming the five questions is enough. Hard depth must preserve that a list of choices is still weak if the choices do not fit or if leaders refuse the tradeoffs required to make them real. The limit is that strategic language can mimic strategy without creating an actual path to winning.
