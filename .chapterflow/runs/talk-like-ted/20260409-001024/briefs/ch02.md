Core Claim:
Gallo argues that stories are not presentation garnish. They are one of the main ways an idea crosses the gap between speaker and audience. Narrative works because it makes abstract claims feel human, memorable, and emotionally legible. Bryan Stevenson's TED talk shows that even a presentation full of difficult statistics becomes more persuasive when stories carry much of the weight. Storytelling helps blend ethos, logos, and pathos, and in many of the strongest talks, pathos does more of the persuasive work than conventional business advice admits.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
This chapter is impossible to confuse with Chapter 1 because it is not about inner conviction alone. It is about the delivery vehicle that gives conviction structure. Its distinct mechanism is that story turns information into exchange, connection, and memory.

Author Logic Chain:
First the author uses Bryan Stevenson to show that stories can carry a serious, data-backed argument without weakening it.
Then the author uses Aristotle's ethos, logos, and pathos to explain why Stevenson's talk worked so well.
Then the author argues that too much data without story creates emotional distance.
Then the author categorizes story types into personal stories, stories about others, and stories about brand success.
Then the author adds structural advice: give the audience a hero, tension, and movement toward resolution.
Then the author concludes that if ideas are currency, storytelling is how that currency gets exchanged.

Required Anchors:
- Bryan Stevenson: stories from his own life and family make a data-heavy injustice talk persuasive rather than alienating.
- Aristotle's rhetoric triad: ethos, logos, pathos as the frame for analyzing persuasive balance.
- Gallo's story taxonomy: personal stories, stories about other people, brand-success stories.
