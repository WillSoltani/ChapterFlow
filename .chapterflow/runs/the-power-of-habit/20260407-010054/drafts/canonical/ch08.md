Chapter 8 applies the book's habit framework to social movements. Duhigg argues that collective action depends not only on shared belief but on social habits: repeated norms, relationships, and routines that make coordinated action possible.

The Montgomery bus boycott and Saddleback Church provide the chapter's two major cases. Strong ties matter because they generate the commitment needed to begin. People often accept risk and sacrifice when someone close to them is already involved or directly expects participation. But strong ties alone do not solve the scaling problem.

Weak ties solve that second problem. They connect clusters. They spread norms across wider networks. Without them, a movement can remain emotionally intense but geographically and socially narrow. With them, participation can travel.

The chapter's central habit insight is that communities install scripts. Through meetings, rituals, expectations, and visible forms of belonging, a movement turns action into routine. Participation becomes less a series of isolated acts of courage and more a repeated social behavior that members understand.

This makes the chapter analytically strong. It explains why passion alone is not enough. A movement without repeated participation routines can flare and fade. A movement with strong ties, weak ties, and durable social scripts can endure beyond the first wave of outrage.

The chapter also invites caution. The mechanism is not automatically virtuous. Shared habits and tight networks can sustain admirable mobilization or destructive conformity. Duhigg's contribution is to show how the structure works, not to romanticize every instance of it.

Chapter 8 therefore turns social change into a networked habit problem. The key question is what relationships and routines make action normal enough to sustain. That is the difference between agreement that stays private and commitment that becomes collective behavior. The chapter stays useful only if the mechanism survives contact with routine life. That is why it keeps pointing toward using a small group to make a new behavior feel normal, designing a team ritual that creates visible expectation instead of private intention, and understanding why belonging often moves people faster than abstract agreement. Those applications test whether Saddleback Church as organizational routine for belonging and scale can still be recognized once the reader leaves the headline example behind. They also block sloppy interpretations. Reducing social change to charisma alone is too broad. Ignoring the role of organization after the first spark is also too broad. The actual claim is tighter: The chapter argues that movements need both emotional ignition and durable social structure; either piece alone is too thin.
