Chapter 5 argues that willpower can function as a habit. Duhigg's Starbucks case supports that claim by showing how difficult moments are managed through training rather than pure resolve. Employees rehearse scripts, responses, and expectations for stressful encounters before those encounters happen.

The mechanism is that pressure reveals defaults. Under emotional load, people are more likely to run practiced routines than to invent excellent behavior from scratch. This is why the chapter treats rehearsal as central. A trained first move becomes accessible when attention narrows, while an untrained aspiration often disappears.

This reframes self-control. Instead of imagining willpower as a fixed trait someone simply possesses, the chapter presents it as partly infrastructural. Scripts, repeated scenarios, and expectations build behavioral pathways that make useful action more available under stress. What appears to be calm strength is often the surface effect of earlier repetition.

The chapter also adds realism. Willpower still fluctuates. Fatigue, context, and overload remain relevant. This is exactly why the chapter favors habit structure over pure determination. Raw resolve is too unstable to anchor a serious system. Prepared routines are better because they reduce dependence on momentary strength.

The limit is that routine cannot solve everything. A person can still be overwhelmed. A poor environment can still erode self-control. The chapter does not overpromise. It says that trained defaults improve the odds. That is enough to matter enormously in work, school, and personal life.

The practical shift is therefore clear: if you want better behavior in difficult moments, practice the response before the moment arrives. Do not wait to become spontaneously excellent under strain. Build a default that strain can expose in your favor. The chapter stays useful only if the mechanism survives contact with routine life. That is why it keeps pointing toward writing a script for a difficult conversation before it happens, planning how to respond when a study session stalls, and using a pre-decided fallback when work stress spikes. Those applications test whether stress planning as a way to automate the better response can still be recognized once the reader leaves the headline example behind. They also block sloppy interpretations. Thinking willpower means brute force in the moment is too broad. Treating training scripts as fake instead of functional rehearsal is also too broad. The actual claim is tighter: The chapter values rehearsal and scripting, but it does not imply that stress disappears; it shows how preparation changes the likely response.
