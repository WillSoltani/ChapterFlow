Chapter 2 introduces the missing driver in habit formation: craving. Cue, routine, and reward explain the structure of a habit, but structure alone does not explain why some routines acquire real pull. Duhigg argues that the cue becomes more powerful once it starts generating desire for the reward before the reward arrives.

The Pepsodent case is the cleanest demonstration. Brushing did not become a routine merely because people were told it was healthy. The routine became habit-forming when marketers linked it to a noticeable and anticipated payoff, the tingling clean-mouth sensation that users could quickly learn to expect. The reward was not only felt after brushing. Over time it was felt in advance as the cue appeared.

The Febreze case sharpens the point from the opposite direction. The product initially failed because it lacked a compelling craving structure. It was a new action inserted into life without a clear emotional payoff. Once marketers repositioned it as the final step of a cleaning routine, the smell took on a different status. It became the signal that the room was finished. The routine now plugged into an existing desire for completion and order.

The general mechanism is that anticipated reward produces momentum. A cue that merely reminds you of a possible action is weaker than a cue that makes the reward feel immediate in imagination. That anticipatory force is craving. It turns habit from inert repetition into a self-starting pattern.

This chapter also broadens the stakes of habit science. Craving can be designed. The same logic that helps an individual make a useful routine more repeatable can help a company create stickier consumer behavior. That is not incidental. It means habit formation is never just a private self-help matter; it is also a design and influence problem.

The chapter's limit is worth keeping in view. Craving is real even when the person cannot articulate it cleanly. Anticipation may be sensory, emotional, social, or tied to a vague feeling of completion. The model remains useful because it tells the reader where to look. Chapter 2 does not replace the loop. It explains why the loop becomes strong enough to matter. The chapter stays useful only if the mechanism survives contact with routine life. That is why it keeps pointing toward pairing a study ritual with an expected moment of relief or satisfaction, designing a workout setup that creates desire before effort begins, and noticing why a phone check feels magnetic before any notification content appears. Those applications test whether the cue only matters once the reward becomes expected can still be recognized once the reader leaves the headline example behind. They also block sloppy interpretations. Reducing craving to greed instead of reward expectation is too broad. Assuming cues work on their own without any learned anticipation is also too broad. The actual claim is tighter: Craving explains why a habit gathers force, but it does not remove the need for structure or later substitution tactics.
