Chapter 7 argues that habits are externally legible in ways that make them commercially valuable. Repeated behaviors create patterns in purchase timing, item combinations, and sequencing. Once those patterns are collected at scale, companies can use them to predict future behavior or infer private life changes with surprising accuracy.

Duhigg's Target example anchors that claim. The case matters because it shows how behavioral traces can become predictive without requiring direct disclosure. A company does not need a confession if the routine itself emits enough signal.

The chapter pairs prediction with influence. People resist abrupt novelty, so marketers often place new products inside familiar routines and environments. Existing habits reduce the friction of adoption. The customer does not have to feel like a new behavior is being installed because the new element arrives under cover of something already normalized.

The moral tension is the heart of the chapter. Prediction can be framed as convenience. It can also become manipulation when one side has an interpretive advantage the other side did not expect. The company knows not only what the customer bought, but what that behavior pattern may mean before the customer would have chosen to reveal it.

The chapter remains measured. It does not imply perfect prediction or omnipotent marketing. The power comes from partial but useful accuracy, especially when combined with scale and strategic placement. A model does not have to be flawless to alter behavior effectively.

Chapter 7 therefore extends habit science into data power. Once routines become readable patterns, they become forecastable and steerable. The book's earlier mechanism of cue, routine, and reward remains true, but now the loop is being observed and exploited from the outside as well as lived from within. The chapter stays useful only if the mechanism survives contact with routine life. That is why it keeps pointing toward auditing what your purchase history reveals about your routines, noticing how apps place new features inside existing clicks, and evaluating whether convenience is masking a stronger steering attempt. Those applications test whether familiar placement lowering resistance to novelty can still be recognized once the reader leaves the headline example behind. They also block sloppy interpretations. Assuming the chapter claims perfect prediction is too broad. Treating all personalization as identical to manipulation is also too broad. The actual claim is tighter: The chapter is about useful partial prediction at scale, not omniscient models or total consumer helplessness.
