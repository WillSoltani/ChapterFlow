Chapter 4 asks the leverage question that naturally follows the earlier chapters: if effort is limited, which habits are most worth changing first? Duhigg's answer is keystone habits, routines with disproportionate influence over surrounding behavior.

The main case is Paul O'Neill's emphasis on worker safety at Alcoa. The chapter's claim is not simply that safety is morally important. It is that safety occupied a strategic location in the organization's operating system. If injuries had to be reported immediately, then information flow had to improve. If risk reports had to be taken seriously, then managers had to respond faster and more honestly. If breakdowns generated learning rather than concealment, then accountability norms changed. Safety therefore functioned as a leverage point, not a narrow policy area.

The mechanism is small wins with spillover. When one central routine begins generating credible improvement, surrounding expectations change. People become more willing to share information, coordinate, and sustain higher standards in adjacent areas. The resulting movement can look surprisingly broad even when the original intervention was narrow.

The chapter also draws an important boundary. Not every habit deserves the label keystone. A habit may be personally important and still have weak systemic reach. The keystone category should be reserved for routines whose improvement changes multiple linked behaviors because of their structural position inside a system.

This is what makes Chapter 4 strategic rather than inspirational. It asks the reader to think in terms of spillover. Which routine changes reporting, trust, identity, timing, or coordination if it improves? Which routine remains isolated? The answers determine whether effort produces a cascade or a local patch.

The limitation is that keystone habits can be misidentified. Leaders can project special status onto a habit that lacks real leverage and then wonder why little else moved. The chapter is strongest when used as a test of causal reach. If the routine changes, what else is plausibly forced to change with it? That is the keystone question. The chapter stays useful only if the mechanism survives contact with routine life. That is why it keeps pointing toward choosing one team ritual that forces better communication downstream, using a morning planning routine to stabilize unrelated work behavior, and picking one household habit that cleans up several adjacent decisions. Those applications test whether keystone habits triggering wider operational change can still be recognized once the reader leaves the headline example behind. They also block sloppy interpretations. Assuming a keystone habit fixes everything automatically is too broad. Treating any visible habit as keystone without evidence of spillover is also too broad. The actual claim is tighter: Keystone habits matter because of the systems they unlock, not because one symbolic practice is magically sufficient on its own. The working pressure point is Paul O'Neill choosing worker safety as the keystone focus. That is where the chapter stops being summary and starts becoming a diagnostic. The unresolved question is How do you tell whether a candidate keystone habit truly changes surrounding behavior instead of just looking impressive?
