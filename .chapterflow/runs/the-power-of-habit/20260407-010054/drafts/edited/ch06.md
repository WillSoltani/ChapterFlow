Chapter 6 argues that crises often act as catalysts for institutional habit change because they expose the cost of entrenched routines and weaken resistance to redesign. Organizations can preserve poor habits for long periods when those habits are familiar, politically distributed, or buffered by normalization. A crisis alters that equilibrium.

Importantly, the chapter does not claim that crisis creates wisdom automatically. The relevant failures often predate the event. What the crisis changes is the organization's ability to deny or compartmentalize them. Once the breakdown is visible, reporting patterns, escalation routines, and accountability structures become easier to challenge.

This creates what the chapter treats as a window of plasticity. Leaders can change routines that would have been much harder to touch under normal conditions. Communication can be restructured, authority clarified, and new habits installed. The old default has been exposed as costly enough that it temporarily loses part of its defense.

The ethical tension is central. A crisis can be used for genuine reform, but it can also be used for superficial reaction or power consolidation. Panic does not equal good design. The key question is what durable routine actually replaces the exposed failure once the immediate emotion of the event subsides.

That is why the chapter's practical lesson is to convert urgency into habit quickly. If the institution waits too long, the opening closes and path dependence reasserts itself. New reporting rules, communication protocols, or accountability expectations need to become normal before the system re-hardens around old behavior.
