Core Claim:
Chapter 3 turns from formation to substitution. Duhigg's core claim is that habits are rarely destroyed outright. They are more often changed by preserving the cue and reward while replacing the routine between them. That is the golden rule of habit change.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
Strongest at making change feel concrete: keep the loop frame, swap the routine, and support it with believable reinforcement.

Author Logic Chain:
First the author establishes the golden rule: keep the same cue and reward, change the routine.
Then the author develops belief as the stabilizer that helps replacement hold under pressure.
Then the author tests the idea against AA and coaching examples as evidence that substitution needs support.

Specific Applications:

- building a work reset routine that keeps the same stress cue but changes the response
- using group accountability to make a replacement routine stick

Common Misreadings:

Counterarguments or Limits:
The chapter argues for substitution, but it also concedes that stress can reactivate old loops if the replacement is weak or unsupported.
