The first response to a setback is often diagnostic. A body overreacts to a cut with inflammation. A system hit by stress may not simply crawl back to where it started. It may rebuild with extra margin, extra sensitivity, or extra reserve. Taleb uses that pattern to make antifragility visible in motion rather than in definition. Some things answer harm with more than repair. They overcompensate.

That idea sounds excessive until you notice how often smooth efficiency depends on thin margins. A system designed with no spare capacity can look intelligent in calm periods because it wastes so little. Then the first disruption arrives and reveals what the design was really buying: neatness at the price of brittleness. Redundancy, by contrast, looks inefficient right until it becomes the reason the system can absorb shock, learn from it, and continue. What appears to be slack may actually be reserve.

Taleb's point is not that every strong response is wise. Panic is not adaptive rebound. The claim is narrower and more structural. A system can gain from stress when it contains room to answer that stress with more capacity than before. That room may take the form of spare inventory, duplicated function, backup pathways, or a rebuilding process that overshoots the original level. What matters is that the response is not linear. The hit does not only subtract. Under the right conditions it produces correction, reserve, and information.

That is why redundancy matters so much here. Modern efficiency talk often treats redundancy as a vice, a sign that a system has not been trimmed hard enough. Taleb flips the judgment. Strip away every spare layer and a system may become more elegant and more fragile at the same time. The reserve that looked wasteful on a spreadsheet can be what allows it to survive interruption without catastrophic failure. More than that, reserve can let interruption teach. A lesson only helps if the structure is still there to learn it.

The argument becomes sharper when Taleb moves to information. Some messages spread because people try to suppress them. A banned book, a censored story, an attacked rumor, a forbidden post: the attempt to kill it can become the mechanism that enlarges its audience. Pressure creates attention, and attention creates spread. Information, in that sense, can be antifragile. Attack does not always shrink it. Sometimes attack is what multiplies it.

That example matters because it keeps the chapter from shrinking into a biology lesson. Antifragility is not confined to tissues or supply chains. It can appear wherever pressure creates amplification rather than silence. Yet the limit stays in force. Not every censorship attempt backfires. Not every attacked idea gets stronger. Context decides whether suppression creates curiosity and spread or whether it simply smothers the thing.

The same limit governs overreaction. Some rebounds strengthen a system and some distort it. Some redundancy protects and some merely clutters. Taleb is not praising excess for its own sake. He is showing why a culture trained to worship lean efficiency will underrate reserve, rebound, and backfire. Systems with no room for error cannot use error. Systems with no reserve cannot turn shocks into information.

Chapter 2 advances the opening map by showing antifragility in action. It appears in overcompensation after injury, in spare capacity that looks wasteful until stress arrives, and in information that grows because someone tried to bury it. The common pattern is bounded response with upside. That naturally raises the next question: what kind of systems can do this consistently, and why do living systems often need stressors that machines simply wear down under?
