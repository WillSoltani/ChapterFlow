# Edited Draft — ch22

Long life often gets imagined as an engineering contest: add more tools, more corrections, more interventions, and eventually the system will be forced into safety. Taleb resists that picture. In an opaque system, many harms arrive through what gets added in the name of improvement. Longevity may therefore depend more on subtraction than on escalation.

That is the via negativa logic here. When the body and its environment cannot be fully read from the surface, additions carry hidden downside. One visible benefit can distract from broader costs created by excess, overmanagement, or intervention that multiplies fragility elsewhere. The strongest principle is not endless optimization. It is refusing to worsen what time has already filtered.

This changes what prudence means. Prudence is not automatically more activity. It may mean removing sources of avoidable stress, declining needless complexity, and resisting the temptation to manage every margin of life. In a partly hidden system, subtraction can outperform addition because it opens fewer new paths for harm.

That is why harm avoidance is not merely defensive in the chapter. It is a positive strategy for longevity. Cutting away what predictably adds fragility can matter more than stacking complicated fixes whose downstream costs remain unclear. The goal is not heroic control. It is durability through not making the system more delicate.

The chapter still needs a limit. It is not preaching that all care is foolish or that doing nothing is always best. The claim is that in opaque domains, complicated additions should not outrank simple removal by default. The alternative to overengineering is selective judgment, not passive neglect.

This also sets up the next move. Once subtraction becomes central, the ethical issue becomes exposure: who bears the downside when someone adds risk to a system. That is how longevity through restraint leads into skin in the game.
