# Chapter 21: Medicine, Convexity, and Opacity

Taleb uses medicine as a central case of intervention under opacity. In such domains, intention and short-run benefit do not settle whether acting is wise because hidden downside may be large, nonlinear, and difficult to observe. The reader is meant to see that when the system is opaque, doing more can require stronger justification than doing less.

That is why opacity matters. A treatment, policy, or correction may produce a visible gain while also carrying harms that the decision-maker cannot easily trace. If the mechanism is only partly visible, the intervention cannot be judged safely by intention alone or by one favorable result. What is hidden may dominate what is immediately seen.

Convexity raises the stakes. In some contexts, downside accelerates faster than expected once intervention grows in intensity or complexity. A small additional action may produce disproportionately larger harm. This means the burden of proof for intervention rises in systems where hidden downside is plausible and asymmetric. More action is not automatically more care.

The chapter therefore emphasizes restraint under uncertainty. Restraint is not magic and it is not always right, but it can be safer when the downside of intervention is opaque and potentially nonlinear. Taleb wants the reader to notice the asymmetry: adding force to a poorly understood system can create damage that inaction would never have introduced.

The boundary matters. This is not a slogan against medicine or against intervention in all forms. It is a warning against confident action in systems whose hidden structure makes harm easy to underestimate. Interventions remain possible, but they should face a higher burden of proof where opacity and convexity are present.

That bridge leads naturally into the next chapter. If poorly understood additions can create hidden harm, then long life may depend less on piling on fixes and more on subtracting avoidable damage, excess, and fragile-making inputs.
