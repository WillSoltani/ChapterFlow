# Edited Draft — ch24

A profession can sound ethical long before it becomes trustworthy. Taleb argues that ethics gains force only when it is fitted to the real structure of a role: what the professional can decide, what downside that role can create, and whether the practitioner is exposed to the consequences of being wrong. A code without consequence is easy to praise and easy to evade.

That is why skin in the game has to become professional rather than merely general. A profession earns trust less by announcing values than by tying practice to exposure. If advisers, leaders, or experts can authorize harm while staying insulated from it, the ethical language around the profession becomes decorative. It may still sound serious, but it no longer disciplines behavior.

The chapter therefore treats fitted ethics as role-shaped rather than universal in the abstract. Different professions create different forms of authority, risk, and asymmetric downside. Ethical norms must be matched to those actual channels of consequence. The real question is not whether a principle appears in a handbook. It is whether the profession's structure makes violation costly and accountability real.

That is where trust comes from. People do not rely deeply on professionals because they read polished statements. They rely when the role itself is bound to reciprocity, consequence-bearing, and shared exposure. Ethics becomes credible when promises have a price for the person making them.

The chapter still needs a limit. Taleb is not rejecting all written ethics or claiming institutions should abandon articulated norms. He is saying that codes become weak when they float above a role without exposure. Ethics without consequence drifts toward performance.

This sets up the conclusion. The final step is to gather the book's practical and ethical threads back into one argument about fragility, optionality, time, and what genuinely benefits from disorder.
