A system can look kinder the moment visible pain disappears. Taleb uses that relief to attack a familiar mistake: some interventions calm the surface while making the system underneath less able to learn. The issue is not helping in general. The issue is help that removes the feedback a system needed.

That is what makes intervention naive in this chapter. A manager who rescues every small issue, a school rule that suppresses every visible disruption, or a family member who over-solves every discomfort may create immediate calm. But the calm can come from blocking correction. Small failures stop teaching, local adjustment weakens, and hidden fragility starts collecting offstage.

The chapter therefore separates visible pain from real improvement. A visible irritation can carry signal about what needs to change. If an intervention erases the irritation without fixing the structure beneath it, the system may look healthier while becoming less adaptive. Delayed harm grows because the system lost its chance to learn early and cheaply.

Taleb is not praising pain or condemning all intervention. Some help prevents ruin, restores function, or shields a system from exposures it cannot safely absorb. The useful distinction is whether the intervention preserves learning while limiting damage or instead trades small visible trouble for a larger concealed problem.

This changes practical judgment. The right question is not whether the surface becomes calmer immediately. The right question is whether useful signal stays alive and whether the system becomes more capable after the help. A team process, attendance policy, or personal routine can all become smoother while quietly losing their ability to self-correct.

The chapter extends the book's attack on smoothness. Systems that reward tidy surfaces often reward interventions that hide trouble instead of resolving it. That sets up the next move in the book: why prediction keeps winning prestige in the same environments that keep losing contact with real feedback.
