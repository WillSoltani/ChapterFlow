Two things can share a label, a polished story, or a neat visual resemblance and still be structurally different where it matters. Taleb is warning the reader not to stop at surface similarity. The real question is whether capability, exposure, and payoff are actually alike underneath the narrative.

That is why the chapter attacks false sameness. A résumé, category, or polished explanation can make two options look equivalent even when one has different hidden asymmetries, different sources of skill, or very different downside. Shared description is not the same as shared structure.

The mechanism matters because narrative compression removes the details that carry consequence. Once a story or label smooths those differences away, comparison becomes dangerously lazy. The reader starts judging by polish and category membership instead of by what each option can actually do, survive, or gain from.

This is also why the chapter insists on structural comparison. The issue is not semantic fussiness. The issue is practical difference. Two candidates, projects, or opportunities can look alike on paper while carrying different exposures, different histories of reality-testing, and different payoff shapes underneath.

Taleb keeps a limit on the argument. The chapter is not saying that comparison is impossible or that labels are useless. It is saying that comparison has to continue past the label. Real sameness requires structural resemblance, not just rhetorical resemblance.

That bridge matters because if polished stories already flatten important differences in the present, retrospective history can flatten them even harder. The next chapter turns to that retrospective distortion and asks how history gets written from the wrong angle.
