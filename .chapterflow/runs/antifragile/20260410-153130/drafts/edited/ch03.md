Put a cat in a room and movement keeps it alive. Put a washing machine in a room and use wears it down. Taleb uses the contrast because it breaks a lazy habit of thought. We often talk as if every system should be protected the same way, maintained the same way, and judged by the same standard of smooth operation. But a living thing and a machine do not meet stress on the same terms. One can use stress as information. The other mainly accumulates damage.

That is why this chapter matters after the previous discussion of reserve and rebound. Overcompensation and redundancy can sound universal if the reader is not careful. Taleb narrows the frame. A machine generally degrades under repeated strain. Parts wear. Friction accumulates. Use shortens life. A living system is different because the right kind of stress can trigger repair, calibration, and adaptation. The cat does not flourish by being treated like a fragile ornament. Motion, challenge, and variation are part of what keeps it regulated.

The distinction is not mystical. It is informational. A living system does not merely endure a stressor. It reads the stressor. The signal can trigger adjustment, growth, or improved response, provided the dose stays within a survivable range. That is what the previous chapters were building toward. Some systems gain from volatility because volatility tells them something and their structure allows them to answer. A machine rarely works that way. Push it harder and you usually get more wear, not more intelligence.

This is where mechanical thinking starts causing trouble. If you treat a living system as something that should be preserved through total smoothness, you may remove the very variation it needs to stay competent. Overprotection can weaken by starving the system of signal. That does not mean all stress is helpful. It means zero stress is not a neutral condition for every organism. Some strength is maintained only through contact with manageable challenge.

The distinction also clarifies why maintenance language travels badly across domains. It makes sense to spare a machine unnecessary strain if strain only subtracts. It does not always make sense to spare a body, a skill, or an adaptive routine from every small challenge. In those cases, the challenge may be part of the maintenance. What looks harsh from a machine model may be what keeps the living system calibrated. Taleb's contrast works because it forces the reader to stop applying one model of care everywhere.

The chapter only works if the limit stays visible. A cat can also be harmed by excess. A body can be broken by overload. A living system is not invincible because it is adaptive. The point is not that organisms benefit from any amount of stress. The point is that their relation to stress is nonlinear and informational in a way mechanical systems usually are not. The same treatment can educate one system and degrade another.

Chapter 3 therefore does more than repeat that stress can help. It identifies the kind of system for which that claim can even make sense. Living systems often need variation, feedback, and bounded stress because those things help them regulate and adapt. Machines mostly need protection from wear. Once that difference is clear, the next question sharpens naturally: when does a small dose of harm strengthen a living system, and when does it turn into plain damage?
