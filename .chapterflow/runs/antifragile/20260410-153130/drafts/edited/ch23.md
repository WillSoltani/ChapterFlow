# Edited Draft — ch23

Ethics is often spoken in abstractions: values, intentions, principles, and respectable language about responsibility. Taleb makes the test harder. Ethics becomes credible when the person making a decision shares the downside of that decision. If one party keeps the upside while others absorb the losses, the moral talk around the arrangement is weak by design.

That is the meaning of skin in the game here. A system becomes ethically distorted when optionality is one-sided. The chooser keeps flexibility, prestige, or gain, while someone else pays when the hidden tail arrives. Taleb's point is not merely that this feels unfair. It is that insulation from downside degrades judgment because action is no longer disciplined by consequence.

This is why shared exposure matters. When people bear the cost of what they authorize, recommend, or impose, their decisions are filtered differently. They become less free to gamble with uncertainty on behalf of others. Skin in the game is therefore more than a moral slogan. It is a structural safeguard against reckless asymmetry.

The chapter also sharpens the target. Optionality is not itself the enemy. The ethical failure appears when optionality is kept for oneself while downside is exported to others. Gain stays private while cost becomes someone else's burden. A system organized that way invites fragility because the chooser is rewarded without being corrected by loss.

The chapter still needs a limit. Taleb is not demanding identical exposure in every human setting, nor is he reducing ethics to punishment. His claim is narrower: people who introduce or authorize downside should not be protected from that downside by the very structures they control.

That carries into the next chapter. If skin in the game is the ethical core, then professions must be judged by whether their norms tie practitioners to consequence or let them hide behind abstraction.
