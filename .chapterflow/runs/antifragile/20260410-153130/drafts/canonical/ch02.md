The first response to a setback is often diagnostic. A body overreacts to a cut with inflammation. A system hit by stress may not simply crawl back to where it started. It may rebuild with extra margin, extra sensitivity, or extra reserve. Taleb uses that pattern to make antifragility visible in motion rather than in definition. Some things answer harm with more than repair. They overcompensate.

That idea sounds excessive until you notice how often smooth efficiency depends on thin margins. A system designed with no spare capacity can look intelligent in calm periods because it wastes so little. Then the first disruption arrives and reveals what the design was actually buying: apparent neatness at the price of brittleness. Redundancy, by contrast, looks inefficient right until it becomes the reason the system can absorb shock, learn from it, and continue. What appears to be slack may be adaptive reserve.

Taleb's point is not that any strong response is wise. Panic is not the same thing as adaptive rebound. The chapter's claim is narrower and more structural. A system can gain from stress when it contains room to answer the stress with more capacity than before. That room may take the form of spare inventory, duplicated function, backup pathways, or the biological habit of rebuilding more than was lost. What matters is that the response is not linear. The hit does not only subtract. Under the right conditions it produces correction, reserve, and information.

That is why redundancy matters so much in the chapter. Modern efficiency talk often treats redundancy as a vice, something bloated systems indulge because they have not been disciplined enough to optimize. Taleb reverses the judgment. If all slack is stripped away, a system may become more elegant and more fragile at the same time. The reserve that looked wasteful on a spreadsheet can be what lets it survive an interruption without catastrophic failure. More than that, reserve can let the interruption teach. The lesson only arrives if the structure survives the lesson.

The argument becomes sharper when Taleb moves to information. Some things spread because people try to suppress them. A banned book, a censored story, an attacked rumor, a forbidden message: the attempt to kill it can become the mechanism that enlarges its audience. The pressure gives it attention, and the attention gives it life. Information, in that sense, can be antifragile. Attack does not always shrink it. Sometimes attack is what multiplies it.

That example matters because it stops the chapter from being read only as a biological or engineering claim. Antifragility is not confined to muscles, tissues, or supply chains. It can appear wherever a stressor produces amplification rather than silence. Yet the limit remains. Not every censorship attempt backfires. Not every attacked idea gets stronger. The structure of the environment decides whether suppression creates curiosity, solidarity, and spread or whether it simply kills the thing.

The same limit governs overreaction. There are rebounds that strengthen a system and rebounds that deform it. There is redundancy that protects and redundancy that merely clutters. Taleb is not praising excess for its own sake. He is trying to show why a world trained to worship lean efficiency will systematically underrate the value of reserve, rebound, and backfire. Systems with no room for error cannot use error. Systems with no reserve cannot turn shocks into information.

So Chapter 2 advances the book's opening map by showing what antifragility looks like in practice. It appears in overcompensation after injury, in spare capacity that looks wasteful until stress arrives, and in information that grows because someone tried to bury it. The common pattern is bounded response with upside. The next question is what kind of systems can do that consistently. Why do living systems often need stressors and variability, while machines break down under the same treatment?
