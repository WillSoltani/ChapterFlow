In many complex domains, people learn how to do something before they can explain it cleanly. Taleb uses the absurd image of lecturing birds on flying to attack the prestige assumption that theory must always come first. The point is not that explanation is worthless. The point is that reality-tested practice often arrives earlier than polished understanding.

That is why the chapter values tinkering, craft, and repeated contact with the world. A person does not need a complete formal theory before discovering what works. Iteration, trial, and adjustment can reveal usable knowledge long before a clean framework appears to justify it.

The mechanism matters because reality filters error in ways that abstraction alone cannot. Repeated attempts expose weak moves, preserve what survives contact, and gradually build embodied skill. In that process, practice becomes a producer of knowledge rather than a mere application of knowledge already certified elsewhere.

This is also why the chapter distinguishes disciplined discovery from empty improvisation. The chapter is not praising random motion, contempt for expertise, or hostility to thought. It is praising contact with consequences. Practice matters when reality pushes back, selection occurs, and bad ideas are forced out before a formal explanation is complete.

Taleb keeps a limit on the claim. Theory can still matter, especially once practice has discovered something worth clarifying, organizing, or transmitting. The chapter is narrower than anti-intellectual rhetoric. It is saying that effective practice often generates the raw material that theory later names and cleans up.

That bridge matters because once polished explanation loses its monopoly on authority, another problem appears. People still confuse polished narratives and labels with real capability. The next chapter turns to that false sameness and asks why neat descriptions keep hiding important structural differences.
