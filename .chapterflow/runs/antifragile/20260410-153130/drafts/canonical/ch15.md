After the fact, events often get retold in cleaner language than the people inside them ever used while the outcome was still uncertain. Taleb is warning that history and commentary written from outside can miss the operative logic of builders, survivors, and practitioners. The story becomes neat just where the real work had been messy, adaptive, and practical.

That is why the chapter attacks retrospective distortion. Once the result is known, observers can retell the path as if it had always been obvious. In doing so, they often replace the living logic of trial, adjustment, and exposure with a cleaner explanation that flatters the narrator more than it describes the process.

The mechanism matters because after-the-fact narration rewards coherence. A clean retrospective story makes events easier to summarize, easier to teach, and easier to admire. But those gains in tidiness can come at the cost of losing the very details that made the outcome possible in real time.

This is also why the chapter focuses on observer mismatch. People who describe the result later may not understand what participants were actually optimizing for, fearing, testing, or surviving while events were still open. A retrospective narrative can therefore miss the practical logic that governed the situation from the inside.

Taleb keeps a limit on the claim. The chapter is not saying all history is useless or that observers can never learn anything. It is saying that retrospective explanation must be treated cautiously whenever neat narration seems to outrun the operative structure that real participants were using.

That bridge matters because once neat retrospective stories lose authority, another question follows. If overclean narration misses so much, what kind of rigor can still survive in trial, mess, and self-discovery directly? The next chapter turns to that lesson in disorder.
