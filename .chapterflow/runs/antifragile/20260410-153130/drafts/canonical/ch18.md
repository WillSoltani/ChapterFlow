# Chapter 18: On the Difference Between a Large Stone and a Thousand Pebbles

Taleb opens the nonlinearity section by attacking a simple habit of thought: the idea that equal totals must mean equal effects. A single large impact and many small impacts can add up to the same amount on paper while producing radically different outcomes in reality. The reason is that many harms and benefits do not scale linearly.

The chapter's image makes the point concrete. A large stone is not the same as a thousand pebbles. Concentration matters. Dose matters. A single heavy hit can break what many light hits would not. Likewise, many small exposures can be tolerable or even informative where one concentrated dose becomes ruinous. Arithmetic remains true, but it is no longer sufficient.

This is why response curves matter. Systems often react disproportionately to scale. The same summed quantity can produce different consequences depending on how it is distributed across time, space, or intensity. Naive aggregation misses this because it acts as if the path from quantity to effect were always straight.

The chapter therefore sharpens practical judgment. In work, policy, medicine, stress, and risk, it is not enough to ask how much in total. One must ask how much at once, how concentrated, and how the receiving system responds. The same total burden can be survivable when spread out and destructive when loaded all at once.

Taleb is not claiming arithmetic has failed. The limit is narrower. Simple addition is incomplete when the world answers nonlinearly. The reader must stop treating equal sums as equal realities whenever concentration and scale change the shape of harm or benefit.

This prepares the next step. If concentration alters consequence so dramatically, then questions of inversion, intervention, and opacity become unavoidable. The structure of response matters more than simple totals suggest.
