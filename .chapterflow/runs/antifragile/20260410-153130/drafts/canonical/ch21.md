# Chapter 21: Medicine, Convexity, and Opacity

Taleb now applies the logic of fragility and time to domains where intervention happens under deep uncertainty. Medicine is his leading example because it is a field full of intention, action, and hidden downside. The point is not that intervention never helps. It is that in opaque systems, acting can create harms that are difficult to see in advance, while restraint may carry less unseen downside than confident action.

This is why opacity matters. When the mechanism connecting intervention to result is partly hidden, the decision-maker cannot safely rely on intention or on one visible benefit. A treatment or policy may help on one margin and still generate damage elsewhere. The trouble is that hidden downside does not announce itself clearly at the moment of action.

Convexity sharpens the warning. In some systems, downside accelerates faster than benefit, so a little extra intervention can create much more harm than a linear mind expects. This makes the burden of proof for acting much higher. If the system is opaque and the downside is asymmetric, then "do something" stops being an obviously prudent default.

The chapter therefore contrasts action with restraint. Restraint is not always wise, but it can dominate when the visible upside of intervention is modest while the hidden downside is large, nonlinear, or poorly understood. Taleb is asking the reader to respect the asymmetry between adding a force to an opaque system and leaving it alone.

This is not an anti-medicine chapter in a crude sense. Its limit is narrower. The claim is that where opacity and hidden downside are serious, interventions should earn trust through stronger evidence and clearer asymmetry. Action is not condemned. Overconfident action under poor visibility is.

This prepares the next move in Via Negativa. If much of the danger comes from adding poorly understood interventions, then long life may depend less on optimization by addition and more on not worsening the system through avoidable harm and excess.
