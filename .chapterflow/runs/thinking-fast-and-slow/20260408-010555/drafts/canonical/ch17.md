# Regression to the Mean

Jonah watches a sales team come off a miserable month. The manager gives a harsh speech, tightens expectations, and warns everyone that another dip will not be tolerated. The next month looks better. In the room, the causal story writes itself. The speech worked. The pressure corrected the problem. Kahneman uses Regression to the Mean to show why that conclusion is often too fast. Extreme outcomes are frequently followed by more ordinary ones even when nobody has discovered the right lesson or applied the right intervention. Random variation naturally drifts back toward typical levels. The mind then misreads that ordinary drift as proof that praise, blame, effort, or discipline caused the change.

The chapter matters because this mistake feels like common sense. If a terrible result is followed by an improvement, people want a reason for the improvement. If a spectacular result is followed by a decline, they want a reason for the decline. A story about coaching, attitude, pressure, or confidence feels more satisfying than a story about ordinary fluctuation. But the swing back toward average often needed no special cause. The first result was unusually high or low. The next one was more likely to be ordinary. That is the whole point. Regression is not an excuse for never intervening. It is a warning that extreme cases create a misleading before-and-after picture even when the world is just returning to its normal range.

The exam example makes the lesson easier to respect. A student gets an unusually high score, receives warm praise, and scores lower on the next test. Another student gets an unusually low score, receives criticism, and scores better next time. A teacher who does not notice regression can walk away believing criticism works and praise weakens performance. The evidence feels personal and moral because the sequence invites that interpretation. Yet the likely explanation may be simpler. The first scores were extreme. The next scores had room to move back toward the average. If that statistical tendency is ignored, ordinary fluctuation turns into a false theory of motivation.

The same pattern shows up in personal routines. Priya has an intense exercise week, feels sore and depleted, then finds that the following week feels more ordinary. It is tempting to explain the second week by one specific recovery tactic, one conversation, or one change in schedule. Sometimes that explanation may be right. But sometimes the better explanation is that the extreme week was unlikely to repeat itself in the same form. Regression creates a false spotlight on whatever happened just before the return to normal. The mind is always ready to hand credit to the last visible cause because it dislikes leaving a change unexplained.

That is why the practical correction has to be explicit. Expect movement toward the average after extremes before assigning credit or blame. Ask whether the first observation was far enough from ordinary that some reversal should have been expected anyway. Ask what the baseline pattern looks like across many cases, not just the memorable pair that happened to come first and second. This does not remove human agency from the picture. It keeps agency from stealing credit that belongs to ordinary variation. Better judgment starts when the reader can say: improvement after a bad result does not by itself validate the intervention, and decline after an exceptional result does not by itself prove failure.

The chapter also keeps a real limit in view. Interventions can matter. Coaching can help. Punishment can sometimes change behavior. Recovery routines can make a difference. Kahneman's point is not that feedback never works. It is that people should not award causality automatically when regression would have produced a similar movement on its own. That distinction matters because false theories become policy very easily. A manager can become harsher than necessary. A teacher can confuse randomness with effective discipline. A person can blame or praise themselves for swings that were partly built into the structure of the situation.

This is one reason extreme events are so dangerous for judgment. They come with strong emotion, strong memory, and a strong appetite for explanation. An awful month and an excellent month both tempt people into overconfident lessons because each one feels like a message. Regression says that some of that message is just distance from the ordinary. Once the reader sees that, a different discipline becomes possible. Instead of asking only what action came before the reversal, the reader asks what reversal would have been likely even without that action. That question does not kill learning. It protects learning from being captured by noise.

The chapter therefore gives the reader a statistical humility that is useful precisely when experience feels most persuasive.
It replaces the cheap certainty of a neat before-and-after story with a harder but more honest question about what the baseline was already likely to do next.
That extra pause is often the difference between learning a real pattern and inventing a lesson from ordinary variance.
It keeps memory from becoming fake evidence.

The bridge to the next chapter is exact. Regression to the Mean teaches the reader not to overread extreme cases and their immediate aftermath. Taming Intuitive Predictions takes the next step. Once the reader sees that extreme observations should be pulled back toward the ordinary, better forecasting becomes possible. Prediction improves when intuition stops treating one vivid result as if it deserves a fully extreme forecast.
