# How Judgments Happen

Maya thinks the team is discussing probability, but the meeting sounds more like a discussion of confidence. The question on the table is whether a launch is likely to go smoothly. What people actually keep responding to is how convincing, energetic, or uneasy the proposal feels. Kahneman uses How Judgments Happen to show that hard judgments are often converted into simpler impressions or intensity matches. When formal calculation is difficult, the mind borrows what is easiest to feel or picture and lets that easier impression do the work.

That is why the chapter matters. The mistake is not only that judgment moves fast. It is that the answer often fits a different question from the one that was asked. A probability question becomes a question about strength of feeling. A rubric-based evaluation becomes a reaction to one vivid feature. Priya sees this when an essay starts getting judged by the force of one memorable line instead of the full criteria. Jonah sees it when he rates an entire week by the mood of the present moment. The substitute answer can feel responsive while quietly missing the original task.

The pattern remains hard to notice because the easier question usually resembles the harder one just enough to pass. People feel as if they answered the real question. They do not experience themselves as switching tracks. If someone asks how risky a plan is, an accessible impression of confidence, vividness, or familiarity may arrive first. If someone asks how good an essay is, the mind may answer with a reaction to style, effort, or one especially sharp sentence. The borrowed impression is easier to produce, so it arrives with speed and force. That speed and force then get mistaken for relevance.

That is what makes the chapter operational. Kahneman is not asking the reader to replace intuition with constant formal analysis. He is asking for one diagnostic pause: state the exact question and ask whether the answer was built from a substitute dimension. Maya's team needs that move in the forecast meeting. Priya needs it when a rubric is being replaced by one striking feature. Jonah needs it when a whole period of life is being summarized by the emotion of the moment. The practical correction is not mystical. It is to name the target judgment clearly enough that the substitute becomes visible.

The chapter also exposes why substitution is so hard to catch in real time. The easier question is often not absurd. It is adjacent. Confidence can look relevant to probability. Style can look relevant to quality. Current mood can look relevant to a broader life rating. Because the surrogate resembles the target, the mind gets the emotional reward of answering without paying the full cognitive price of judging. That is why the answer can feel so persuasive. It is not random. It is merely misaligned.

That misalignment matters most when the substitute dimension is easier precisely because it is less demanding. A forecast discussion built around how convincing a presenter feels may miss base rates, outside cases, or unresolved operational constraints. An essay judgment built around one memorable sentence may miss structure, evidence, or consistency. A life judgment built around today's mood may erase the actual pattern of the week. Kahneman is helping the reader see that the problem is not only mental laziness. It is task drift. The mind quietly changes the question in order to keep moving.

That drift can become institutional just as easily as it becomes personal. Teams often start with one question on the slide and answer a different one in the room. Classrooms can announce a rubric and still reward the feature that is easiest to notice. People can say they are evaluating a week, a relationship, or a plan when they are really reporting the intensity of the present reaction. The chapter matters because naming that drift gives the reader a way to interrupt it before the surrogate answer becomes official.

That is the moment when a private shortcut becomes a shared error.

It also explains why some corrections feel strangely effortful. To repair the judgment, the reader has to recover the original question and hold onto it long enough for the easier answer to lose some of its grip. That is uncomfortable because the substitute usually arrived first and already feels fluent. Kahneman is showing that better judgment often begins with that recovery step. Before calculating more, the reader has to restore the right target.

That restoration takes discipline.

This chapter also advances the sequence from the last one. A Machine for Jumping to Conclusions showed how a thin slice can start feeling like a full case. How Judgments Happen shows how the mind actually produces the judgment once it is leaning on what is easiest to access. The answer is often assembled from whatever impression is available fastest, not from the dimension that the original question truly requires.

Kahneman keeps the limit clear here too. Shortcuts help under pressure, but they mislead when the borrowed impression is too loose for the task. Daily life requires ordinary approximation. The problem appears when the substitute dimension is badly matched to the stakes or the question. Better judgment therefore means checking surrogate answers where the cost of mismatch is high, not treating every rapid impression as suspect by definition.

That is why the bridge to the next chapter is so direct. Once the reader sees that hard questions are often answered by easier ones, the next task is to study that substitution more explicitly. Answering an Easier Question is already waiting inside this chapter. The durable lesson here is simple: before trusting the answer, make sure it belongs to the question you meant to ask.
