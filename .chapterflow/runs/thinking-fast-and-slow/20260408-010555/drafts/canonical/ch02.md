# Attention and Effort

If Chapter 1 gives the mind two characters, Chapter 2 explains why one of them is so often absent when you need it. Kahneman's point is not merely that slow thinking is slower. It is that slow thinking costs attention. You can feel the cost whenever a task recruits enough effort that it narrows the rest of your awareness. The body tightens, distraction becomes more expensive, and competing demands start to collide instead of coexisting.

That is the chapter's main contribution. System 2 is a limited resource user. It can calculate, compare, resist impulses, and follow rules, but it cannot do all of that without drawing from a scarce mental budget. Once the budget is occupied, other effortful demands suffer. A person under load may miss a cue, lose track of a step, or become less able to monitor what they are doing. The chapter matters because it turns careful thought from an abstract virtue into a constrained capacity.

Kahneman uses this to explain why difficult tasks interfere with each other. If one job is consuming effort, another job that also requires monitoring or control cannot simply run at full strength beside it. That is a much stronger claim than saying people dislike multitasking. The point is structural. Two automatic activities may coexist easily, but two effortful ones compete because they are drawing on the same scarce pool. The limitation is built into the architecture, not into personal weakness.

Once that is visible, the chapter starts to explain behavior that people often moralize too quickly. A loaded mind is not just slower. It is less available for checking, restraint, and noticing. Under pressure or distraction, people may become more literal, less patient, less observant, or more likely to accept the first workable answer. That does not excuse every error, but it does change the explanation. Some failures are not failures of motive. They are failures of remaining bandwidth.

The chapter is careful about one limit. Effort is a sign of cost, not a guarantee of quality. People can work hard on the wrong thing. They can feel strain and still reach a bad answer. The gain comes from understanding what effort tells you about system availability. If a judgment requires sustained checking, and the checking system is already loaded, the odds of a weak answer rise whether or not the thinker feels sincere and serious.

That is why the bridge to The Lazy Controller matters. Chapter 1 said that System 2 often endorses what System 1 proposes. Chapter 2 explains one major reason why: deliberate correction is expensive, and the mind does not have endless attention to spend on it. The next chapter turns that resource limit into a behavioral one by showing how often the monitoring system simply accepts the easy answer and moves on.
