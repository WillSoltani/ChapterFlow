# Answering an Easier Question

Maya walks into a strategy meeting expecting a hard argument about market size. The plan on the screen is supposed to answer whether the opportunity is large enough to justify the next year of work. Within minutes, the discussion has shifted. People talk about whether they like the product, whether the founder seems sharp, whether the demo felt persuasive. Those things are not irrelevant, but they are not market size. Kahneman uses Answering an Easier Question to name that shift. When the real question is hard, abstract, or uncomfortable, the mind often answers a nearby question that is easier to handle and then returns that answer as if the original task had been completed.

That is the chapter's core warning. The problem is not simply speed. It is question drift. A judgment that looks complete may be built from the wrong dimension. Maya's room feels as if it has made progress because people have strong reactions and those reactions are easy to state. The trouble is that a discussion about likability or confidence is being smuggled into a discussion about size, demand, or probability. The mind returns a confident answer to the wrong question, and the confidence can feel completely sincere because the substitute question was real. It just was not the one that mattered most.

The chapter matters because the swap is usually hard to feel from the inside. People do not say, I will avoid the hard question now. They say, I have an impression, and the impression must count for something. Priya feels the same shift when she is trying to choose a paper topic. The real question is which topic best fits her long-term interests, the course goals, and the work she can sustain over time. The easier question is which topic feels least painful this week. The easier answer arrives first, so it starts to sound wise. Jonah faces the same pattern when thinking about relocation. The real question is what city would fit his work, relationships, and daily life over years. The easier question is how good one recent weekend visit felt. That answer is vivid, so it quickly starts impersonating a broader judgment.

Substitution works because the easier question is often adjacent to the harder one. Liking a product might tell you something about market response. Current energy might tell you something about whether a topic is viable. A pleasant weekend in a city might tell you something about whether living there could work. The substitute does not feel absurd. It feels relevant enough to borrow legitimacy. That is why the mechanism is so dangerous. If the easier question were obviously unrelated, the mind would reject it. Instead, the easier answer feels like a usable shortcut, and the shortcut quietly becomes the whole judgment.

This is also why the chapter is more precise than it first appears. Kahneman is not only saying that people use shortcuts. He is showing how a shortcut can rewrite the assignment without announcing itself. The original question may have required base rates, tradeoffs, or time horizons. The easier one can often be answered from mood, vividness, familiarity, or immediate liking. Once that happens, the person feels oriented. But the orientation may have been purchased by lowering the difficulty of the task instead of by improving the quality of the answer.

That misalignment matters in institutions because groups are especially vulnerable to substitute questions that are easy to talk about. A room can hold a confident conversation about charisma, clarity, or momentum more easily than it can hold a disciplined conversation about uncertain demand. A classroom conversation about long-term fit can slide into what seems manageable right now. A family discussion about relocation can become a collage of favorite moments instead of a serious look at daily constraints. In each case, the easier question is socially attractive because it produces fluent speech, quick agreement, and emotional texture. The harder question often produces hesitation, numbers, and unresolved tradeoffs.

That is why the practical correction has to be blunt. Rewrite the target question in plain language and test whether the answer actually belongs to it. Maya can ask, Are we judging market size, or are we reporting how much we like the product? Priya can ask, Am I choosing for long-term fit, or am I choosing for short-term relief? Jonah can ask, Am I evaluating life in this city, or am I replaying one good weekend? The correction is not elegant, but it is effective because it forces the target back into view. Before the reader can improve the answer, the reader has to recover the real assignment.

The chapter also explains why better judgment can feel awkward. Once the substitute answer has arrived, it already carries fluency. Returning to the real question usually means returning to uncertainty, slower reasoning, or less flattering evidence. That feels like a step backward even when it is actually a step toward accuracy. People prefer the easier answer partly because it is easier, but also because it creates a sense of completion. Restoring the harder question reopens the case. Kahneman is showing that this reopening move is part of mature judgment, not evidence of confusion.

He also keeps the limit clear. Proxy questions can be helpful starting points, but they become dangerous when they impersonate the real task. Everyday life runs on approximation. A quick feeling can sometimes be good enough. The chapter breaks if it turns into a command to distrust every fast answer. Its actual demand is selective. When the stakes are meaningful and the target question is materially different from the easier one, the reader has to resist the emotional convenience of the substitute.

That is what makes the bridge backward and forward so clean. How Judgments Happen showed how the mind borrows accessible impressions when formal calculation is hard. Answering an Easier Question names the move directly and turns it into a practical diagnostic. The Law of Small Numbers then picks up a related problem. Once the mind is already willing to live on substitutes and simplifications, it also becomes eager to extract stable meaning from runs of evidence that are much too small. The durable lesson here is plain: before trusting an answer, make sure it belongs to the question you actually needed to solve.
