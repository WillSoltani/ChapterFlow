# Risk Policies

Jonah is reviewing a proposal that would be unwise if it were the only risky move the company planned this year. In isolation it looks uncomfortable. Inside the full portfolio, however, it is one of many bets, some cautious and some aggressive, all meant to work together. Kahneman uses Risk Policies to explain why that broader view matters so much. People make erratic decisions when each risky choice is treated as a separate emotional episode. Judgment improves when choices are governed by a policy set at the broader level, where the real objective and the real distribution of risk can be seen.

This chapter matters because narrow framing makes local prudence look better than it really is. A person can reject every individually risky opportunity and end up with a portfolio that is too timid for its goal. Another person can take one vivid risk after another, each defended on its own terms, and end up with an exposure they would never have accepted if the whole set had been visible at once. Kahneman's point is not that single decisions never matter. It is that many single decisions belong to a class, and the class is the right place to decide the policy. Once the broad rule is set, the local case becomes easier to judge.

The work example shows why this matters. Maya's team is deciding whether to fund a project with uncertain upside. Evaluated alone, the project invites all the usual anxieties about loss, blame, and regret. Evaluated inside the broader strategy, it may be exactly the kind of risk the company needs to take occasionally if it wants growth rather than pure stability. The portfolio view does not eliminate danger. It changes its meaning. A loss on one project can be acceptable inside a broader policy that expects a mix of outcomes. When people ignore that frame, they start demanding certainty from each decision even when certainty at the policy level is impossible.

The school example has the same structure. Priya is choosing courses, internships, and applications. If she judges every item alone, she may avoid each difficult option because any one of them feels risky. When she looks at the whole package, a different logic emerges. Perhaps one stretch class, one safer class, one ambitious application, and one reliable option form a better overall strategy than treating every choice as if it had to be safe on its own. Risk policy here does not mean becoming reckless. It means deciding the acceptable shape of the whole package before letting the anxiety of one item dominate.

The household example makes the lesson even plainer. A family can react to each financial risk case by case, buying protection whenever the latest scenario feels vivid and avoiding exposure whenever the latest story feels scary. That style feels responsive, but it often produces inconsistency and waste. A broader rule about savings, insurance, and acceptable exposure may look less emotionally satisfying in the moment, yet it can outperform improvisation because it resists the distortions of whichever case is currently salient. The policy is not smarter because it is colder. It is smarter because it is designed at the level where repetition and aggregation can actually be seen.

The practical correction follows directly from that shift. Before deciding the local case, ask what policy this case belongs to. Is the point to maximize the expected success of one decision, or to manage a series of decisions under one objective? What pattern of wins, losses, and tolerable failures does the broader strategy allow? Once those questions are answered, the isolated choice stops pretending it is the whole story. That does not mean blindly obeying a rule. It means making the exception earn its case against the policy instead of letting every vivid case rewrite the policy from scratch.

That distinction changes the emotional tone of decision-making. A single bad outcome no longer has to justify the whole policy, and a single tempting success no longer gets to rewrite it either. The policy becomes the level where regret, ambition, and acceptable failure are decided in advance rather than improvised after each vivid case.

The chapter also keeps an important limit in view. Policies can become stale. Environments change, portfolios drift, and a rule that once made sense can become lazy habit. Kahneman is not arguing for rigid obedience. He is arguing against episodic improvisation masquerading as wisdom. A good policy is revised at the level of the policy, not silently abandoned one emotionally persuasive case at a time. That distinction protects both discipline and adaptability.

In practice, that means the right question is often not "Do I like this case?" but "Does this case belong inside the rule I already claim to follow?"

This is why the chapter belongs before Keeping Score. Risk Policies asks the reader to widen the frame from isolated choices to broader sets of decisions. Keeping Score then shows that even once the frame has widened, people still keep separate mental ledgers inside it. Policy can be broad while accounting stays fragmented. The next chapter explains that tension by showing how people mentally bracket gains, losses, costs, and offsets instead of naturally integrating them into one total.

The wider frame is not a luxury here. It is the condition for consistent judgment.
Without it, every vivid case gets a vote larger than it deserves.
That is the avoidable error.
