# Anchors

Maya expects the negotiation to begin once the vendor explains the work. Instead, the first thing she hears is a number. The vendor says the redesign will cost forty thousand dollars, then starts listing reasons why that figure is reasonable. Maya knows she still has to judge the scope, the alternatives, and the actual value of the project, but the room is already behaving as if the conversation has a center. Every later number will feel like a move away from that first one. That is the pressure Kahneman isolates in Anchors. An initial value does not merely arrive early. It helps define what later estimates feel high, low, bold, or fair.

That is why the chapter matters. People like to believe that once they start thinking, they are correcting freely from the evidence in front of them. Kahneman's point is harsher and more useful. A starting number can pull later judgment even when it should have no authority at all. Priya sees the same structure in school when an early grade estimate starts shaping how she reads every later assignment. Jonah sees it when a used bike is posted at an ambitious asking price and suddenly every lower price still feels expensive instead of ordinary. The mistake is not that people stop thinking. The mistake is that thinking starts from a frame that already bends the field.

Anchoring works because the starting number does more than suggest an answer. It changes what counts as a plausible answer. Once Maya hears forty thousand, twenty-eight can feel like a sharp correction and thirty-two can feel moderate, even before anyone has rebuilt the estimate from the actual work. Priya hears a teacher guess that the final grade will probably land near a B and starts reading later scores through that expectation. Jonah sees a used bike posted for nine hundred dollars and begins bargaining within the seller's frame instead of asking what similar bikes actually sell for. The first number supplies orientation, and orientation can feel a lot like information.

That is also why the error feels sensible from the inside. A person under anchoring pressure does not think, I am being manipulated by sequence. The person thinks, I am moving toward a realistic answer. Adjustments from the anchor can feel thoughtful, balanced, and independent even when they remain too close to the first number. In practice, the mind often treats adjustment as proof of independence. If Maya moved from forty to thirty-two, it feels as if she resisted the frame. Kahneman's warning is that resistance can still remain trapped inside the frame it is resisting.

The chapter becomes especially concrete in negotiations because the opening number is so visible there. A vendor's first price sets the tone for a whole exchange. The first offer does not only state preference. It tells both sides what region of the map to take seriously. That is why experienced negotiators care about who speaks first, how aggressively, and with what comparables in view. But the mechanism is not confined to bargaining. It shows up in forecasts, grading, hiring, pricing, planning, and personal decisions. The anchor can be a number in the room, an asking price on a listing, or a guess that arrived before the real estimate did.

The practical correction is simple to say and harder to perform. Generate an estimate from scratch or use a second reference point before accepting the first number's pull. If Maya builds the project estimate from pieces before discussing the vendor's opening price, the anchor loses some of its leverage. If Priya looks at the grading weights and computes what later scores could actually do, the early classroom estimate stops feeling like destiny. If Jonah checks comparable bike listings before negotiating, the posted price has to compete with a real outside frame. The point is not to eliminate all influence from sequence. The point is to stop the first number from posing as neutral ground.

Kahneman also leaves an important limit in place. Some first numbers contain real information. A seller may know the market better than the buyer. A teacher's estimate may reflect patterns the student has not yet seen. A posted price may capture actual scarcity. The correction is therefore not to distrust every opening figure on principle. The task is to separate informative anchors from irrelevant ones. Better judgment depends on asking whether the starting value deserves authority or merely has timing on its side.

That distinction matters because anchoring can survive even when people know about it. A team can laugh at a high opening estimate and still negotiate too close to it. A student can tell herself that an early grade guess is only a guess and still experience later scores through that lens. Awareness helps, but only if it changes process. That is why the chapter keeps pushing toward a concrete discipline rather than a general attitude. Build an estimate independently. Bring in a second frame. Ask what answer you would have reached if a different number had appeared first.

Read that way, Anchors is not a narrow negotiation trick. It is a chapter about how judgment inherits its starting conditions. The Law of Small Numbers showed how too little evidence can gain too much authority. Anchors shows how the first available number can do the same thing to later estimation. The bridge to The Science of Availability is direct. Once the mind is already being steered by what arrived first, it is ready to be steered by what comes to mind most easily. That is the larger use of the chapter. It teaches the reader to inspect the frame before trusting the adjustment.
