# Tom W's Specialty

Priya is helping sort applications for a student research program when one profile starts attracting unusual confidence. The candidate sounds exactly like the kind of person people imagine for the most technical track. The hobbies fit. The tone of the essay fits. The self-description sounds analytical and inward. Soon the room is talking as if the applicant's likely specialty has already been revealed. What is missing is the prior distribution. Most applicants do not come from that track, and the program itself has far more people headed elsewhere. Kahneman uses Tom W's Specialty to show how representativeness can overpower base rates. A detailed profile feels so diagnostic that it hides the statistical background that should still anchor the judgment.

The force of the chapter comes from how reasonable the mistake appears from the inside. The description is not meaningless. It does carry information. That is why people trust it. They are not choosing between a vivid lie and a dry truth. They are choosing between a compelling fragment and a broader frame that feels less immediate. Once the fragment is vivid enough, the mind starts treating it as sufficient. The fit between the story and the stereotype becomes the whole case. That is why description quality can crowd out the prior odds that should anchor inference. The mind does not announce that it has dropped the base rates. It simply stops feeling their absence.

The hiring example makes the danger concrete. A resume looks exactly like the stereotype of a brilliant but isolated coder, and a team starts acting as if this applicant must come from a narrow technical pool. But if most candidates actually come from more general backgrounds, then the prior odds still matter. The descriptive fit cannot erase them. A profile that sounds diagnostic may still be less informative than it feels. The same structure appears when someone guesses a stranger's profession from clothes, speech patterns, or hobbies. The guess feels sharp because the profile is easy to imagine. It feels like evidence because the image is internally coherent. What it lacks is a serious comparison with the actual distribution of possibilities.

Kahneman's example with Tom W matters because it exposes that confusion without needing extreme stakes. A person reads a personality sketch and starts feeling that one academic field is obvious. The sketch sounds orderly, self-contained, and familiar. The category fit becomes a source of confidence. Yet the judgment is still incomplete if the observer has ignored how common the other possibilities are. This is not a complaint about intuition in general. It is a complaint about one specific shortcut. The profile is being asked to do too much work. It supplies a stereotype match, then quietly begins impersonating a probability estimate. The better question is not whether the description feels right. It is whether the description has enough weight to override the class frequencies around it.

The pull of representativeness is strong because base rates are hard to picture and profiles are easy to narrate. A frequency statement about how many people major in one field or work in one profession feels cold and abstract. A character sketch feels human. It has texture, and texture creates confidence. That is why intelligent people still get trapped by this chapter. They are not failing to understand statistics in the abstract. They are letting a vivid story occupy the whole stage. Once that happens, the background distribution begins to feel like a technicality rather than part of the evidence. The chapter's discipline is to make the background visible again before the story locks in the verdict.

That is why the practical correction has to be explicit. Bring the base rates back into the judgment before letting the vivid profile carry the case. Ask what the relevant comparison class is. Ask how often this kind of person actually appears in each category under consideration. Ask whether the detail in the profile is genuinely diagnostic or merely familiar. Those checks do not kill judgment. They discipline it. A vivid profile can still matter, but it stops being allowed to monopolize the conclusion. Once the base rates are visible again, the description moves from dictator to evidence source, which is the role it should have held from the start.

The chapter also keeps its own boundary condition in place. Descriptions do carry information, and base rates are not a magic replacement for all individual detail. If the profile contains evidence that is strongly tied to one category, then the background frequencies should not be treated as the whole story either. Kahneman's point is not that statistical context always wins. It is that statistical context cannot be silently deleted just because a stereotype fit feels strong. Overcorrecting into pure base-rate thinking would be another mistake. The real discipline is joint reasoning, not the victory of one cue over all others.

That is harder than it sounds because the cleaner story usually arrives before the cleaner comparison.
