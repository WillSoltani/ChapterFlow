# Expert Intuition: When Can We Trust It?

Jonah listens to someone make a confident market call and notices how quickly confidence gets mistaken for expertise. The speaker sounds certain, the story is fluent, and the forecast is memorable. Kahneman uses Expert Intuition: When Can We Trust It? to draw a sharper line than people usually expect. Genuine expert intuition exists, but only under specific conditions. The environment has to be regular enough to contain learnable patterns, and the expert has to receive repeated, high-quality feedback. Without those conditions, what looks like intuition may be only confidence living inside a noisy domain.

This chapter matters because people want to trust skill wherever conviction appears. A confident investor, consultant, or manager sounds like someone who has learned the field deeply. But stable learning is not available in every environment. Some domains are too chaotic, too slow in feedback, or too ambiguous for intuition to calibrate itself well. In those places, experience can accumulate without becoming true expertise. The person feels seasoned because they have seen many cases, but the cases may not teach the same lesson clearly enough to deserve strong intuitive trust.

The contrast with a coach or tutor helps. A coach who repeatedly sees the same play pattern, gives feedback, and observes the result may genuinely learn to spot trouble quickly. A tutor who sees the same student mistake hundreds of times and watches which correction works may develop real intuition. The feedback is fast enough, clear enough, and repeated enough to tune the pattern recognition. In those environments, intuition can become intelligent because the world keeps teaching the same lesson with enough reliability. Kahneman is not hostile to expertise. He is asking what kind of world is capable of producing it.

That is why the market-call example is so useful. Financial forecasting, political punditry, and other noisy domains often reward confidence while starving the learner of clean feedback. Outcomes arrive late, contain many interacting causes, and can be read in self-serving ways afterward. The forecaster may believe they are learning, but the environment may not be stable enough to teach what they think it is teaching. This is where the chapter bites. People trust intuition in chaotic domains where the conditions for valid intuition never really existed. Experience then becomes a costume for expertise instead of evidence of it.

The practical correction is diagnostic. Ask whether the environment is regular and whether the expert has had enough clear feedback to learn it. Ask whether the patterns recur often enough to be internalized. Ask whether misses are visible or whether they can be explained away indefinitely. These questions do not insult skill. They protect the idea of skill from being diluted by domains that cannot support it. Better judgment means reserving strong trust for intuition that has actually been trained by reality rather than merely accompanied by repetition and confidence.

The chapter also keeps a necessary limit in view. Genuine expertise does exist. Some firefighters, nurses, teachers, coaches, and technicians do develop rapid judgment that deserves respect. Kahneman is not flattening all intuition into guesswork. He is distinguishing intuition that has been earned from intuition that has only been rehearsed. That distinction matters because people often defend dubious expert judgment by pointing to years of experience alone. Experience is important, but experience inside a poor learning environment may teach remarkably little.

This distinction is practical because modern work is full of domains that imitate expertise better than they produce it. People sit through years of meetings, forecasts, and postmortems and begin to sound like veterans. The vocabulary of mastery grows faster than the underlying calibration. A person may be highly practiced at explaining outcomes after the fact while still being badly trained at anticipating them ahead of time. The chapter teaches the reader to separate fluency from learning. A domain that cannot deliver regular patterns and honest feedback may create charismatic experts without creating much expertise.

That is why the chapter is so useful in personal decisions too. People often call something intuition when it is really just familiarity with their own stories. Self-knowledge works best where habits generate clear, repeated consequences. If someone always feels tired after a certain routine, or reliably loses focus under a certain workload, they may genuinely learn something intuitive about themselves. But when the feedback is delayed, noisy, or easy to reinterpret, inner certainty can become just another unreliable expert voice. The same test applies inside the self as it does in the world: is the environment stable enough, and has the feedback been honest enough, to teach anything trustworthy?

This matters because people often defend bad intuition by pointing to sincerity instead of learning conditions. The forecast felt honest, the judgment felt seasoned, the expert felt calm, so others grant it more authority than it earned. Kahneman’s standard is tougher. He wants to know whether reality has been correcting the intuition often enough and clearly enough to make it worth trusting. That question is less flattering than admiration for confidence, but it is more protective of real expertise.

The bridge to the next chapter is exact. Expert Intuition: When Can We Trust It? shows when inside-view judgment has real authority and when it does not. The Outside View then offers the correction for settings where intuition lacks those conditions. When the environment cannot train expertise well, broader reference classes become more trustworthy than felt mastery.
