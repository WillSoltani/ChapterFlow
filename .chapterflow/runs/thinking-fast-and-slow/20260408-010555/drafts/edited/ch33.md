# Reversals

Priya is choosing between two fellowship applicants. Evaluated one at a time, the candidate with warmer recommendations and fewer visible flaws feels like the safer pick. When both files are placed side by side, the other candidate suddenly looks stronger because a rarer accomplishment becomes easier to compare directly. Kahneman uses Reversals to explain why rankings shift this way. Preference is not always a fixed signal waiting to be discovered. It is partly shaped by the mode of evaluation. What looks best alone may lose when compared jointly, because different features become salient under different forms of judgment.

This chapter matters because people often treat their first ranking as if it reveals a stable underlying value. The chapter shows that the ranking may depend heavily on whether options are judged separately or together. In separate evaluation, the mind leans on whatever is easiest to assess in isolation. In joint evaluation, it can compare features that are difficult to value alone but easy to order once both options are visible. The result is a reversal that feels surprising from the outside and perfectly sensible from the inside. Kahneman's point is not that people are randomly inconsistent. It is that evaluation mode changes what the mind counts as decisive.

The candidate example shows the mechanism clearly. Jonah first reviews one applicant who seems balanced and competent. That file feels strong because balance is easy to appreciate alone. Another applicant has a striking strength paired with a visible weakness and feels harder to endorse in isolation. Once the files are read jointly, the rare strength becomes easier to compare and may dominate the ranking. The judgment has not become more moral or less moral. It has become differently structured. The mind is now comparing dimensions rather than merely reacting to the overall feel of one file at a time.

The same thing happens in school and project choices. Maya may think one scholarship option is better when she reads each offer separately because one feels cleaner and less risky. When the two offers are compared side by side, a feature that was hard to price alone, such as flexibility or prestige, becomes more legible. A project proposal that looked mediocre in isolation may suddenly look superior once its strengths are contrasted directly with the alternative. The chapter matters because many real decisions move back and forth between these modes without people noticing that the mode itself is helping produce the verdict.

Consumer choice makes the point even easier to see. A purchase option with one clear defect may lose when considered alone because the defect is vivid. Put beside a competitor, the same option may win because another feature, like durability or overall value, becomes easier to compare directly. This is why pairwise comparison can feel clarifying while also being destabilizing. It exposes that some preferences were never as deep as they felt. They were supported by the ease of evaluating one object at a time rather than by a fully stable ordering of values.

That instability matters in institutional settings because many decisions move through both modes without anyone treating the shift as a substantive change. A hiring committee may screen separately and then decide jointly. A grant program may rank proposals one by one and then revisit them as a slate. A shopper may love an item online and then abandon it when standing in front of two alternatives. The chapter warns that these are not merely procedural details. The procedure can change the preference itself. Once that is admitted, better process design becomes part of better judgment, because the order and mode of comparison are no longer invisible.

The practical correction is to test important preferences under more than one evaluation mode. Look at the option alone, then side by side with the nearest alternative. Ask which feature became salient only after comparison. Ask whether the isolated judgment overweighted what was easiest to assess without a reference case. That does not guarantee one final correct ranking, but it exposes whether the current preference survives changes in the comparison frame. When a ranking flips too easily, the right lesson may be caution rather than confidence.

The chapter also keeps a useful limit in view. Some reversals do reflect genuine plural values rather than pure noise. One mode may highlight kindness, another competence. One may emphasize symmetry, another ambition. Kahneman is not claiming that all reversal is error. He is claiming that the stability people attribute to their own preferences is often exaggerated. Better judgment starts when a person admits that the mode of evaluation may be doing more work than it first appeared to do.

That admission makes people slower in a useful way. It creates room to ask whether a reversal reveals a better comparison or merely a louder one.
