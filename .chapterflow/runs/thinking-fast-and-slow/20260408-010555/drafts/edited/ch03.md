# The Lazy Controller

Chapter 3 asks a sharper question than the earlier chapters asked. It is no longer enough to know that System 2 exists or that attention is scarce. The real issue is why the supervising system so often does less checking than the reader hopes it will. Kahneman's answer is blunt: the controller is lazy. It prefers not to spend effort when an easy answer already looks usable.

That preference gives System 1 a structural edge. A suggestion arrives quickly, feels coherent, and relieves the discomfort of uncertainty. If nothing in the scene forces resistance, System 2 may accept the suggestion with only a shallow review or no meaningful review at all. The first story becomes the working judgment, not because it proved itself, but because it was simple to accept.

This is what makes bias feel natural from the inside. People often picture bad judgment as something noisy, emotional, or obviously reckless. Kahneman describes something quieter. A familiar explanation appears, the mind feels no urgency to test it, and the explanation hardens into confidence. The person experiences that whole sequence as plain good sense. The inspection standard dropped, but it dropped without announcing itself.

The practical gain of the chapter is that it explains why minds settle too soon. In planning, evaluation, estimation, and social interpretation, the first coherent answer often gets more trust than it deserves because deeper review costs effort. System 2 is capable of challenging the answer, but capability is not the same as willingness. Unless surprise, conflict, higher stakes, or deliberate caution forces a second look, the controller often conserves energy and waves the answer through.

Kahneman does not turn this into a command for permanent suspicion. The limit matters. Some effort saving is necessary, and not every low-stakes impression deserves heavy analysis. The danger begins when effort saving becomes the default even in situations where the cost of error is rising. Then "good enough to stop thinking" quietly becomes "good enough to believe."

The chapter sits exactly where it should in the book. Chapter 2 showed that deliberate thought is expensive. Chapter 3 shows that expense creates a standing temptation to conserve control. The next step is obvious. If the reviewer is so willing to accept easy stories, what machinery produces those stories so efficiently in the first place? The Associative Machine answers that question.
