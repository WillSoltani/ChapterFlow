# Norms, Surprises, and Causes

The meeting changes the second something stops making sense. Maya feels it during a service review after a customer-facing error. One event has broken what the team expected, and the room is already acting as if the job is no longer to understand the sequence but to name what caused the break. Kahneman uses that fast change in atmosphere to explain why surprise is so powerful. The mind does not like events that slip free of expectation. It wants the break tied back into a pattern, and the fastest way to do that is to find a cause.

That is the chapter’s mechanism. Surprise is not only interruption. It is the recognition that a norm has been violated. Something happened that should not have happened, or something failed to happen that should have. Once that recognition appears, the mind moves quickly toward explanation, intention, and often blame. A missed deadline starts to look like carelessness. An unusual answer in class starts to look like attitude. An unexpected message starts to look like motive. The first story that restores order begins acquiring authority almost at once.

The force of that story does not come only from evidence. It also comes from relief. Priya sees this when one classroom mistake suddenly changes how a student is being read. Jonah feels it when a text lands oddly and immediately seems to reveal what the sender “must have meant.” In both cases, the mind is trying to repair a broken expectation. Relief arrives when a cause is found, and that relief is easy to mistake for proof that the cause is correct.

That is why the chapter is useful. Kahneman is not mocking people for wanting explanations. Causal hunger is often necessary. People need to orient themselves when the world stops making immediate sense. The problem begins when the need for explanation is confused with the right to trust the first explanation that feels satisfying. A tidy account closes the gap between event and understanding, so it feels stronger than an open question. That emotional payoff often outruns the evidence.

Kahneman does not ask the reader to stop searching for causes. He asks for one disciplined pause inside that search. What norm was broken? What expectation was violated? What other explanations could still fit the same surprise? Those questions do not erase the need for order. They stop the first neat repair from hardening too quickly into certainty.

That is also why the next chapter is already waiting inside this one. Once the mind has accepted a causal story quickly, it becomes easier to treat the material already in view as enough. A Machine for Jumping to Conclusions extends exactly that pattern.
