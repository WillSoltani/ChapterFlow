# Keeping Score

Maya's department refuses to cut a struggling project line because its budget has already been approved and "belongs" to that program. From the standpoint of the company as a whole, the money is simply money. Inside the team, it lives in a separate box with its own history, expectations, and emotional force. Kahneman uses Keeping Score to explain why this happens. People do not naturally evaluate gains and losses in one clean total. They keep mental accounts. Once outcomes are placed in separate ledgers, the same overall position can feel very different depending on how it has been bracketed.

This chapter matters because it explains why equivalent totals often fail to produce equivalent choices. A rebate can feel like a windfall while an equal fee feels like an insult. A side purchase can seem harmless when charged to one mental account even though it would look foolish if integrated into the broader spending picture. A grade loss in one assignment can feel catastrophic even when the course total remains strong. Kahneman's point is not merely that people like categories. It is that the categories change valuation. The bookkeeping frame is part of the experience, not just a way of reporting it afterward.

The budget example shows the mechanism in work. Jonah argues that a project budget should be protected because the money was allocated for that use. Another manager points out that the broader company picture now makes the project a poor use of resources. Both people are talking about the same dollars, but they are not counting them inside the same account. One ledger is local and historical. The other is integrated and current. Keeping Score explains why the local view can feel morally stronger even when it is strategically weaker. Once money or effort is boxed inside a separate account, defending the box starts to feel like defending good judgment itself.

The school version is just as clear. Priya thinks of quiz points, participation credit, and one bad exam as separate emotional events. In total the course may still be healthy, but the mind does not always experience the total first. It experiences the ledgers. One loss in the exam account can overshadow several gains in the participation account. A student may chase a small symbolic recovery because the isolated account feels unfinished or painful. This is not simple confusion about arithmetic. It is a different way of organizing value, where separate bins carry their own emotional balance sheets.

Consumer choices reveal the same structure every day. A rebate can justify an extra purchase because it feels as if it belongs to a bonus account rather than to the same pool as the spending. A fee on top of a purchase can feel outrageous because it is coded as a separate loss instead of integrated into the total cost from the beginning. Side purchases, discounts, sunk costs, and credits all change emotional force depending on which ledger they land in. The chapter's point is that mental accounting is not a minor quirk around otherwise stable valuation. It is one of the systems by which valuation gets organized in the first place.

The practical correction is not to abolish every mental account. Some separate ledgers help with self-control, planning, and discipline. The useful question is whether the current ledger is helping or distorting. Should this cost be integrated with the broader total? Should this rebate really change the evaluation of the next purchase? Should this project budget be protected because of its local history, or reconsidered because the larger picture has changed? Once those questions are asked, the mind becomes less captive to the first bookkeeping frame that made the outcome feel clear.

That is why mental accounting can make reasonable people sound inconsistent across settings. They are not always changing their values. They are often moving the same outcome from one mental ledger to another, and the emotional meaning shifts with the bookkeeping. The chapter gives a cleaner explanation for those reversals than simple hypocrisy or arithmetic weakness.

The chapter also keeps a real limit in view. Integrated totals are not automatically superior in every context. People do need categories for planning and restraint. A savings account, a travel budget, or a study schedule can work better because it is partitioned. Kahneman is not mocking all mental bookkeeping. He is showing that the same tool that supports self-control can also create distortions when separate ledgers become too insulated from one another. Better judgment depends on knowing when to preserve the partition and when to dissolve it.

The correction is therefore selective. Merge the ledgers when separation is hiding the real total, and keep them separate when separation is serving a real discipline rather than an illusion.

This is why the bridge to the next chapter works so well. Risk Policies widened the frame from isolated choices to broad rules. Keeping Score shows that even inside broad rules, people keep carving experience into separate accounts. Reversals then pushes further by showing how preferences can flip when the framing or accounting changes. Once mental ledgers are visible, the next question is what happens when those ledgers produce opposite judgments over equivalent choices. The next chapter takes up that reversal directly.

Once the ledger changes, the preference often changes with it.
That is the practical sting the chapter wants the reader to notice.
It is small, but decisive.
