# Attention and Effort

Chapter 1 introduced System 1 and System 2 as the two main characters in Kahneman's story. Chapter 2 explains why the slower character cannot simply step in whenever accuracy matters. System 2 runs on attention, and attention is limited. That sounds simple, but it changes the whole picture. Careful thought is not just slower than automatic thought. It is expensive.

You can feel that expense in any task that requires sustained concentration. The mind narrows. Distraction becomes costly. Competing demands stop cooperating and start interfering with each other. Kahneman uses that felt difference to make a larger point: the same resource that supports calculation, monitoring, memory, and restraint can be overloaded. When it is, judgment quality changes.

This is why effortful tasks collide. Two automatic activities can often coexist with little trouble. Two demanding ones cannot. They draw from the same limited pool. The result is not merely inconvenience. A loaded mind becomes less available for checking, less able to resist the easy answer, and more likely to miss what would otherwise have been obvious.

That observation matters beyond laboratory tasks. People often moralize these failures too quickly. They read distraction as disrespect, slower correction as incompetence, or a missed cue as indifference. Kahneman's framing is more precise. Sometimes the problem is not motive. Sometimes the attentional budget was already thin, and the monitoring system had less to spend than the situation required.

The chapter is careful not to overclaim. Effort is not proof of truth. A person can work hard and still be wrong. The real value is diagnostic. Once you understand that deliberate thought has a cost structure, later chapters on bias and error stop sounding mysterious. Many mistakes happen not because people refused to think, but because the kind of thinking that might have corrected the problem was not fully available.

That is the bridge to The Lazy Controller. If careful review costs attention, the mind has a standing incentive to accept quick suggestions and conserve effort. The next chapter shows what that tendency looks like when the checking system does less than the reader would like to imagine.
