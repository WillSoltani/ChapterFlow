# Intuitions vs. Formulas

Maya sits in a hiring meeting where each person has a different favorite candidate and a different reason for trusting their favorite. One interviewer values energy. Another trusts polish. Another feels they can read grit from a short exchange. The more they talk, the more the discussion sounds intelligent, but not more consistent. Kahneman uses Intuitions vs. Formulas to make a sharp point: in stable prediction tasks, simple formulas often outperform expert judgment. A mechanical rule that scores the same ingredients the same way each time avoids the mood shifts, private weighting, and improvisational noise that enter when people decide case by case.

This chapter matters because discretion feels intelligent. A room that says it is using judgment sounds more sophisticated than a room following a simple rubric. But the freedom to improvise often invites instability rather than wisdom. Two people can look at the same evidence and reach different decisions because each one has a different internal formula they are not even fully aware of. Human discretion adds variation that feels insightful but often lowers accuracy. The mind experiences that variability as nuance. The record often shows it as noise.

The grading example makes the point easy to respect. A teacher using a clear rubric may end up more consistent than a teacher who grades each paper holistically according to how strong it feels in the moment. The second method may sound richer. It may even feel fairer. But if small shifts in mood, order effects, or passing impressions change the verdict, then the human judgment is doing more than reading the work. It is adding instability. A simple rule does not become wise because it is mechanical. It becomes useful because it applies the same standard each time instead of reinventing the standard under the cover of insight.

The same pattern appears in budgeting, admissions, and routine personnel decisions. Jonah may tell himself he is flexibly responding to the reality of the case when he is really changing weights from one day to the next. Priya may think a spreadsheet is too crude for a complex problem, then discover that the spreadsheet quietly outperforms her own shifting priorities over time. The chapter’s real challenge is not sentimental attachment to formulas. It is resistance to the flattering idea that private judgment is naturally superior because it feels more human. Kahneman asks the reader to separate dignity from accuracy. A more personal process is not automatically a more predictive one.

That is why the practical correction has to be procedural. Use simple rules when the domain is regular and the predictive ingredients are known. Decide in advance what variables matter, how they will be combined, and when judgment is allowed to override the rule. If overrides are constantly needed, the domain or the formula may be wrong. But if the domain is stable and the ingredients are defensible, a simple rule can do something human intuition resists doing: remain boringly consistent. That consistency is often where the predictive advantage comes from. It prevents the decision-maker from adding fresh noise each time they feel especially perceptive.

The chapter also keeps a real limit in view. Formulas only deserve trust when the environment is regular enough and the variables are sensible. A bad rule does not become good because it is applied consistently. Mechanical error is still error. Kahneman is not preaching automation for its own sake. He is showing that once the ingredients are known and the task is stable, the human tendency to embellish can hurt more than help. The virtue of the formula is not depth. It is disciplined repeatability.

This is one reason the chapter feels insulting on first contact. People hear formula and imagine a disrespectful shortcut that ignores what makes cases unique. Kahneman’s point is harder than that. The insult is not to judgment. It is to noise masquerading as judgment. When a rule quietly beats a roomful of experts, it is usually because the experts are adding unstable variation they cannot see in themselves. The formula is not magical. It is simply refusing to change its mind for reasons it cannot justify. That kind of stubbornness can be a predictive advantage when the environment rewards consistency more than flair.

The practical consequence is larger than most people expect. Once a domain has defensible ingredients and stable feedback, every discretionary override becomes a cost that has to be earned. If the override is not improving the record, it is probably protecting ego rather than improving prediction. This is why formula-like methods feel so threatening. They expose how much of professional identity is tied to the performance of personal judgment. Kahneman is not asking the reader to humiliate experts. He is asking them to compare methods honestly. If the simpler process wins, sophistication should not be allowed to veto it just because the winning method feels less prestigious.

The chapter therefore asks for a kind of procedural humility. If a rule is transparent, stable, and at least as accurate as the expert, then the burden shifts to the expert to justify every departure from it. That reversal is uncomfortable because it makes intuition answer to evidence rather than the other way around. But it is also what protects decisions from becoming theater. Once the reader sees that, formulas stop looking cold and start looking like a defense against avoidable self-deception.

The bridge to the next chapter is exact. Intuitions vs. Formulas shows why people should be less impressed by discretionary judgment in stable tasks. Expert Intuition: When Can We Trust It? then asks the natural follow-up question. If formulas can beat intuition in many settings, when does intuition actually deserve confidence? The answer depends on the structure of the environment and the quality of the feedback that trained the expert.
