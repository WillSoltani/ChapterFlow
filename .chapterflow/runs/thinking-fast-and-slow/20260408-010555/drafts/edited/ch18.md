# Taming Intuitive Predictions

Maya leaves an interview convinced that a candidate is going to be a star. The conversation was sharp, the energy was high, and the candidate handled pressure well. The forecast that forms in Maya's head is not modest. It runs all the way to top-tier performance. Kahneman uses Taming Intuitive Predictions to show why that kind of forecast needs discipline. Case-specific information can matter, but intuitive predictions usually stay too extreme because they fail to shrink back toward what typically happens. Better forecasting starts with the base rate, then adjusts for the signal in the case without forgetting regression. In other words, strong evidence does not justify pretending that ordinary outcomes have stopped existing.

This chapter is the practical partner to Regression to the Mean. The previous chapter shows that extreme observations often move back toward the average. This chapter asks what to do with that fact when a forecast has to be made anyway. The answer is not to suppress judgment entirely. It is to separate stable signal from noise and then pull the prediction back toward the norm by an amount that matches the quality of the evidence. An interview, one standout assignment, or one impressive week may contain real information. The mistake is to let that information dictate an equally extreme forecast as if the case had erased the background distribution.

The semester example makes the logic clear. Priya sees one exceptional assignment and starts forecasting a spectacular term. The assignment may indeed show talent, preparation, or better fit with the material. But the next step is not to project the same level straight across the semester. The next step is to ask what usually happens in courses like this, how stable performance has been over time, and how much one assignment really predicts later work. Intuition wants the vivid performance to produce a vivid prediction. Kahneman's correction is that prediction should be more conservative than impression. The observation can be extreme while the forecast should be moderated.

The same thing happens in personal life. Jonah watches a friend succeed dramatically once and starts talking as if the next success will be equally dramatic. The story feels generous and supportive, but it may still be a bad forecast. One strong event can reveal something important without proving that future results will sit at the same extreme. This is what the chapter means by taming intuition rather than silencing it. Intuition notices the signal. Judgment then has to ask how much weight that signal deserves once ordinary variation and regression are put back into the picture.

That is why the practical move has to be procedural. Start with the base rate, then adjust for the case-specific information without forgetting regression. Ask what the average outcome looks like in comparable cases. Ask how predictive the observed feature really is. Ask whether the current impression is trying to turn correlation into certainty. These questions do not make forecasting mechanical in a dead way. They make it proportionate. The aim is not to deny standout evidence. The aim is to prevent standout evidence from generating a forecast that is as extreme as the evidence feels.

The chapter also clarifies a limit that matters. Unique facts do matter. Sometimes a case really does contain stronger predictive information than the average base-rate estimate. Kahneman is not telling the reader to flatten every forecast into mediocrity. He is saying that intuition rarely shrinks enough on its own. People tend to move too far from the norm because a compelling case feels like full permission for a compelling prediction. The better discipline is to move part of the way, not all of the way. That is often what realistic forecasting sounds like: less thrilling than intuition, but more defensible.

That restraint matters because people often confuse confidence with sharpness. A forecast that sounds bold feels more useful than a forecast that stays partly anchored to the ordinary case. But realistic prediction often sounds modest. It gives the signal real credit while refusing to pretend that one interview, one project, or one dramatic week has dissolved the background statistics. The chapter teaches the reader to tolerate that modesty. A disciplined forecast is not weaker because it is less extreme. It is stronger because it has priced in how noisy real life usually is.

That lesson is useful in hiring, grading, friendships, and any decision where one strong impression is trying to impersonate a long-run pattern.
It also explains why disciplined forecasts often sound less impressive than intuitive ones. A forecast that admits uncertainty, shrinkage, and ordinary outcomes can feel timid in a room that wants conviction. Kahneman's point is that realism and drama are different virtues. The better prediction often sounds quieter because it has not confused vivid evidence with unlimited predictive reach.
That quieter forecast is often the one that survives contact with the broader pattern.
It is also the one least likely to embarrass the forecaster later.
It earns trust by staying proportional.

The bridge to the next chapter is precise. Taming Intuitive Predictions gives the reader a more disciplined forecast rule. The Illusion of Understanding then shows why disciplined forecasting is so rare. Once people are attached to strong stories about what happened, they become attached to strong stories about what will happen next. This chapter offers a restraint on that impulse by making base rates and regression part of every serious prediction.
