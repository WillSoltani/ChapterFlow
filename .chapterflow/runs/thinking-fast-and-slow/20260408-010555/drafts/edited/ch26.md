# Prospect Theory

Priya is choosing between two internship offers. One is described as a guaranteed stipend with a small performance bonus. The other is described as a higher target payout, but part of it will be withheld if certain goals are missed. The expected numbers are close enough that a spreadsheet does not settle the choice. What changes her reaction is the frame. The first feels like a gain with upside. The second feels like a possible loss from money she has started to count as hers. Kahneman uses Prospect Theory to explain why this happens. People do not value outcomes on one smooth scale detached from context. They evaluate gains and losses around a reference point, and losses usually carry more force than equivalent gains.

That is what makes the chapter more than an extension of Bernoulli's Errors. The previous chapter established that final wealth alone cannot explain felt value because outcomes are judged relative to a baseline. Prospect Theory adds a sharper claim: the value curve is not balanced around that baseline. The psychological distance between losing and gaining is uneven. A modest gain can feel pleasant, but a similar loss can feel disproportionately painful. This is why choices under risk change so dramatically when the same stakes are framed in terms of keeping what one has versus risking a shortfall. Expected utility offers a cleaner mathematical surface than actual judgment usually follows.

The work example makes the point concrete. Jonah is offered an incentive plan that promises extra pay if the quarter goes well. He is cautious but interested. The plan is then reframed so that part of his usual bonus is considered provisionally his and will be removed if performance slips. The financial possibilities may remain close, yet the emotional response changes immediately. In the first frame he imagines gains. In the second he imagines losses. Prospect Theory says that shift is not accidental decoration around a stable preference. It is part of how preference is formed. The reference point determines what counts as being ahead or behind, and the asymmetry between gains and losses changes the appetite for risk.

The school version is just as revealing. Maya is deciding whether to keep a solid grade or gamble on a difficult extra-credit project. If she already feels safely above the target she may protect the sure outcome. If she feels below the line she may suddenly welcome a risky option she would otherwise reject. The objective structure of the gamble has not changed much, but the location of the reference point has. What counts as a loss or a chance to recover matters more than an abstract description of expected value. This is why the chapter has such practical force. It explains why people can look inconsistent across situations that are formally similar. They are not reading only the numbers. They are reading the numbers through a frame of gain and loss.

Certainty matters here too, which is why the chapter extends beyond a simple point about dislike of losses. A sure gain and a probable gain are not weighed in the same emotional register. A sure loss and a probable loss are not either. Kahneman's larger point is that the shape of value changes with framing, certainty, and position relative to the reference point. That is what classical expected utility smooths away. The model can still be elegant, but elegance is not the same thing as descriptive accuracy. Prospect Theory earns its place by explaining choices that otherwise look erratic, especially when people become cautious in the domain of gains and desperate in the domain of losses.

The practical correction is not to sneer at these reactions as irrational noise. It is to surface the frame before obeying it. What is the current reference point? Which option is being experienced as a loss rather than a neutral alternative? Is the preference being driven by the actual stakes or by how those stakes were presented? These questions help when evaluating incentive plans, insurance choices, grading gambles, or negotiations. They do not guarantee one right answer, but they stop a framed preference from disguising itself as timeless judgment. Once the frame is named, a person can decide whether they still endorse the preference after the emotional asymmetry has been exposed.

The chapter also keeps a necessary limit in view. Prospect Theory is powerful because it captures recurring structure in ordinary choice, not because it explains every motive people have. Duty, identity, loyalty, and long-term plans can all shape decisions too. Kahneman is not claiming that all behavior reduces to one curve of gain and loss. He is claiming that any account of real judgment is incomplete if it ignores reference dependence and the greater sting of losses. That is enough to revise how many decisions are understood, especially decisions involving risk, incentive design, and fairness.

This chapter therefore changes the reader's standard for evaluating a choice. It is no longer enough to ask what the objective outcomes are in the abstract. One must ask how the outcomes are framed, what baseline is active, and whether the person feels they are protecting gains or escaping losses. That does not make objective analysis irrelevant. It makes objective analysis harder and more honest. A clean model that misses the live psychology of the decision is not yet a full explanation. Prospect Theory supplies much of what the cleaner model left out.

The bridge to the next chapter is direct. Bernoulli's Errors established the importance of reference points. Prospect Theory shows that the value response around those points is asymmetric and framing-sensitive. The Endowment Effect then brings that logic even closer to everyday possession and exchange. If losses loom larger than gains, giving something up should feel worse than acquiring the same thing feels good. The next chapter turns that implication into a concrete pattern people encounter constantly.
