# Linda: Less Is More

Maya is listening to two forecasts about a product launch. The first says adoption will probably be uneven, with several different outcomes still possible. The second says adoption will be strong among early users, the press will love the design angle, and the product will become a case study in premium positioning. The second forecast feels sharper, more intelligent, and more persuasive. It also covers fewer possible futures. Kahneman uses Linda: Less Is More to show how that mistake happens. A richer story can feel more probable than a broader truth because detail raises plausibility even while it lowers actual probability. The mind rewards coherence and specificity, then mistakes that reward for evidence about likelihood.

The force of the chapter comes from how hard the error is to feel from the inside. The detailed option does not seem foolish. It seems informed. It looks like the forecaster has thought harder, noticed more, and earned more confidence. But the added conditions make the claim narrower, not wider. Every extra requirement has to come true for the whole story to come true. The broader statement needs less from the world. That is why the chapter matters. The more vivid option can feel likelier even when it is logically smaller. The structure of the mistake is simple, but the emotional pull is strong because people like stories that seem complete.

The title's lesson becomes practical the moment the reader notices how often detail is mistaken for probability. A multiple-choice answer on a test can sound right because it includes extra explanation. A motive story in a personal conflict can feel truer because it ties several details together. A market prediction with more moving parts can seem more realistic because it sounds like real life. In each case, the same pressure appears. A subset starts borrowing credibility from the richer picture it provides. The judgment no longer asks which option covers more possibilities. It asks which option feels like the better story.

That is why Linda matters as more than a classroom logic puzzle. It reveals a habit of mind that turns coherence into confidence. Once a narrative feels textured, the mind starts treating narrowness as sophistication. The details appear to rescue the judgment from vagueness, but they may really be loading extra conditions onto it. The chapter therefore belongs next to Tom W's Specialty. In the previous chapter, a profile felt diagnostic because it matched a stereotype. Here, an option feels probable because it contains more story. Both chapters show the same appetite for fit. The mind prefers the option that sounds more like a world it can picture, even when the pictured world is mathematically less likely.

That is also why the conjunction fallacy survives outside formal puzzles. Everyday language trains people to reward the answer that sounds more complete, not the answer that makes the weakest commitment. When two options are presented side by side, the narrower one can feel as if it includes the broader one plus added insight. In reality it includes the broader one plus added demands on reality. The mind hears added detail as added understanding, and understanding feels like probability. That is the psychological bridge Kahneman is exposing. A logically smaller claim gets promoted because it leaves the listener with a stronger feeling of explanation.

The practical correction has to be explicit because intuition will not offer it on its own. Check whether one option is a subset of another before trusting the more detailed story. Ask what extra condition the vivid answer is adding. Ask whether the narrow option can only be true inside the broader option. Once that structure is visible, the spell weakens. The reader can still prefer detail for explanation, but not confuse explanation with probability mass. Better judgment does not mean becoming hostile to nuance. It means keeping logical inclusion in view while the mind is being seduced by the more cinematic option.

This is also where the chapter's limit matters. Detail is not useless. Added information can improve understanding, distinguish cases, and guide action. The problem begins only when detail is allowed to impersonate likelihood. A richer account can be more useful and still be less probable. Those two facts can coexist. Kahneman's real demand is that the reader stop treating them as if they rise and fall together. Otherwise people reward the answer that feels smartest instead of the answer that makes the fewest claims about the world.

That distinction matters in work, school, and personal life because the polished answer often wins the room first. A forecast with more clauses sounds seasoned. A relationship explanation with more motives sounds insightful. A study answer with more detail sounds better prepared. None of those reactions are crazy. They become errors only when the added texture is treated as if it expanded the odds instead of narrowing them.

The chapter gives the reader a clean way to resist that pull without pretending that elegance and detail have no value at all.
It is a logic check built for moments when narrative polish is starting to outrun disciplined judgment.

The bridge to the next chapter is precise. Linda: Less Is More shows how a satisfying story can outrun logic. Causes Trump Statistics shows what happens when that same hunger for explanation starts dominating the way people weigh evidence. Once narrative fit already feels trustworthy, it becomes even easier for a causal story to push clean statistical reasoning out of the room.
