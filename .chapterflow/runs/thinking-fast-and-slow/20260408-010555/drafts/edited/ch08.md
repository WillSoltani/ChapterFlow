# How Judgments Happen

Maya sits in a forecast meeting where the official question is about probability, but the conversation keeps drifting toward conviction. People talk about how strong the plan feels, how much they like the team, and how persuasive the presentation sounded. Kahneman uses How Judgments Happen to explain what is going on. When formal calculation is hard, the mind borrows accessible impressions. Hard judgments are often converted into simpler impressions or intensity matches, and the easier answer gets returned as if it addressed the harder question.

That is the chapter's core warning. The problem is not only speed. It is substitution. A difficult judgment starts wearing the clothes of an easier one. The answer fits the easier impression rather than the original question. Maya's probability discussion turns into a conversation about gut strength. Priya sees an essay judged by one vivid feature instead of the full rubric. Jonah notices how easy it is to rate an entire week by the mood of the present moment. In each case, the mind has answered something real, but not necessarily the thing that needed answering.

The substitution stays hidden because the easier question is usually close enough to the harder one to feel appropriate. People do not think, I am avoiding the real task. They think, I have a reaction, and the reaction must be relevant. If the original question is abstract, numerical, or effortful, an accessible impression arrives faster. The speed of that impression then gets misread as evidence that it belongs. What feels clear is not always what is most diagnostic.

That is why the chapter becomes useful in practice. The right correction is simple to state and difficult to maintain: state the exact question and ask whether the answer was built from a substitute dimension. In Maya's meeting, that means distinguishing likelihood from enthusiasm. In school, it means distinguishing rubric performance from vividness or charm. In personal life, it means separating a momentary mood from a broader evaluation of how life is going. The gain is not perfect calculation. The gain is noticing when the mind has quietly changed the assignment.

The chapter also exposes why substitution is so hard to catch in real time. The easier question is often not absurd. It is adjacent. Confidence can look relevant to probability. Style can look relevant to quality. Current mood can look relevant to a broader life rating. Because the surrogate resembles the target, the mind gets the emotional reward of answering without paying the full cognitive price of judging. That is why the answer can feel so persuasive. It is not random. It is merely misaligned.

That misalignment matters most when the substitute dimension is easier precisely because it is less demanding. A forecast discussion built around how convincing a presenter feels may miss base rates, outside cases, or unresolved operational constraints. An essay judgment built around one memorable sentence may miss structure, evidence, or consistency. A life judgment built around today's mood may erase the actual pattern of the week. Kahneman is helping the reader see that the problem is not only mental laziness. It is task drift. The mind quietly changes the question in order to keep moving.

That drift can become institutional just as easily as it becomes personal. Teams often start with one question on the slide and answer a different one in the room. Classrooms can announce a rubric and still reward the feature that is easiest to notice. People can say they are evaluating a week, a relationship, or a plan when they are really reporting the intensity of the present reaction. The chapter matters because naming that drift gives the reader a way to interrupt it before the surrogate answer becomes official.

That is the moment when a private shortcut becomes a shared error.

It also explains why some corrections feel strangely effortful. To repair the judgment, the reader has to recover the original question and hold onto it long enough for the easier answer to lose some of its grip. That is uncomfortable because the substitute usually arrived first and already feels fluent. Kahneman is showing that better judgment often begins with that recovery step. Before calculating more, the reader has to restore the right target.

That restoration takes discipline.

This chapter also extends the sequence cleanly. A Machine for Jumping to Conclusions showed how a thin slice can feel like a full case. How Judgments Happen shows how the judgment itself often gets assembled from whatever dimension is easiest to access. The mind is not only filling gaps too quickly. It is often answering with the wrong kind of material.

Kahneman also keeps the limit in view. Shortcuts help under pressure, but they mislead when the borrowed impression is too loose for the task. Everyday judgment depends on approximation, and the chapter breaks if it becomes a demand for permanent formality. The real issue is mismatch. When the stakes are high and the substitute dimension is a poor fit, the answer can feel persuasive while still missing the point.

That is what makes the bridge forward feel inevitable. Once the reader sees that judgments are often built from easier surrogates, the next chapter can ask the obvious question directly. Answering an Easier Question names the move that this chapter makes visible. The durable lesson here is to check whether the answer belongs to the question before treating it as insight.
