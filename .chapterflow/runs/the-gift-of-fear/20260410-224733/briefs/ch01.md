Core Claim:
Fear is useful when it is tied to a real signal in the environment rather than to imagination or memory. The opening chapter establishes this through Kelly's assault case, where discomfort appears early but is repeatedly overridden by politeness and social pressure. The chapter argues that survival often depends less on collecting proof than on respecting credible signals before the full explanation arrives. Its deepest lesson is that social conditioning can be more dangerous than uncertainty when danger is already present.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
This chapter is impossible to confuse with a later threat-assessment chapter because it is not yet a catalog of indicators. It is a foundational argument about why a person should trust a real warning signal before a situation becomes legible enough to satisfy social standards of proof.

Author Logic Chain:
First the author places the reader inside a concrete assault setup where danger is felt before it is explained.
Then the author shows how politeness, denial, and the wish to seem reasonable keep overriding that signal.
Then the author concludes that fear can be life-preserving when it is treated as information instead of embarrassment.

Frameworks or Terms Introduced:
- true fear versus worry: one is tied to a present signal, the other to imagination or memory
- social override: politeness and the wish to seem reasonable can block protective action
- signal before proof: a survival-relevant cue can arrive before a neat explanation

Specific Applications:
- leaving earlier when a person keeps pressing past a refusal
- treating repeated insistence as data rather than kindness by default
- letting safety outrank the wish to avoid seeming rude

Common Misreadings:
- that the lesson is to become suspicious of everyone
- that fear needs courtroom-level proof before it should affect action

Counterarguments or Limits:
The chapter is not a license for generalized anxiety or social paranoia. It works only when fear is tied to real cues in the environment. Without that distinction, the idea collapses into constant worry, which the author explicitly rejects.
