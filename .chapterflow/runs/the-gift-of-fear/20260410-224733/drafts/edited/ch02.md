You walk into a room and something feels off before you can say why. Most people know that moment, then immediately begin arguing with it. Maybe nothing is wrong. Maybe you are being unfair. Maybe you are tired, touchy, dramatic. The feeling lands first. The cleanup crew of explanations arrives a second later. This chapter lives in that gap.

De Becker does not treat intuition as magic. The title points the other way. A technology is a process, something usable, something that works for reasons even when those reasons are not fully verbal yet. Intuition matters here because it is not random. It is fast pattern recognition. It notices a mismatch, a boundary test, a pressure shift, or a detail that does not fit before the conscious mind has organized the pieces into a neat report.

That becomes practical once the real interference is visible. The problem is not perception. The problem is judgment arriving too early and demanding a polished case before it grants perception any authority. A person notices strain in a hallway, a refusal being pushed past, or behavior that does not match the story, then starts cross-examining themselves instead of the room. Social training helps that mistake along. People are rewarded for looking fair, calm, and non-dramatic. They are not often rewarded for leaving because a pattern is starting to form.

That is why the chapter's core distinction matters so much. Judgment is useful when it tests a signal honestly. It becomes dangerous when it tries to erase the signal before the signal has even been named. Intuition does not ask you to worship a feeling. It asks you to identify what the feeling noticed. What changed in the room? What cue pulled your attention before your mind started building excuses? The chapter becomes disciplined the moment it moves from \"I have a bad feeling\" to \"he ignored my first no\" or \"the story and the behavior stopped matching.\"

That move also keeps intuition from drifting into mysticism. A vague sense of certainty can sound impressive and still be useless. A narrow cue is much better. It gives the mind something real to work with. It lets action stay proportionate. A person can leave, slow down, ask another question, or refuse access without pretending to possess supernatural insight. Intuition is strongest when it can eventually point back to something observable, even if the observation came faster than language.

The same boundary from the opening chapter stays active here. True fear is tied to something present in the environment or circumstance. Worry is tied to imagination or memory. That difference keeps intuition from becoming a glamorous name for anxiety. If every strong feeling counts as wisdom, perception gets drowned in noise. If every early signal is dismissed until it becomes fully explainable, perception gets silenced before it can help. The usable middle is narrower: notice the cue, name the cue, act on the cue.

This chapter is not anti-reason. It is anti-premature rationalization. Reason still matters, but only after perception has been allowed to speak. Good judgment does not begin by mocking the signal. It begins by asking what the signal is attached to. Once that is clearer, reason can help size the response. What it must not do is demand impossible certainty while the pattern is still early and the room is still moving.

Seen this way, intuition is less romantic and more reliable. It is the mind doing quick work with real fragments before the formal explanation is ready. The next chapter can widen that into prediction only because this one strips intuition of mystique, strips worry of authority, and leaves one disciplined habit behind: when something feels wrong, find the cue before you start defending the feeling against yourself.
