Stalking risk can be underread because politeness changes what people think a rejection means. A refusal that is softened to avoid embarrassment, anger, or awkwardness may sound socially graceful, yet it can leave enough room for a boundary-ignoring pursuer to keep claiming hope. This chapter is about that gap. It does not say kindness causes stalking. It says unwanted pursuit can exploit ambiguity and keep treating courtesy as continued access.

That matters because persistence still outranks romance story here. Repeated messages after refusal, repeated appearances, repeated efforts to reopen contact, and repeated reinterpretation of a clear boundary do not become harmless just because the pursuer sounds sincere or wounded. The flattering story may describe longing, confusion, or affection. The behavior still shows refusal not being accepted.

This is why soft rejection needs limits. A gentle letdown may feel safer in the moment, but if the person on the other side is already pushing past boundaries, politeness can become material for more pursuit. The chapter is not blaming the person who is trying to stay safe, kind, or calm. It is warning that ambiguity can be turned into a false invitation by someone who has already decided not to hear no clearly.

The absence of an explicit threat does not settle the read either. People often wait for openly frightening language before they call something dangerous. Yet repeated contact, repeated appearances, and repeated reinterpretation can already show a serious boundary problem. The chapter pushes the reader away from asking whether the pursuer still sounds romantic and back toward asking what the pattern keeps doing.

Outside observers can fail here for familiar reasons. Friends, counselors, or bystanders may praise the target for being nice, or they may tell a reassuring story about someone who is only trying harder because he cares. That response can underread persistence badly. Once refusal has been given, the stronger question is whether contact keeps returning anyway and whether each contact act keeps trying to reopen access.

The chapter still needs a limit. Not every awkward follow-up means the same level of risk, and the book should not pretend every clumsy pursuit belongs in one fixed category. The point is narrower than that. When persistence, reinterpretation, and access-seeking continue after rejection, politeness should not be allowed to hide what the pattern is becoming.

That harder read prepares the next move in the book. Adults can miss danger when social scripts soften warning. The same problem becomes even more charged when the subject shifts to children, fear, and early signs of violence.
