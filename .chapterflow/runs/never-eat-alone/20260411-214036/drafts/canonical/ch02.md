The hidden ledger is one of the fastest ways to ruin a relationship while still telling yourself you are being generous. You help, you introduce, you show up, and somewhere in the background a quiet arithmetic begins. Maybe you never say it out loud. Maybe the other person never knows. But once the bond starts carrying private scorekeeping, every disappointment gets heavier. That is the tension Ferrazzi moves toward with "Don't Keep Score."

The chapter's power comes from refusing an easy split. It does not say generosity is pure and ambition is dirty. It says the relationship changes quality when help is offered as contribution instead of as a disguised invoice. That is why the official framing matters so much. It is not simply about getting what you want. It is also about helping other people get what they want. The sentence matters because it widens the direction of the bond.

That widening changes how networking works. In a ledger mindset, every favor is a chip and every conversation is future leverage. In a contribution mindset, help builds trust, memory, and goodwill before any obvious return shows up. The chapter does not ask the reader to become passive. It asks the reader to stop shrinking every interaction into a deal.

This is where small acts matter. Ferrazzi's practical point, as the frozen secondary support makes clear, is not that generosity has to be grand. It is often the repeated, useful, low-drama kind of help that changes a relationship. A thoughtful introduction. A useful article. A fast answer. A remembered detail. Small help matters because it proves the bond is alive outside moments of crisis.

The chapter also pushes against selective generosity. If you help only people who already look important, the pattern becomes obvious fast. Broad generosity is stronger because influence changes over time and because today's low-status contact may matter much more later. More important, broad generosity keeps you from turning kindness into status calculation. It protects the relationship from the smell of opportunism.

Still, the chapter weakens if it drifts into naive giving. Not every relationship deserves unlimited energy. Not every request is healthy. And unspoken resentment is often proof that the giver's motives have become mixed. A person can call themselves generous while quietly building a case against the other person in their own mind. That is not generosity. It is delayed disappointment.

So the chapter lands on a harder standard than simple kindness. Give in ways that are real, useful, and free enough to avoid turning into private accounting. Ask how you can help, but do not confuse that with having no boundaries. The point is to build a bond strong enough to carry trust without requiring a running tally. Once that principle is in place, the next question becomes sharper: if you cannot build every relationship equally, what mission should guide where you invest?
