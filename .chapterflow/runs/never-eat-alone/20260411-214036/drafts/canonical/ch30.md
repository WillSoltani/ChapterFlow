Success can distort a person before it announces itself as a problem. Attention arrives more easily, invitations multiply, and gratitude can quietly give way to expectation. Ferrazzi's chapter matters because it notices that network strength begins to rot when success turns into self-importance, entitlement, or a weaker capacity to notice and care about other people.

That is what the title means at its strongest. Never giving in to hubris is not a command to shrink, deny accomplishment, or turn against ambition. It is a warning that influence becomes unstable when a person starts treating status as permission to listen less, reciprocate less, and expect more. The chapter is strongest when hubris feels like relational decay rather than like a vague moral flaw.

This is why ego matters strategically as well as morally. A self-important person becomes harder to trust, easier to resent, and less able to sustain the generosity that made the network strong in the first place. The chapter is strongest when success is shown to require discipline against entitlement.

That also explains why the chapter follows convening so naturally. Building rooms and influence can widen consequence, but widening consequence also creates a new corruption risk. The more attention a person receives, the easier it becomes to confuse earned respect with a permanent right to deference.

The hard line matters here. If confidence curdles into arrogance, status performance, or entitlement, the chapter collapses. The person may still look successful for a while, but relationships begin weakening because attention narrows, reciprocity thins out, and trust becomes harder to maintain.

So the stronger reading is disciplined. Keep ambition, but protect it from ego by staying attentive, reciprocal, and difficult to intoxicate with your own success. The chapter is about sustaining humane strength, not pretending accomplishment should disappear.

That points forward cleanly. Once the reader sees how success must be protected from hubris, the next question is how growth can stay healthy through mentoring, reciprocity, and repeated human development across relationships.
