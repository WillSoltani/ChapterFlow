Some relationships carry more weight than they first appear to. A network can look broad and active while depending quietly on a few ties that hold trust, access, continuity, or belonging in place. Ferrazzi's chapter matters because it notices that not every relationship stabilizes the wider structure equally.

That is the point of the anchor-tenant frame. A few relationships function like steady supports in the background. They may not be the loudest or flashiest connections, but they often hold together rooms, communities, introductions, and future openings that would feel shakier without them.

This is why the chapter pushes beyond light maintenance. Once the reader knows how to keep many threads alive, the harder question becomes where deeper investment belongs. Some ties deserve more than occasional signs of life because they steady a wider circle around them.

But the chapter only works if that investment stays grounded in care and usefulness. Feeding the anchor is not flattery, orbiting, or selective attention to the most impressive person in sight. It is sustained notice, generosity, and reliability toward relationships that genuinely support larger continuity.

The hard limit matters here. If the reader treats anchor relationships as a shortlist of socially superior people, the chapter collapses into prestige strategy. The wider network stops mattering, and the important tie becomes little more than a ladder.

So the stronger reading is disciplined. Notice which relationships repeatedly stabilize trust, access, or community, then invest in them with durable care before you need anything. The chapter is about social architecture, not hierarchy worship.

That points forward cleanly. Once the reader sees why a few ties deserve deeper feeding, the next question is what the wider edge of the network can offer that the core alone cannot: surprise, reach, and fresh access.
