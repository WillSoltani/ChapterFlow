The hidden ledger ruins relationships early and quietly. You help, you introduce, you show up, and somewhere in the background a private arithmetic begins. Maybe you never say it. Maybe the other person never sees it. But once the bond starts carrying scorekeeping, every delay and every disappointment gets heavier. That is the pressure behind "Don't Keep Score."

The chapter works because it refuses a lazy split. It does not say generosity is pure and ambition is dirty. It says the relationship changes quality when help is offered as contribution instead of as a disguised invoice. That is why the official framing matters: it is never simply about getting what you want. It is also about helping other people get what they want. The sentence widens the direction of the bond.

That widening changes the whole logic of networking. In a ledger mindset, every favor becomes a chip and every conversation becomes future leverage. In a contribution mindset, help builds trust, memory, and goodwill before any obvious return appears. The chapter is not anti-ambition. It is anti-accounting where a relationship should be.

This is where small acts matter. The frozen secondary support keeps the claim practical: generosity does not have to be grand to be strong. A useful introduction, a fast answer, a remembered detail, a helpful article, a quiet offer of time. Repeated small help matters because it proves the relationship exists outside moments of urgent need.

The chapter also pushes against selective generosity. If you only help people who already look important, the pattern shows. Broad generosity is stronger because influence changes over time and because a low-status contact today may matter much more later. More than that, broad generosity protects you from turning every kind act into status calculation.

But the chapter loses credibility if it drifts into martyrdom. Not every request deserves a yes. Not every relationship deserves unlimited energy. And resentment is usually the warning sign that the inner ledger is already active. A person can keep calling themselves generous while quietly collecting evidence that they have not been repaid. That is not openhandedness. It is delayed disappointment.

So the standard is harder than "be nice." Give in ways that are real, useful, and free enough to avoid private accounting. Ask how you can help, but keep your boundaries intact. The goal is a bond strong enough to carry trust without requiring a running tally. Once that principle is clear, the next question follows naturally: what mission should guide where your long-term relationship energy goes?
