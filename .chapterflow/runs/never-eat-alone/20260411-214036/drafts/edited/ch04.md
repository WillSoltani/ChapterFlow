Few social moves feel thinner than reaching out only when you need something. The message may be warm and well written, but the timing gives it away. Ferrazzi's point in this chapter is that timing changes the emotional quality of an ask. A relationship built under pressure feels like an interruption. A relationship built earlier feels like a continuation.

That is the chapter's first distinction. Trust is not built best in the same moment it is asked to carry weight. Familiarity, goodwill, and memory work better when they exist before the favor arrives. The frozen support is simple but strong here: relationships should be built before they are needed.

This does not mean everyone deserves constant contact. The chapter is lighter than that. It is about pre-need investment, not endless maintenance theater. A thoughtful check-in, a useful note, a shared article, a brief reconnection before life gets urgent: these small contacts make later outreach warmer because they show the bond existed before the ask.

The chapter is really about credibility. If someone hears from you only in emergencies, they naturally wonder whether the relationship matters on its own. If they have seen you before the pressure point, the later request lands differently. It sounds less like opportunism and more like an extension of an existing relationship.

Still, the idea gets weak if it becomes mechanical. Maintenance can turn fake fast. Forced contact, overmessaging, and artificial persistence do not build trust. They create noise. So the chapter needs restraint built into it. Presence matters, but it has to feel human rather than managed.

By the end, timing has become a relationship principle. Build trust early, stay in light contact where it is real, and do not wait until urgency makes your outreach sound hollow. Once that is clear, the next question becomes bolder: if some relationships are worth building in advance, how much audacity should you bring when you reach beyond your current circle?
