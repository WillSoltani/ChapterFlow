Chapter Promise:
This chapter will show the reader that charisma has multiple workable styles and that the right style depends on room, role, and congruent balance of presence, power, and warmth.

Opening Move:
Open on the mistake of borrowing someone else's charisma whole, as if one style could fit every room and every person.

Anchor Allocation:
- anchor 1 used in paragraph(s): P1-P2
- anchor 2 used in paragraph(s): P2-P5
- anchor 3 used in paragraph(s): P5-P6

Paragraph Job Map:
- P1: reject the one-style fantasy and explain why charisma cannot mean one ideal personality
- P2: show that different styles are really different balances of presence, power, and warmth
- P3: explain why room demands and role demands change which style reads best
- P4: show why copied style or over-adaptation looks false and drains credibility
- P5: explain style mismatch and how to choose a style that fits both the room and the person
- P6: bridge into Chapter 7 by asking how style starts landing in the first seconds of a new room

Required Distinction or Mechanism:
The chapter's mechanism is contextual fit. Different styles succeed because different rooms need different balances of attention, consequence, and visible goodwill.

Hard-Depth Tension:
Hard depth must keep this question alive: how do you adapt to a room without flattening yourself into a borrowed role or a fake social costume?

Threshold Question:
What balance of presence, power, and warmth fits this room while still feeling inhabited by you?

Takeaway Count Lock:
- Easy: 3
- Medium: 5
- Hard: 5

Hard Takeaway Topics:
- Takeaway 1: charisma does not have one universally correct style
- Takeaway 2: style differences reflect different triad balances
- Takeaway 3: room and role determine which style reads best
- Takeaway 4: mimicry and over-adaptation damage charisma
- Takeaway 5: style choice sets up the fast read of first impressions

Scenario Lesson Map:
- Scenario 1 (vignette, work): teaches how a calm, narrower style can outperform forced high-energy leadership
- Scenario 2 (inner_monologue, school): teaches a student-club leader not to imitate the outgoing predecessor
- Scenario 3 (contrast, personal): teaches how different styles change the feel of the same hard conversation
- Scenario 4 (dialogue, work): teaches discussing style fit before a presentation or team handoff
- Scenario 5 (decision_point, school): teaches choosing a leadership approach for a club election or meeting
- Scenario 6 (dilemma, personal): teaches when warmth-forward presence works better than force in a private conflict

Scenario Vividness Targets:
For at least 4 of 6 scenarios, include one concrete detail: campaign flyers curling on a table, handoff binder corners bent, coffee ring on an agenda, trophy case reflection, buzzing fluorescent club room, pen tapping against a notebook.

Preview Constraint:
The preview must ask how style lands quickly in a new room. It must not pre-explain Chapter 7's full first-impression chapter.

Genericity Risks:
- drifting into personality-typing language with no practical leverage
- treating style as a brand choice instead of a room-fit choice
- flattening all styles into the same advice

Forbidden Drift:
- filler move 1: be whatever style feels coolest
- filler move 2: copy charismatic people you admire
- invented authority move 1: unsupported ranked taxonomy or prestige examples
- brief-language leak 1: style language that excuses coldness, manipulation, or shallow image management
