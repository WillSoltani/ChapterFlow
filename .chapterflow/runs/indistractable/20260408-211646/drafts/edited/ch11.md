# Edited Draft — Ch11 "Ask the Critical Question"

External triggers borrow authority from speed. A ping appears, a person asks for something, an alert flashes, a browser tab tempts, and the body moves before judgment catches up. That is why so many distractions feel less like choices and more like reflexes.

Eyal's answer in this chapter is simple and sharp: insert a critical question before obeying the trigger. The question is not whether the trigger exists. It does. The question is whether responding now serves what you already planned to do, or whether it is pulling you away from prior intent.

That move matters because external triggers are powerful precisely when they feel self-justifying. A message looks social, a notification looks informative, a request looks responsible, and a cue in the environment looks harmless. The critical question breaks that spell by asking whether the trigger is serving traction or simply commandeering attention.

The chapter works only if the question stays concrete. It is not an invitation to deep self-analysis every time a screen lights up. It is a fast filter. Does this deserve action now? Does it fit the block I chose? Is this response aligned with what I meant to be doing, or am I slipping into reactivity because the trigger arrived first?

The chapter also preserves a necessary limit. Some triggers do deserve action. A true emergency, an expected deadline, or a responsibility that has already been agreed upon should not be ignored simply because it interrupted a preferred task. The point is not denial. The point is to stop treating every external cue like a command.

That makes the bridge to Chapter 12 clean. Once a person learns to question triggers instead of automatically obeying them, work interruptions become easier to surface, measure, and negotiate. The next chapter takes that logic into the specific costs and design of interruption at work.
