By the time the book reaches Chapter 5, the reader has already been taught to notice the trigger and to question the meaning attached to discomfort. The next move is more practical. Sometimes the task itself is built in a way that creates unnecessary resistance. A project looks too large, the first step is foggy, the feedback is delayed, or the work feels so flat that the mind keeps shopping for relief. In those cases, the task is not just being avoided. It is helping generate the avoidance.

That is what it means to reimagine the task. The goal is not to pretend hard work should feel easy or to turn every commitment into a game. The goal is to reduce avoidable friction. A clearer next action, a smaller starting point, a visible sign of progress, or a better level of challenge can change how the work feels to enter. When the task stops presenting itself like a brick wall, distraction loses some of its advantage.

This chapter matters because people often treat task design as morally irrelevant. They think the only serious option is to force themselves through whatever shape the task currently has. Eyal's move is more strategic. If the current shape keeps producing avoidable dread or drift, then redesign is not cheating. It is part of doing the work honestly.

The chapter still keeps a limit in view. Good task design does not erase the cost of meaningful effort. Some work remains repetitive, exposed, slow, or uncertain even after it is improved. The point is not to remove all friction. The point is to stop donating extra friction for free.

That sets up the next chapter well. Once a task is designed better, another source of resistance becomes easier to see: the identity story a person carries about what they are supposedly like.
