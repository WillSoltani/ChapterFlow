# Edited Draft — Ch10 "Sync with Stakeholders at Work"

Many workplace distractions do not start as random temptations. They start as uncertainty between people. A manager does not know when an update will arrive, a teammate does not know what counts as urgent, a client does not know which channel merits a fast answer, and a remote team has never agreed on whether every message implies instant response. In that vacuum, interruptions multiply.

That is why Eyal closes the traction section by turning to stakeholders at work. If attention matters, expectations around that attention have to be made visible. Stakeholders are the people whose work overlaps with yours: bosses, peers, direct reports, clients, partners, and collaborators. Syncing with them means aligning on availability, response windows, interruption rules, escalation paths, and the conditions under which focus time should stay intact.

This is not workplace theater. It is not etiquette varnish on real productivity. It is a traction move. A calendar block is weak if everyone around it has to guess whether it is real, interruptible, or merely decorative. When expectations stay vague, other people fill the gap with assumptions, and most of those assumptions favor immediacy.

That pressure has become easier to intensify in remote and video-heavy work. A person can be reachable by chat, email, calendar invite, text, call, and instant meeting link at the same time. Without explicit norms, every channel starts acting like an emergency lane. The problem is not only technology. It is ambiguity moving through technology.

The chapter therefore pushes proactive coordination. Tell stakeholders when deep-work blocks are protected. Clarify when routine responses usually arrive. Separate genuine escalation routes from ordinary requests. Set meeting expectations so default attendance does not swallow the week. If a role genuinely requires faster responsiveness, say that plainly. Honest alignment beats private resentment.

The chapter also preserves a necessary limit. Syncing with stakeholders is not permission to disappear or to shift the cost of delay onto everyone else. Shared work still carries shared obligations. The point is not unilateral isolation. The point is reducing needless interruption by replacing unspoken guessing with explicit agreement.

That limit makes the bridge to the next chapter clean. Once expectations are aligned, a person is in a stronger position to face incoming triggers without reflexively obeying them. Chapter 11 asks the critical question when interruption arrives. Chapter 10 makes that question more realistic by clearing the coordination fog first.
