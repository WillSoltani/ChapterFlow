The hard part of distraction often happens before anything visible appears on the screen. A person says the phone pulled them in, the browser swallowed the afternoon, or the interruption broke the flow. Sometimes that is partly true. But Nir Eyal's next move is to look earlier. Before the click, there is often an inner shove. Restlessness, uncertainty, boredom, irritation, or dread starts pressing for relief. The external distraction then arrives as the answer, not the first cause.

That is what it means to deal with distraction from within. The job is not to become emotionless or to pretend the urge is fake. The job is to notice that the urge is an event happening inside you, not a command you have to follow. That distinction sounds small until you see how much leverage it creates. If the urge and the action feel fused, the person believes the distraction is already underway. If the urge can be noticed one beat earlier, there is finally a place to intervene.

This chapter also explains why tool-only defenses keep underperforming. By the time a person is arguing with the app, the internal trigger has often already recruited attention. A blocked site, a hidden icon, or a silenced notification can help, but it is late help if the person still treats discomfort as an instruction to escape. Dealing with distraction from within means staying with the first pressure long enough to keep it from hardening into behavior.

That does not mean every urge is easy to handle once it is seen. Some internal triggers hit hard. They repeat. They feel personal and urgent. The chapter stays honest by refusing to promise that awareness makes discomfort pleasant. It only says that once the inner move becomes visible, obedience stops being the only option.

That is the bridge forward. If a person can notice the internal trigger without instantly serving it, the next question is not just how to resist it. The next question is how to understand it differently in the first place.
