# Edited Draft — Ch20 "The Power of Precommitments"

Precommitments are powerful because future weakness is often predictable. People usually know which moments will tempt them to drift, delay, or abandon what they meant to do. The problem is not that those moments are mysterious. The problem is that they keep getting negotiated too late, after the urge has already arrived.

That is why Eyal introduces pacts. A pact is a precommitment that shapes future behavior before temptation shows up. Instead of trusting the future self to improvise under pressure, the person changes the future choice environment in advance. The unwanted path becomes harder, slower, costlier, or less available, and prior intent gains structural support.

The chapter also explains why pacts come late in the book. They are not the first answer. Internal triggers, traction, and external-trigger design all matter first. A pact works best when it protects a plan that already reflects genuine values rather than trying to replace that work with brute force.

That keeps the chapter from becoming self-punishment theater. The point is not cruelty toward the future self. The point is honesty. If you already know which moment tends to break the plan, then shaping that moment in advance is often wiser than promising to be stronger inside it.

The chapter introduces the whole pact category without exhausting it. Some pacts add effort or friction. Some add explicit cost. Some tie consistency to identity. All of them matter because they are decided before the heat of the moment asks for a rewrite.

That prepares Chapter 21. Once the reader understands why a pact works, the next step is to study one form of pact that makes distraction more expensive than it looks.
