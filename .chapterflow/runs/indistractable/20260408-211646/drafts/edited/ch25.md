# Edited Draft — Ch25 "Understand Their Internal Triggers"

Chapter 25 says children have internal triggers too. When a child drifts, avoids, explodes, or disappears into a distraction, the visible object is not always the whole story. Eyal's framework asks what uncomfortable feeling or urge is underneath the behavior before blaming the device or the child.

That is why the chapter keeps discomfort at the center. Boredom, shame, frustration, loneliness, social tension, or anxiety can all make distraction feel relieving. If an adult only reacts to the surface object, the inner trigger remains untouched and the same pattern can reappear through a different outlet.

The chapter matters because the first explanation changes the whole relationship. If a parent or caregiver starts from accusation, the child learns to hide. If the adult starts from curiosity, there is at least a chance to understand what the distraction is doing for the child emotionally.

The chapter also preserves an important limit. This is not an argument against boundaries. It is an argument against confusing boundaries with understanding. A child may still need a limit, but the adult will use it more wisely if they can see the discomfort underneath the urge to escape.

That keeps the chapter from becoming blame in either direction. The child is not just weak, and the caregiver is not automatically the villain. The useful move is to see distraction as an attempt to move away from discomfort before deciding what response makes sense.

That prepares Chapter 26. Once inner triggers are clearer, children and caregivers can begin to plan traction together instead of only negotiating after discomfort has already turned into distraction.
