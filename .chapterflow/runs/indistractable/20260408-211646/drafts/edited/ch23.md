# Edited Draft — Ch23 "Fixing Distraction Is a Test of Company Culture"

Chapter 23 says chronic distraction at work is often a culture problem before it is a character problem. When employees are interrupted constantly, expected to be instantly responsive, or made to feel risky for protecting focused time, the company is teaching a pattern. The problem is not only what one person fails to resist. The environment keeps rewarding the same drift.

That is why the chapter treats culture as a coordination test. What gets normalized? What gets praised? What gets punished? A workplace can talk about priorities while still rewarding interruption, reactive meetings, and ambient urgency. Those contradictions shape attention more powerfully than slogans do.

The chapter matters because culture often hides inside routine. A calendar packed with low-clarity meetings, a chat tool treated like a siren, or a manager who confuses speed with seriousness can all make distraction structural. Remote work and videoconferencing do not erase this problem. They simply carry the same norms through different channels.

The chapter also resists an easy mistake. This is not an argument that individuals are helpless. It is an argument that repeated workplace distraction usually signals a system that keeps teaching people to betray traction. If protecting focus feels politically unsafe, the culture is part of the problem.

That is why the key question becomes whether people can surface the costs honestly. Can a team admit that interruption has a price? Can someone push back on bad defaults without being treated as uncooperative? A company that cannot answer those questions well is not merely busy. It is culturally training distraction.

That prepares Chapter 24. Once the diagnosis is cultural, the next step is to describe the actual habits and norms of a workplace that protects attention instead of draining it.
