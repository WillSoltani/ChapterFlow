# Edited Draft — Ch29 "Spread Social Antibodies Among Friends"

Chapter 29 asks what becomes of a child's growing self-control once friends enter the room. The answer is not to repeat a simple anti-peer-pressure speech. Eyal's stronger move is to treat peers as part of the environment itself. Other people can function as external triggers, and group norms can either multiply distraction or help contain it.

That is why the chapter introduces social antibodies. A child does not only need private resolve. They need social protections that work in live friendships: a phrase they can really say, an agreement a group can really hold, an ally who makes a healthier norm less lonely, or an exit move that protects dignity while still protecting attention. The chapter stays practical because the pressure is practical.

This matters because the child-owned pacts of Chapter 28 are being tested here. A promise that holds only in solitude is not yet socially durable. Peer settings reveal whether the commitment can survive jokes, invitations, and habits that reward immediate reaction over intention.

The chapter also keeps its moral balance. It is not trying to make children suspicious of friendship or heroic against every peer. Friendship is not the contaminant. Unexamined norms are. Children are social learners, which means better peer culture can become part of the solution instead of something the child must simply endure.

That is what makes the chapter more than a warning. It shows that attention is already relational among children. The final chapter only widens that same insight into adult love, where repeated partial attention can tax intimacy just as quietly and just as deeply.
