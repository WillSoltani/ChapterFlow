# Edited Draft — Ch12 "Hack Back Work Interruptions"

Once a person starts asking whether a trigger deserves attention, the next question becomes obvious: why are so many interruptions designed to arrive as if they were unanswerable facts? Work interruptions often survive because they are treated as background weather rather than as choices with real costs.

Those costs are not mysterious. An interruption breaks the current line of thought, imposes a switching cost, and leaves recovery work behind after the conversation ends. When teams normalize constant interruption, they also normalize invisible restart labor.

That is why Eyal shifts from private judgment to shared design. Hacking back work interruptions means making them visible enough to negotiate. Count where they come from. Notice which ones are routine rather than urgent. Separate true escalation paths from ordinary requests. Create office hours, batching windows, documented questions, or update rhythms that reduce random break-ins.

This is not anti-coworker doctrine. Many interruptions happen because the current system makes interruption the easiest path. If questions are always answered by whoever is closest, loudest, or most available, people learn to interrupt because the environment rewards it. A better system changes the reward structure.

The chapter also keeps an important limit. Some interruptions are legitimate. Certain roles require responsiveness, some moments truly are urgent, and collaboration cannot be reduced to silent parallel work. The goal is not zero interruption. The goal is interruption design that matches real needs instead of default habit.

That leads naturally into email. Once the reader sees interruptions as negotiable rather than inevitable, the inbox becomes easier to treat as a system to redesign rather than as an always-open obligation. Chapter 13 narrows the same logic into email specifically.
