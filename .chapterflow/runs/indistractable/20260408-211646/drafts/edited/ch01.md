The phone gets blamed because the phone is visible. It lights up. It pings. It sits on the desk like a small public enemy with a battery. Nir Eyal starts one step earlier. He argues that many distractions begin before the screen. They begin when something inside the person feels bad enough that relief starts looking valuable. Boredom, uncertainty, stress, loneliness, and the sting of difficult work create demand. The device often only fills the order.

That shift changes what counts as a serious answer. If the deepest cause were the tool alone, then deleting an app or silencing a notification would solve the problem more often than it does. But one outlet disappears and another one appears. Email replaces feeds. Feeds replace chat. Chat replaces snacking. The route changes while the pressure stays the same. Technology is often the channel, not always the engine.

Eyal also makes motivation less sentimental. People do not act only to chase rewards. They also act to get away from discomfort. That is why so much distraction looks irrational only from far away. In the moment, the move often makes emotional sense. It lowers tension fast. The cost arrives later, which is why the habit can feel understandable and expensive at the same time.

The chapter's practical gain is a better question. Before asking which app stole your attention, ask what feeling made escape look worth buying. That question does not solve the whole problem, but it moves the diagnosis upstream. Once the diagnosis moves upstream, the rest of the book can stop looking like a pile of clever tricks and start looking like a system.
