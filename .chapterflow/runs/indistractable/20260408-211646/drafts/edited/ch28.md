# Edited Draft — Ch28 "Teach Them to Make Their Own Pacts"

Chapter 28 asks what happens when the pact idea reaches children. The answer is not instant autonomy, and it is not endless parental control either. Eyal's stronger move is to help children begin making commitments they can understand and increasingly own, with adult support still visible around the edges.

That is why the chapter treats pacts as developmental self-authorship. A child-owned pact is not just a rule announced from above. It is a precommitment the child can increasingly understand, help shape, and connect to something they care about. Ownership is partial at first, then stronger over time.

The chapter matters because there is a real difference between imposed compliance and practiced ownership. A child may follow a rule because an adult is present. A pact they increasingly author can teach a more durable sense of agency. But the chapter refuses the fantasy that this process is frictionless or complete all at once.

That is why scaffolding stays central. Adults still protect the process, still notice developmental limits, and still help a child revise commitments that are too vague, too big, or too performative to own realistically. Guidance is not the enemy of ownership here. It is part of how ownership grows.

The chapter also keeps its moral balance. The goal is not to turn a child into a tiny productivity machine. It is to help them practice making commitments they can actually understand and keep with support.

That prepares Chapter 29. Once children are beginning to own commitments, the next test is what happens when those commitments meet peer pressure, friend norms, and the social environment.
