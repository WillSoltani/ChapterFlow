Chapter 7 makes the book's standard harder and more useful. Up to now, the reader has been working on distraction from the inside. Here Eyal turns toward proof. If something matters, where is the time for it? Values sound noble in conversation, but the calendar tells a less flattering truth. A person can sincerely say that health, deep work, family, or reflection matters and still give those things no protected time at all.

That is why the chapter says to turn values into time. The point is not to worship a planner. It is to make priorities concrete enough to test. Once a value claims time on the calendar, the person can see whether their day actually supports what they say they care about. Without that step, values stay sentimental and easy to misreport.

This chapter also explains why to-do lists are not enough. Lists collect intentions, but they do not decide where life will actually go. The calendar forces a more serious question: when will this matter in real time, and what will lose that space instead? That is why timeboxing belongs here. It is less a productivity trick than a contract between stated values and scheduled reality.

The chapter still preserves a limit. A calendar is not proof that life will go exactly as planned, and revisions are not betrayals by themselves. The point is not perfect control. The point is making priorities visible and adjustable in a form that can withstand honest review.

That bridge matters because once time reflects values, the next question becomes more exact. If outcomes cannot be guaranteed, what part of the plan can still be controlled? Chapter 8 answers that by turning from outcomes to inputs.
