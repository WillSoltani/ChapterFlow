Chapter 9 takes the calendar logic into a domain where people often resist it on emotional grounds. Relationships matter, but they are frequently left to whatever time survives after work, chores, errands, and reactive demands finish taking their share. Eyal's point is not that love should feel scheduled in a cold way. It is that relationships are values, and values that never claim time are easy to neglect while still feeling sincere.

That is why the chapter says to schedule important relationships. The move is protective, not mechanical. Putting time on the calendar for a partner, a child, a close friend, or a parent does not manufacture care. It protects care from being displaced by whatever screams loudest that day. Without that protection, people often end up giving the people they value most only the scraps of their attention.

This chapter matters because many people confuse spontaneity with seriousness. They think if a relationship is real, it should not need a calendar. But the rest of life is already scheduling itself aggressively. Meetings, deadlines, errands, and notifications do not wait politely. Protected time is often what keeps relationships from becoming permanent leftovers.

The chapter still keeps a limit visible. A scheduled dinner or weekly walk does not guarantee depth, repair, or presence by itself. The calendar can protect the space, but emotional quality still has to be brought into it. That is why the chapter never reduces affection to logistics. It only refuses to let logistics quietly crowd affection out.

That prepares the next step well. Once the reader sees that important human relationships deserve protected time, the same logic can be applied to workplace relationships where alignment and expectation-setting determine how much distraction the environment creates.
