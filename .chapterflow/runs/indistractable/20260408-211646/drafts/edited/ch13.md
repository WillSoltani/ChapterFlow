# Edited Draft — Ch13 "Hack Back Email"

Email becomes distracting when it stops behaving like a queue and starts behaving like a live feed. A person refreshes the inbox between tasks, leaves the tab open all day, and treats each arrival like a possible command. In that setup, the inbox is no longer a tool. It is an external-trigger loop with office language.

That is why Eyal's answer is not heroic willpower and not inbox-zero theater. The stronger move is to bound email. Check it in planned windows. Process it in batches. Reduce how often the inbox gets to present itself to your attention. Let response expectations become explicit and reasonable instead of imaginary and constant.

The chapter matters because most email does not truly require immediate action, yet the inbox is designed to imply that it might. The unread count behaves like a scoreboard, the arrival sound behaves like urgency, and the open tab behaves like a standing invitation to defect from whatever was already planned.

Hacking back email therefore means giving the inbox rules. Decide when it gets checked. Use subject lines and reply habits that reduce ambiguity. Keep routine correspondence from masquerading as live interruption. If a channel genuinely needs faster response, name that instead of letting email borrow emergency status it did not earn.

The chapter also keeps an important limit. Some email matters. Deadlines, approvals, logistics, and responsibilities do not disappear because the inbox is stressful. The point is not neglect. The point is to stop handling email as a permanent open loop.

That sets up the next chapter cleanly. If email is a slow-burn interruption system, group chat is a faster and more socially pressurized one. Chapter 14 will show how default responsiveness and ambient conversation can consume attention even more aggressively.
