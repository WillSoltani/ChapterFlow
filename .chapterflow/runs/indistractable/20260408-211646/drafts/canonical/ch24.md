# Canonical Draft — Ch24 "The Indistractable Workplace"

If Chapter 23 names distraction at work as a cultural problem, Chapter 24 asks what a healthier workplace actually does. An indistractable workplace is not defined by one unusually disciplined employee. It is defined by shared habits and norms that make focus more normal and ambient urgency less powerful.

That means expectations are clearer. Meetings are more disciplined. Interruptions are less casually normalized. Response pressure is less theatrical. People can protect focused time without acting as though they are breaking a social rule. The workplace becomes a designed environment instead of a loud collection of competing defaults.

The chapter matters because good culture has to become operational. It is not enough for a company to say it values focus. The question is whether the team's routines actually support it. How are meetings handled? How is urgency distinguished from noise? What happens when someone needs uninterrupted time? What coordination habits prevent constant renegotiation?

The stronger claim is that an indistractable workplace is built through shared practice. One person protecting traction in isolation helps, but the real change comes when the group understands those behaviors as normal and legitimate. Office, remote, and hybrid settings all need this same logic, even if the channel changes.

The chapter also avoids utopian language. The point is not perfect calm. The point is a workplace where attention is not quietly treated as disposable. Healthier norms make focus more survivable in ordinary work.

That lightens the transition into the next section. Once the book has shown how adult workplaces shape behavior through norms, it can widen again and ask how children experience their own internal triggers and environments.
