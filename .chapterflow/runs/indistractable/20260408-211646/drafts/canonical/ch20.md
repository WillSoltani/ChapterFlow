# Canonical Draft — Ch20 "The Power of Precommitments"

Precommitments matter because many distractions are predictable before they arrive. A person often knows where a later moment will get slippery. They know what they will want to click, postpone, open, eat, or avoid once the urge is live. Eyal's answer is to stop pretending that every future moment must be negotiated from scratch.

That is what a pact does. A pact is a precommitment that shapes future behavior before temptation appears. Instead of trusting the future self to improvise perfectly, the person adds structure in advance. They make the unwanted path harder, slower, costlier, or less available so prior intent has a better chance of surviving contact with the moment.

The chapter matters because it arrives late in the sequence for a reason. Pacts are not the first tool. They work best after internal triggers have been understood, traction has been scheduled, and external triggers have been reduced. Once that groundwork exists, a pact becomes a practical way to protect what the person already decided matters.

The stronger claim is not that people need punishment. It is that they need honesty about predictable weakness. Precommitment respects the fact that future urges can be strong and that prior intent deserves structural help, not just moral speeches.

The chapter therefore introduces pacts as a category. Some pacts add friction. Some attach explicit cost. Some reinforce chosen identity. What unites them is that they are decided ahead of time so the future moment has less room to betray the plan.

That leads into price pacts next. Once the reader sees why future-facing constraints work, the next chapter can zoom into one version that makes distraction more expensive than it first appears.
