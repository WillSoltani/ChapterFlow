# Canonical Draft — Ch15 "Hack Back Meetings"

Meetings become distracting when they stop being deliberate tools and start becoming the default way to handle uncertainty, status, or hesitation. A room gets booked, a call gets scheduled, and suddenly shared attention is committed before anyone has proven that the meeting actually helps the work.

That is why Eyal's correction is not to hate meetings but to make them earn time. A meeting is a claim on multiple people's calendars at once. That makes it expensive by definition. If the purpose is vague, the agenda is missing, the attendance is bloated, or the issue could be handled asynchronously, then the meeting is functioning as an attention sink rather than as a useful decision tool.

Hacking back meetings means tightening the claim. Ask what decision, clarification, or coordination requires live time. Shrink the attendee list. Use shorter windows. Replace status ritual with written updates where possible. Stop treating calendar grabs as harmless because they look organized.

The chapter matters because meetings are socially protected in ways smaller interruptions are not. Once a calendar invite exists, people often treat the time as already justified. That lets weak meeting design hide inside procedure and politeness.

The chapter also keeps an important limit. Some meetings are useful. Real alignment, hard tradeoffs, sensitive conversations, and time-bound collaboration can deserve synchronous time. The point is not abolition. The point is that the meeting should earn the claim it makes.

That prepares the shift to the smartphone well. Once shared workplace claims on attention have been tightened, the next obvious trigger source is the most portable one a person carries everywhere. Chapter 16 moves from organizational attention claims to the device that follows the person all day.
