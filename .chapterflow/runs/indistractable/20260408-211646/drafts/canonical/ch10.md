# Canonical Draft — Ch10 "Sync with Stakeholders at Work"

Many workplace distractions do not begin as random temptations. They begin as uncertainty between people. A manager does not know when updates will come, a teammate does not know what counts as urgent, a client does not know which channel deserves a fast answer, and a remote team has not agreed on whether every message implies immediate response. In that vacuum, interruptions multiply.

That is why Eyal turns from protecting relationships at home to syncing with stakeholders at work. If attention matters, expectations around that attention have to be made visible. Stakeholders are the people whose work overlaps with yours: bosses, peers, direct reports, clients, partners, and collaborators. Syncing with them means aligning on when you are available, how quickly you usually respond, what deserves interruption, and how genuine urgency should escalate.

This is not workplace theater. It is not a soft-skills garnish on real productivity. It is a traction move. A calendar block is weak if everyone around it has to guess whether it is real, interruptible, or merely decorative. When expectations stay vague, other people fill the gap with their own assumptions. Most of those assumptions favor immediacy.

That pressure has become easier to intensify in remote and video-heavy work. A person can now be reachable by chat, email, calendar invite, text, call, and instant meeting link at the same time. Without explicit norms, every channel starts behaving like an emergency lane. The problem is not simply technology. It is ambiguity moving through technology.

The chapter therefore asks for proactive coordination. Tell stakeholders when deep-work blocks are protected. Clarify when routine responses usually arrive. Separate true escalation paths from ordinary requests. Set expectations for meetings so default attendance does not eat the whole week. If a role genuinely requires fast responsiveness, name that too. Honest alignment is better than private resentment.

The chapter also keeps an important limit intact. Syncing with stakeholders is not permission to disappear or dump delay costs onto other people. Shared work still has shared obligations. The point is not unilateral isolation. The point is reducing needless interruption by replacing unspoken guessing with explicit agreement.

That is what makes the bridge to the next section work. Once a person has aligned expectations with the people around them, they are in a stronger position to face incoming triggers without reflexively obeying them. Chapter 11 will ask the critical question at the moment of interruption. Chapter 10 makes that question more realistic by cleaning up the surrounding expectations first.
