# Canonical Draft — Ch21 "Prevent Distraction with Price Pacts"

Price pacts matter because distraction often looks cheaper in the moment than it really is. The person sees the quick escape, the skipped block, the unnecessary scroll, or the delayed task as if the cost were small. Eyal's answer is to stop letting that false cheapness govern the decision. A price pact adds a consequence chosen in advance so breaking prior intent no longer feels free.

That is why the chapter must stay sequenced correctly. A price pact is not the first answer. It works after the person has already done the earlier work of understanding triggers, making time for traction, and reducing external triggers. Once a real plan exists, an explicit cost can help protect it.

The chapter matters because explicit cost changes the shape of the temptation. The bad default is no longer just pleasant, easy, or immediate. It now carries a price that the person deliberately attached before the weak moment began. That does not make temptation disappear, but it does make the choice more honest.

The stronger claim is not that people need punishment theater. It is that some plans deserve a consequence when they are abandoned too casually. The pact should fit the value being protected. If the cost becomes random, performative, or cruel, the chapter loses its point.

That is why price pacts are one subtype of precommitment rather than the whole story. They are useful when distraction keeps pretending to be cheap. But cost is only one way to reinforce prior intent.

That leads into identity pacts. Once explicit price has been explored, the next question is how a chosen self-concept can also make follow-through feel more coherent and drift feel less like a neutral option.
