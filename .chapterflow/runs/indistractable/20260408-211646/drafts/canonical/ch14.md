# Canonical Draft — Ch14 "Hack Back Group Chat"

Group chat is not just faster email. It is a different pressure system. Messages arrive in a shared visible space, response times feel socially legible, and silence can start to look like absence even when the person is doing the work they meant to do.

That is why Eyal treats group chat as a norm problem, not only as a notification problem. The app matters, but the stronger force is what the group has silently decided a message means. If every post implies immediate acknowledgment, if every channel acts urgent, and if everyone is expected to stay ambiently available, chat begins to tax attention all day.

Hacking back group chat means putting boundaries around those norms. Clarify what each channel is for. Separate true urgency from routine discussion. Mute by default where possible. Let asynchronous response become normal again. Stop using visible presence as the standard for contribution.

The chapter matters because chat turns social uncertainty into repeated checking. A person wonders whether they are expected to respond, whether silence will be noticed, and whether everyone else is already reacting. That makes chat more pressuring than email even when the individual messages are smaller.

The chapter also keeps an important limit. Group chat can be genuinely useful for coordination, quick clarification, and shared visibility. The point is not to destroy it. The point is to stop letting it operate as a permanent claim on attention.

That leads into meetings. When chat channels stay unclear and everything feels unresolved in real time, teams often compensate by grabbing time on the calendar. Chapter 15 follows by asking how meetings should earn their right to interrupt entire blocks of time.
