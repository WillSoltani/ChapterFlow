# Canonical Draft — Ch25 "Understand Their Internal Triggers"

When children get distracted, adults often rush straight to the visible object: the phone, the game, the show, the homework avoidance, the sudden complaint. Eyal's earlier framework asks a harder question first. What discomfort is underneath the behavior? If internal triggers come from uncomfortable feelings and urges, then children's distraction also has an inner side before it has a device side.

That is why this chapter resists the simple ban reflex. A device can be involved, but the deeper issue may be boredom, loneliness, anxiety, shame, frustration, social pressure, or the wish to escape something difficult. If the adult only attacks the surface object, the child may lose the device without learning anything about the discomfort that made distraction attractive.

The chapter matters because the wrong explanation produces the wrong response. If every distracted moment is treated as defiance or screen addiction, then caregivers miss the child's internal world. Curiosity becomes more useful than accusation. What are they feeling? What are they trying not to feel? What inner state is this behavior helping them leave?

The stronger claim is not that boundaries do not matter. It is that boundaries without understanding are shallow. Children still need limits, but those limits work better when the adult understands the discomfort underneath the pull toward distraction. The child is not only a rule-breaker. They are also a person trying to regulate something.

That keeps the chapter from becoming blame in either direction. It does not accuse the child of weakness, and it does not accuse caregivers of causing every problem. It simply insists that distraction starts internally before control strategies can make much sense.

That leads directly to the next chapter. Once the inner triggers are better understood, caregivers and children can begin to make time for traction together instead of fighting only at the moment of distraction.
