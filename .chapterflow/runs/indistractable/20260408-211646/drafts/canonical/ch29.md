# Canonical Draft — Ch29 "Spread Social Antibodies Among Friends"

The prior chapter argued that children can increasingly own commitments they understand. Chapter 29 asks what happens when those commitments meet other children. The answer is not that peers are a side issue. Friends are part of the environment, and other people can act like external triggers too.

That matters because peer norms can quietly spread distraction. A group chat can reward instant response. A joke can make focus look socially risky. A lunch-table habit can normalize constant checking. A child may have a real commitment and still lose it once the crowd starts teaching a different rule.

That is why the chapter turns to social antibodies. The point is not to lecture children about being stronger than every friend around them. The point is to help them build usable social protections: honest phrases, small group agreements, ally patterns, and exit moves that make it easier to keep what matters in company.

The chapter also refuses a lazy moral story. Friendship is not the enemy. Children are social learners, and peers can strengthen traction as well as weaken it. The stronger move is to help shape cultures where healthier norms are easier to inhabit.

That makes this chapter the true social test of the child section. A pact that works only in private is still fragile. Social antibodies help that commitment survive contact with real peers.

The bridge to the closing chapter is now clear. Once attention is seen as something friendships can either protect or erode, the book can widen the same truth into adult intimacy.
