# Canonical Draft — Ch26 "Make Time for Traction Together"

If Chapter 25 explains the inner triggers that can pull children away from what matters, Chapter 26 asks what a family does before that pull arrives. Eyal's answer is to make time for traction together. In the book's language, traction means actions aligned with values and prior intent. That logic should not belong only to adults at work. It can shape family life too.

That is why this chapter resists becoming generic calendar management. The point is not simply that parents should schedule everything. The stronger move is collaborative planning. Children and caregivers can make routines, blocks, and expectations visible together so important actions are less likely to be negotiated only in the heat of distraction.

The chapter matters because shared traction reduces friction later. Homework, play, rest, chores, family time, and personal time all become easier to protect when the plan is clearer beforehand. The family is no longer improvising every value at the moment it is tested.

The stronger claim is that family traction should stay tied to values, not just efficiency. A family plan should reflect what matters to the people in it, not just what makes the household easier to control. That is what keeps the chapter from becoming command-and-control scheduling.

The chapter also keeps the unit of change relational. One person can make a plan for a child, but traction together works better when the child can understand, help shape, or at least meaningfully see the commitments around them. Shared intent creates a different kind of buy-in than pure imposition.

That leads naturally into external triggers. Once a family has clearer shared routines and visible traction, it becomes easier to shape the environment around those commitments instead of only reacting to every distraction as it appears.
