# Canonical Draft — Ch28 "Teach Them to Make Their Own Pacts"

The earlier pact chapters argued that precommitments shape future behavior. Chapter 28 asks how that logic changes when the learner is a child. The answer cannot be that adults simply hand over perfect autonomy. But it also cannot be that children live forever under rules they did not help author. Eyal's stronger move is developmental self-authorship.

That means children can increasingly participate in making commitments they understand and can begin to own. A pact for a child is not just a rule imposed from above. It becomes more meaningful when the child can see what it protects, how it works, and why it exists. Ownership grows in layers.

The chapter matters because imposed rules and self-authored commitments do different things. A child may comply with a rule temporarily, but a pact they increasingly own can teach agency. The point is not to make the child a tiny optimization robot. It is to help them practice a form of responsibility that fits their development.

That is why the chapter keeps scaffolding visible. Adults still guide, still protect, and still notice when a child is not ready to carry a full commitment alone. The chapter must not pretend that autonomy is sudden or neat.

The stronger claim is that children learn self-control partly by practicing authorship. They begin to make promises they can understand, test, and revise with support. That is different from blind obedience and different from abandonment.

That leads naturally into the peer chapter. Once children begin to own commitments, the next question is how those commitments survive contact with friends, group norms, and social pressure.
