# Canonical Draft — Ch23 "Fixing Distraction Is a Test of Company Culture"

Workplace distraction is often treated as a personal weakness, but Eyal's later framing makes a harder claim: chronic distraction at work tests company culture. If people are constantly interrupted, forced into performative responsiveness, or punished for protecting time, then the problem is not only what one employee does in a weak moment. The environment is teaching them what behavior is normal and safe.

That is why this chapter has to stay systemic. Culture shows up in what meetings are allowed to become, how quickly people are expected to respond, whether interruptions are treated as urgency by default, and whether someone can raise coordination problems without social penalty. These are not just private habits. They are shared rules, even when nobody wrote them down.

The chapter matters because culture can hide inside ordinary workflow. A team may say it values focus while rewarding constant availability. A manager may praise deep work while filling calendars with reactive check-ins. A remote team may blame the screen when the deeper issue is that no one feels permitted to set boundaries. Videoconferencing and chat can carry the same culture as an office hallway.

The stronger claim is not that individuals are absolved of responsibility. It is that repeated distraction across a workplace usually signals a coordination and norm problem, not only a willpower shortage. If people cannot safely protect traction, the company is teaching distraction while complaining about it.

That is why company culture becomes the test. Can people speak honestly about interruption costs? Can expectations be clarified before they become ambient pressure? Can focus be treated as legitimate rather than antisocial? The answers reveal more than any individual productivity tip.

That leads naturally into the next chapter. Once workplace distraction is diagnosed as cultural, the book can describe what an indistractable workplace actually looks like in its habits, norms, and shared practices.
