The phone gets blamed because the phone is visible. It lights up. It pings. It sits on the desk like a small public enemy with a battery. Nir Eyal starts somewhere less flattering and more useful. He argues that many distractions begin before the screen. They begin when something inside the person feels bad enough that relief starts looking valuable. Boredom, uncertainty, stress, loneliness, and the small sting of difficult work create demand. The device often only fills the order.

That shift matters because it changes what counts as a serious answer. If the deepest cause were the tool alone, then deleting an app or silencing a notification would solve the problem more often than it does. But people know what actually happens. One outlet disappears and another one appears. Email replaces feeds. Feeds replace chat. Chat replaces snacking. The route changes while the pressure stays the same. The chapter's core claim is that technology is often the channel, not always the engine.

Eyal also makes motivation less sentimental. People do not act only to chase rewards. They also act to get away from discomfort. That is why so much distraction feels irrational only from far away. In the moment, the distracting move often makes emotional sense. It reduces tension fast. The cost arrives later, which is why the habit can feel both understandable and expensive at the same time.

The practical gain is not moral outrage at devices. It is a better question. Before asking which app stole your attention, ask what feeling made escape look worth buying. That question does not solve the whole problem, but it moves the diagnosis upstream. And once the diagnosis moves upstream, the rest of the book can stop being a stack of clever tricks and start becoming a system.
