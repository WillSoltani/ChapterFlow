# Canonical Draft — Ch17 "Hack Back Your Desktop"

The desktop is easy to misread as neutral because it sits still. But a computer workspace is full of cues: open tabs, unread badges, pop-ups, tempting shortcuts, noisy docks, and the visible possibility of switching away from the task in front of you. In longer work blocks, that environment matters a great deal.

That is why Eyal extends the trigger-design logic from the smartphone to the desktop. The answer is not aesthetic neatness for its own sake. It is reconfiguring the workspace so it offers fewer visible invitations to drift. Reduce unnecessary notifications. Keep fewer open tabs. Simplify what appears by default. Make the work path easier to stay on than the distraction path.

The chapter matters because a cluttered desktop keeps asking attention to renegotiate itself. Every visible option is a small prompt. Every easy switch route is an invitation. A person can still be serious about the work and lose time simply because the environment keeps presenting alternatives.

Hacking back the desktop therefore means treating it like a designed workspace rather than as an accidental pile of options. Keep what helps the current block. Hide what does not. Reduce the number of ways the computer can keep reintroducing lower-value choices.

The chapter also keeps an important limit. The computer is not the enemy. It is often the place where the work actually happens. The point is not to strip it into uselessness. The point is to make it less likely to keep breaking the work it is supposed to serve.

That leads naturally into online articles. Once the desktop is seen as a space where one open path can turn into many, the browser's reading drift becomes the next obvious problem. Chapter 18 narrows the same logic into the way online reading slips into unbounded wandering.
