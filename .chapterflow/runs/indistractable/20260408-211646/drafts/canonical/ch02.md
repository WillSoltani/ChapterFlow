People like to describe time problems as if they were almost mechanical. The system is weak. The calendar is messy. The priorities are not clear enough. Nir Eyal does not deny any of those possibilities, but he asks a more revealing question. What if the task keeps getting moved because the task feels bad? A lot of delay looks technical from the outside while doing emotional work underneath.

That is the point of calling time management pain management. A task can be boring, uncertain, repetitive, vulnerable, or hard enough that the person keeps buying relief with delay. The substitute task often looks respectable. It might be email, planning, cleanup, prep work, research, or lower-stakes work that still creates the feeling of productivity. The day fills up. The painful task remains untouched. The person then describes the problem as poor scheduling because that story sounds cleaner than admitting avoidance.

The chapter matters because it turns a full day into a less flattering question. Was the day genuinely productive, or did it simply protect you from the one task that carried the most friction? That distinction explains why so many planning systems underperform. They organize the surface while leaving the emotional cost of the work intact. A prettier system can still become a prettier alibi.

Eyal is not saying every delay is emotional avoidance. Some tasks really are vague, overloaded, or blocked by missing information. The chapter is strongest where the person knows what the work is and still keeps escaping into lower-friction substitutes. In those cases the problem is not time alone. It is pain management with a calendar.

That honesty sets up the next chapter well. Once delay is named accurately, the question stops being how to arrange the day more beautifully and starts becoming how to meet the discomfort that makes delay feel useful.
