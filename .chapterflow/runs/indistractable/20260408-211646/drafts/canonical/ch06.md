Chapter 6 goes after one of the most efficient ways distraction protects itself: identity. A person says, "I am just distractible," "I have always been this way," or "Focus is not really my temperament." Those statements sound descriptive, but they often do more than describe. They lower the standard of what the person expects from themselves and quietly excuse the next drift before it arrives.

That is why Eyal asks the reader to reimagine temperament. The chapter does not deny that people have tendencies. Some people do get bored faster, feel more restless, or respond more strongly to novelty. The sharper point is that a tendency does not have to become a destiny story. The moment a person turns a pattern into an identity verdict, distraction starts getting advance permission.

This chapter matters because language changes effort. If the person keeps saying they are "just the kind of person" who loses focus, then every future lapse arrives pre-explained. That explanation can feel honest, but it can also become a way of protecting the pattern from challenge. Reimagining temperament means moving from fixed identity talk toward more workable statements about current habits, environments, and choices.

The chapter keeps an important limit visible. It is not saying everyone can rename themselves into perfect concentration. Real differences remain real. The point is narrower and more defensible: fatalistic self-stories make traction harder than it already is.

That is why this chapter closes the first part of the book well. Once the reader stops letting triggers, task shape, and identity stories run the show, the next question becomes how to turn what actually matters into time on a calendar.
