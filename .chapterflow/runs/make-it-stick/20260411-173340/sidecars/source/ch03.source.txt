Core claim:
- Mixing and interleaving practice often produce stronger discrimination and transfer than blocked repetition alone.
- Blocked practice can create smooth short-run performance while leaving the learner weaker at selecting the right move later.
- Variation helps because the learner must compare, classify, and choose.

Distinct mechanism:
- The chapter focuses on discrimination: recognizing what kind of problem or situation this is and which response fits.
- It treats mixed practice as a choice-building routine, not just a variety routine.
- It keeps structure visible so the learner is not drowned in chaos.

Required anchors:
- Blocked practice can flatter performance in the moment.
- Interleaving helps learners tell similar cases apart.
- Mixed practice supports transfer into changed situations.

Common misreadings:
- Variety itself is always good.
- Interleaving means abandoning all structure.

Specific applications:
- Use mixed problem sets during review.
- Build training scenarios that require choosing among similar responses.
- Add controlled variation instead of one uninterrupted block of the same drill.

Hard-edge requirement:
- Hard mode must preserve the line between productive mixing and unusable chaos.

Threshold question:
- When does variation sharpen the learner's eye, and when does it scatter attention so much that the pattern disappears?
