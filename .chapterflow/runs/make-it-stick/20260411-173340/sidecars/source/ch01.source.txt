Core claim:
- Many common study habits feel effective because they increase familiarity, not because they build durable recall.
- Re-reading, highlighting, cramming, and massed practice often create short-run fluency while weakening long-run retention.
- Retrieval, spacing, interleaving, and effortful reconstruction are harder in the moment but more durable over time.

Distinct mechanism:
- The chapter resets the reader's learning instincts by separating ease from durable learning.
- It treats subjective confidence as a weak signal when that confidence has not faced recall pressure.
- It introduces productive difficulty as structured effort with a learning mechanism, not random hardship.

Required anchors:
- Re-reading can produce confidence without reliable later recall.
- Massed practice and cramming improve immediate performance more than long-term retention.
- Spaced retrieval and interleaved practice feel more difficult because they require reconstruction and discrimination.

Common misreadings:
- Harder always means better.
- Passive review never has any place.

Specific applications:
- Close the notes and try to reconstruct the key ideas from memory.
- Judge a study session by what survives later, not by how smooth it felt while looking at the material.
- Mix and delay practice to expose what still needs work.

Hard-edge requirement:
- Hard mode must keep the distinction between desirable difficulty and useless struggle visible.

Threshold question:
- If effortful practice works better, what makes a difficulty productive instead of merely frustrating?
