Core Claim:
This chapter argues that mixing and varying practice often produces stronger discrimination and transfer than keeping one skill or problem type blocked into a single uninterrupted run. Blocked practice can make performance feel smooth because the learner keeps using the same move. Interleaving and variation feel messier because the learner must identify which tool fits which situation. The chapter's job is to show that this harder-feeling design improves flexible learning rather than merely making practice less comfortable. It also needs to connect mixed practice to real decisions about when to switch, compare, and choose.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
The chapter is not a generic defense of variety. It has to make the discrimination mechanism visible: mixed practice helps learners tell similar cases apart and select the right response under changing conditions. If it turns into a broad argument for novelty or randomness, it loses the chapter's real value.

Author Logic Chain:
First the authors show why blocked practice creates a misleading sense of progress.
Then they explain how interleaving and variation force the learner to notice differences and choose the right move.
Then they argue that this messier practice improves later transfer and flexible performance.
Then they connect mixed practice to concrete training and study design.

Required Anchors:
- Blocked practice can make a single skill feel easier while weakening later choice and transfer.
- Interleaving helps learners discriminate between similar problems or responses.
- Variation and mixing can produce slower-feeling practice that leads to stronger flexible performance.

Frameworks or Terms Introduced:
- interleaving: mixing related but distinct problems or skills during practice
- blocked practice: repeating one type in a continuous block
- discrimination: recognizing which method fits which problem
- transfer: carrying learning into a changed setting

Specific Applications:
- Mix problem types during review instead of solving one full block at a time.
- Vary practice examples so the learner has to identify which response fits.
- Design training sessions that require comparison and selection, not only repetition.

Common Misreadings:
- Mixing means random chaos without structure.
- Blocked practice has no use at any stage.

Counterarguments or Limits:
The chapter cannot claim that mixing should replace all early scaffolding. The narrower claim is that learners need enough variation and interleaving to build discrimination and flexible selection. It also cannot imply that messier practice always works without explanation; the mechanism has to stay visible.
