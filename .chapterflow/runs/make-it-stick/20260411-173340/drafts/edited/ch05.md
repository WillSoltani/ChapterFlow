# Avoid Illusions of Knowing

Familiarity can impersonate knowledge. A passage looks known because the learner has seen it again, the highlight marks feel reassuring, and the next reread moves smoothly. This chapter argues that those feelings are weak evidence. A learner can recognize the material and still fail to retrieve, explain, or apply it once the page is gone.

That is why rereading and highlighting are treated as poor judges of mastery when they stand alone. The answer stays visible while the learner is trying to decide whether it is learned. Recognition props up confidence. The result is an illusion of knowing: certainty that has not survived an honest check.

Calibration gets stronger when the learner has to produce something unaided. A closed-book explanation, a delayed self-quiz, or a transfer prompt forces knowledge to stand on its own. Those checks feel less flattering because they expose gaps. They are more trustworthy for the same reason.

So the chapter makes confidence answer to evidence. Honest checks do not just reveal weakness; they also help repair it. The learner stops asking whether the page looks familiar and starts asking whether the idea can be retrieved, explained, and used without help. That bridge matters because the next chapter turns toward another popular but misleading explanation for learning problems: learning styles.
