Quit Social Media pushes the book's standards into the digital environment. Earlier chapters established that deep work requires concentration, training, and deliberate structure. This chapter asks what happens when optional tools keep claiming slices of attention by default. Newport's answer is that most people use a standard that is far too permissive. They ask whether a tool is useful at all. Because the answer is often yes in some narrow sense, the tool stays. The chapter argues that this logic is lazy and expensive.

The craftsman mindset raises the bar. A tool should be judged by whether it makes a substantial contribution to something that genuinely matters. That is a stricter test than generic usefulness because it keeps priorities in view. The existence of some upside is not enough. The upside has to be meaningful relative to the goals the reader actually cares about.

Once that standard is in place, tool costs become harder to ignore. Platforms do not merely occupy isolated moments. They create habits of checking, inject fragmentation into thought, and compete against better uses of attention. A small benefit can therefore still be a poor bargain if it keeps charging a daily tax on focus. The chapter is powerful because it forces those opportunity costs into the open.

This is also why strategic subtraction matters. Newport does not frame refusal as fear, backwardness, or moral purity. He frames it as selectivity. People who care about deep work should be willing to remove tools whose contribution is weak compared with their cost. The decision is not anti-technology. It is pro-priority.

By the end, the reader is left with a sharper question than before: what important thing does this tool strongly support, and what does it keep taking in return? Chapter 7 reconnects digital life to deliberate judgment. If a platform cannot justify its hold on attention under that judgment, it should not remain in the foreground by default.

The chapter also makes an important social move. It refuses the pressure to treat universal participation as maturity. Not joining every platform, or sharply limiting one, does not mean a person is hiding from reality. It may mean they are finally pricing attention correctly. That is what makes the craftsman standard so strong. It gives the reader a language for deliberate refusal that is grounded in priorities, not in panic or image management. That turns the chapter into a broader lesson about ownership of attention. A person who cannot say no to weak digital claims will struggle to keep saying yes to deeper priorities. Tool selection is therefore part of life design, not just preference management. The point is not abstinence for its own sake. The point is that priorities become easier to honor when weak claims on attention stop being treated like permanent rights. That stricter threshold is what gives the chapter its practical bite. Weak claims have to go. The rule has teeth. That is the standard now.
