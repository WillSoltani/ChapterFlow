Chapter 7 applies the book's logic to digital tools. Newport is not interested in blanket rejection of technology. His target is passive adoption. Many people let new platforms into their lives because the tools are popular, convenient, or mildly useful. The chapter argues that this standard is too weak for anyone who wants to protect deep work. Optional tools should have to earn their place.

The craftsman mindset provides the test. Instead of asking whether a tool offers any benefit at all, the reader should ask whether it substantially supports something important. That distinction matters because almost every platform can make some claim on usefulness. The harder question is whether the gain is strong enough to justify the recurring cost in time, fragmentation, and mental pull.

Those costs are central to the chapter. A tool is never competing against nothing. It is competing against alternative uses of the same finite attention. That means a small convenience may still be a bad trade if it keeps inserting itself into the day, pulling the mind toward shallow checking, or reducing the room available for serious work and meaningful leisure.

The chapter therefore treats subtraction as a disciplined choice rather than as technophobia. Saying no to an optional platform can be a sign that priorities are finally being defended. The practical lesson is not to hate technology. It is to stop letting popularity and habit decide which tools get permanent access to your mind.

This makes the chapter useful well beyond social media itself. It is really about standards for admission. Anything that wants recurring access to your mind should face a more serious test than habit or trend. Once the reader adopts that posture, technology stops being a stream of defaults and becomes a series of choices that can be accepted, limited, or refused. Once that standard is active, default digital life starts looking less neutral and much more expensive. That is the chapter's stricter freedom. It is demanding in the right way.
