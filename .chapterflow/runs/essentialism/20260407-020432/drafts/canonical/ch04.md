Trade-Off: Which Problem Do I Want? is built around a simple claim: trade-offs are real, and the essentialist stops asking how to do both and starts choosing which problem is worth carrying. McKeown uses the chapter to expose how people waste time trying to escape the cost built into every meaningful choice. People often tolerate that pattern because it comes wrapped in good intentions, ambition, duty, or convenience.

The answer is to name the trade-off openly and choose the burden attached to what matters most. This is the operative skill of the chapter. Nonessentialists ask how to do both, while essentialists ask which problem they want. The better question is not what must be sacrificed, but what deserves disproportionate investment. The reader is being pushed away from vague preference and toward a clear operating rule.

The stakes are practical, not cosmetic. until trade-offs are accepted, priorities stay theoretical and overcommitment keeps winning.
