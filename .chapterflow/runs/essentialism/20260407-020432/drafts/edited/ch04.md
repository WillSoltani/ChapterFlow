Trade-Off: Which Problem Do I Want? sharpens the book by attacking a pattern most people keep excusing. people waste time trying to escape the cost built into every meaningful choice. McKeown's counterpunch is to name the trade-off openly and choose the burden attached to what matters most. The chapter insists that trade-offs are real, and the essentialist stops asking how to do both and starts choosing which problem is worth carrying.

That claim lands because the chapter keeps moving from diagnosis into rule. Nonessentialists ask how to do both, while essentialists ask which problem they want. The better question is not what must be sacrificed, but what deserves disproportionate investment. Instead of giving the reader one more slogan to admire, it gives them a harder standard to obey.

The stakes are obvious once the chapter is taken seriously: until trade-offs are accepted, priorities stay theoretical and overcommitment keeps winning. It also advances the architecture of the book because the chapter converts discernment into commitment by forcing the cost of choice into the open. The chapter keeps pressure on the reader by refusing to hide the cost: the reader wants expansion without loss, but essentialism insists that every yes already contains a no. McKeown is trying to make the principle operational before the reader meets the next layer of the system.
