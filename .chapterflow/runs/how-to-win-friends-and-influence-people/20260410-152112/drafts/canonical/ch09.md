People rarely warm to us merely because we seem polished, witty, or eager to be liked. Carnegie's next move is sharper than charm. People tend to warm quickly when they can feel that they matter to you.

That effect depends on visible regard. Respect that stays private has less social force than respect that becomes legible in the exchange. When another person can feel seen as important, goodwill appears faster because the interaction stops feeling like a contest for attention.

This is why the chapter does not need flattery to work. It does not praise inflated compliments or generic niceness. It praises truthful signals of importance that fit the person in front of you. The warmth is faster precisely because the respect feels real.

The chapter also explains why self-presentation is weaker than many people assume. You may come across as capable or appealing and still fail to create quick liking if the other person cannot tell what regard you hold for that person. Visible appreciation changes the social temperature more directly than self-display does.
