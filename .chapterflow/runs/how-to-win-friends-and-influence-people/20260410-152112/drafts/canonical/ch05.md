People begin reading first impressions before conversation has really started. They notice the face, the ease or tension in it, and whether the first contact feels inviting or closed. Carnegie's next move is to make that early social signal visible. A good first impression often begins with visible warmth.

That matters because expression reaches another person before explanation does. Long before anyone understands your intention, the other person is already feeling whether the exchange seems easy to enter. A warm expression can lower strain quickly. It can make the other person feel received instead of merely assessed.

The chapter stays stronger when it is kept narrow. The point is not to wear friendliness like a costume. It is to let real welcome become visible. If the face stays guarded, flat, or preoccupied, the room often feels a little harder before any words have a chance to repair it.

That is why forced brightness usually fails. People can often sense the difference between ease and effort, between a human expression and one that is being pushed into place for effect. Carnegie is not recommending a sales smile. He is pointing toward sincerity that becomes readable on the surface.

A limit keeps the chapter credible. Visible warmth is not an order to grin constantly or to perform a personality that is not yours. The principle works only when the expression grows out of genuine good will. Otherwise the first impression may become polished, but it will not become trustworthy.
