People may comply under pressure, but durable influence usually needs more than compliance. Carnegie closes the book by saying action lasts better when the other person can feel some motive, pride, or self-respect inside it.

That is why this chapter asks how to make people glad to do what you want. The move is not simply to get agreement by any means. It is to align the action with something in the other person that can endorse it from within.

The chapter becomes weak if this turns into total manipulation doctrine. The useful version keeps an ethical tension visible. Influence can be shrewd without becoming a license to override the other person's reality or interests.

This matters because resentful compliance is brittle. A person who feels used may obey once and resist later. A person who can see pride, purpose, or self-story in the action is more likely to sustain it.

So the finale is not asking for a universal trick. It is synthesizing the book's larger pattern. Influence works best when it respects motive, dignity, and the other person's need to act in a way they can live with.
