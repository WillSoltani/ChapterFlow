Criticism can sound like superiority even when the point is valid. Carnegie says one way to reduce that pressure is to admit your own mistakes first.

That move matters because it changes the posture of the conversation. The speaker is no longer standing outside error and pointing downward. The correction starts inside shared fallibility, which can lower defensiveness and make the other person more willing to listen.

But the chapter does not want confession for its own sake. Self-disclosure only helps when it is brief, relevant, and functional. If the speaker wanders into performance, autobiography, or attention-seeking, the method loses force.

The useful version admits a real failure and then turns toward the issue that needs correction. The point is not to take over the conversation. It is to remove some accusation while keeping the standard visible.

That balance matters because humility can open the room, but only clarity can still direct change. The chapter works when both are present.
