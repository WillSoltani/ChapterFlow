People often react to the other person's position as if the words on the surface are the whole problem. Carnegie offers a more useful formula. Ask what the person thinks they are protecting or pursuing.

That move matters because arguments often ride on hidden motives. Someone may sound stubborn, defensive, or difficult while actually trying to guard status, avoid loss, preserve dignity, or reach a goal they think is at risk. The position becomes easier to work with once the motive becomes more legible.

This is why the chapter treats perspective-taking as concrete rather than sentimental. The task is not to feel warmly about the other person in general. It is to ask a sharper question about what they believe they are doing. Understanding that internal logic gives the conversation more traction.

The chapter also keeps a necessary limit in place. Seeing the motive does not mean approving it. You can understand what the other person thinks they are protecting without surrendering your own judgment about whether the move is wise, fair, or true.

That leads naturally into the next chapter. Once motives are easier to read, sympathy for the other person's ideas and desires can become more precise instead of vague.
