People resist orders not only because of the content but because orders press directly on agency. Carnegie says questions often work better because they preserve dignity while still moving action.

That matters because a question can invite participation, thought, and ownership. Instead of being pushed from the outside, the other person has more room to step into the decision. That often produces better buy-in.

But the chapter does not pretend questions solve everything. Some situations still require speed, clarity, and direct instruction. The point is not to erase authority. It is to use authority in a way that does not create unnecessary resistance when time and context allow a better route.

The method also becomes weak if the question is fake. A disguised order wearing a question mark does not preserve dignity for long. The other person can still feel controlled.

The useful version asks in a way that leaves real room for thought or contribution while still keeping direction visible. That balance is the chapter.

This leads naturally into the next chapter. Once direction is handled with more dignity, the book can ask how to let the other person save face when things go wrong.
