People do not become enemies only because they disagree with us. They become enemies more easily when disagreement arrives carrying superiority. Carnegie's next move is to show that certainty can fail not because it is clear, but because it can sound like contempt.

That matters because people defend dignity as fiercely as they defend opinions. Once your tone tells another person that the person is plainly beneath your judgment, the exchange stops being about the issue alone. It becomes a fight over standing.

This is why the chapter treats blunt certainty as dangerous. A true point carried with superiority often produces more resistance than movement. The person may hear your claim and still reject your posture hard enough to reject you with it.

The chapter does not ask for fake uncertainty or weak thinking. It asks for conviction without belittlement. Humility here is not passivity. It is the refusal to make another person's smallness the price of your correctness.

That move leads naturally into the next chapter. If superiority creates enemies, what happens when the fault is actually yours and a quick admission removes the other person's appetite for attack?
