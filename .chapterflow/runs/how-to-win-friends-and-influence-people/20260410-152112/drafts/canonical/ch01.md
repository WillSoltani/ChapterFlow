Two-Gun Crowley was surrounded, armed, and headed toward the electric chair, yet he still saw himself as the injured party. Carnegie opens there for a reason. The point is not that criminals are strange. The point is that even people whose guilt looks obvious from the outside often keep a private story in which they are justified, cornered, or misunderstood. Al Capone did it. Dutch Schultz did it. Crowley did it. Once that pattern is visible, the chapter stops being about gangsters and starts being about everyone who hears blame as an attack on identity before hearing it as a correction of conduct.

That is why criticism so often fails even when it is deserved. The critic feels clear, factual, even righteous. The target feels cut, exposed, and pushed toward self-defense. Pride moves faster than reflection. A person who has just been condemned usually starts gathering reasons, not lessons. Carnegie's opening move is sharper than a plea for politeness. He is saying that fault-finding is weak at producing willing change because it invites resentment first. The critic gets the hot satisfaction of saying the truth out loud. The other person gets the colder urge to protect a self-image that now feels under siege.

Lincoln's near-letter to General Meade is the chapter's clearest test of that claim. After Gettysburg, Lincoln believed Meade had a chance to crush Lee's army and end the war sooner. He was furious that the chance had slipped away. He wrote the rebuke. He had the argument. He had history on his side. But he did not send it. Carnegie treats that restraint as more intelligent than the criticism itself. Lincoln could have emptied his anger onto the page and gained the temporary relief of saying exactly what he thought. He also might have hardened the very man he still needed. That is the chapter's turning point: being correct about failure does not automatically tell you what response will produce the best next outcome.

That is a hard distinction for proud people to accept because criticism feels useful from the inside. It feels decisive. It feels honest. Sometimes it even feels brave. But the chapter keeps exposing the same defect. Criticism often serves the emotions of the speaker better than the future conduct of the listener. If the other person walks away replaying the humiliation, the correction has already lost force. The scene with Lincoln matters because it shows restraint as a form of discipline, not softness. He was not confused about the mistake. He was refusing to let his need to condemn outrun his real objective.

Bob Hoover gives the same principle a different kind of pressure. A mechanic's mistake nearly killed him by putting the wrong fuel in his plane. Hoover had every moral right to explode. Instead he found the man wrecked by his own error and responded without crushing him. Carnegie uses that moment to show something criticism misses: people often punish themselves more deeply than we could. Add humiliation to that state and you do not create care. You create fear, paralysis, and private bitterness. Hoover's restraint did not say the error was small. It said the next goal was reliability, not emotional revenge.

The domestic version is quieter and, for most readers, closer. Father Forgets hurts because it shows how easily correction turns into atmosphere. A parent keeps noticing the child's faults, manners, noise, delay, clumsiness, carelessness. None of the individual complaints sounds outrageous by itself. The damage comes from accumulation. A home can become a place where one person is always being measured and found wanting. Carnegie's point lands hardest there. Criticism is not only a tool people use in moments of major failure. It can become a steady habit of diminishing other people while telling ourselves we are only being accurate.

The chapter does not prove that standards should disappear. Some wrongs still need boundaries, consequences, or removal from trust. Carnegie's claim is narrower and more useful than that. If your goal is influence, criticism is usually a poor opening move because it makes pride brace for impact. The reader leaves with a negative principle first: do not begin by criticizing, condemning, or complaining. The next question opens naturally from there. If blame is such a weak lever, what positive force actually moves people?
