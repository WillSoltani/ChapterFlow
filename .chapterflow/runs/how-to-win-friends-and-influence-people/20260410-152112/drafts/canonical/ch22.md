Criticism often fails before the corrective point is even spoken. Carnegie says the opening emotional frame matters. If fault must be named, the beginning shapes whether the person braces or listens.

That is why the chapter starts with praise. Sincere recognition can lower defensiveness and remind the other person that the conversation is not built on pure attack. The point is not to decorate criticism. It is to change the emotional condition in which criticism is heard.

The chapter becomes weak if praise turns fake. Empty compliments, manipulative sweetness, or mechanical compliment sandwiches make the listener suspicious. Praise only helps when it is specific, honest, and connected to something real.

That also means correction cannot disappear. The method is not avoidance. It is a way of making the criticism hearable without removing its substance. The standard still has to be named.

The useful sequence is therefore respect first, correction second. When the person feels seen before they feel faulted, resentment has less reason to dominate the exchange.

This leads naturally into the next chapter. Once the emotional frame is steadier, the book can ask how to criticize more indirectly without making the point vague or cowardly.
