People do not only choose with facts. They also choose with stories about who they are. Carnegie says a better appeal often works by giving the person a nobler motive they can recognize in themselves.

That matters because most people like to see themselves well. They want their action to fit some version of fairness, responsibility, loyalty, dignity, or generosity. When a choice can honestly connect to that higher picture, it becomes easier to inhabit.

This is why the chapter is not just about flattery. It is about self-story. A nobler-motive appeal changes the meaning of the action in the person's own mind. The move becomes stronger when the higher motive is believable rather than preachy.

The chapter also keeps a needed limit in place. People are not pure, and motives are usually mixed. Carnegie is not asking us to pretend otherwise. The point is to offer the better motive as a real path, not to moralize the person into performance.

That leads naturally into the next chapter. Once a better story is available, dramatizing the idea can make that story felt more strongly.
