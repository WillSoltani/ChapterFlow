People do not only need correction. They also need signs that progress is happening. Carnegie treats small improvements as leverage because noticed gains can change effort from something fragile into something self-sustaining.

That is why this chapter turns to praise. The praise here is not flattery and not mood management. It is a way of marking real movement early enough that the other person feels improvement is possible and worth continuing.

The chapter becomes weak if praise is detached from evidence. Empty encouragement can sound manipulative or unserious. The useful version names an actual gain, however small, and lets that gain become proof that the next gain is reachable.

This matters because people often quit when change feels invisible. Small progress, clearly noticed, can lower discouragement and create momentum. The person is no longer working in a vacuum of uncertainty.

So the chapter is not asking for sweetness. It is asking for credible reinforcement. Praise should make effort feel consequential, not imaginary.

This leads naturally into the next chapter. Once progress is noticed, the book can ask how a good reputation gives people an identity they will try to honor.
