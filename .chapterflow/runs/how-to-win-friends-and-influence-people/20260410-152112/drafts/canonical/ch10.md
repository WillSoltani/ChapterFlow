Arguments often feel winnable because facts, logic, and speed can make one side look stronger in the moment. Carnegie's title cuts against that confidence. You may win the point and still lose the practical outcome.

That is because arguments usually harden the very person you hope to influence. Once the exchange turns into a contest, being defeated rarely makes someone more open. It more often makes the person defensive, embarrassed, or determined to hold position.

This is why the chapter treats argument as a trap rather than as a clean path to persuasion. The ego starts caring about victory, and the relationship starts paying the price. A person can leave the exchange officially wrong yet emotionally less reachable than before.

The chapter does not say truth is irrelevant or that every disagreement should be abandoned. It says argument is often the wrong vehicle for changing minds because it makes public defeat more important than real movement. Strategic restraint is different from surrender.

That move opens Part Three cleanly. If open argument often creates resistance instead of influence, what kind of certainty turns ordinary disagreement into active enmity?
