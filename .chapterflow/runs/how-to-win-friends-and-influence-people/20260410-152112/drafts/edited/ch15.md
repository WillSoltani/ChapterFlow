People often respond to complaints as if the fastest path is to correct them immediately. Carnegie asks us to notice the pressure inside the complaint first. When people feel cut off too early, the complaint can intensify rather than weaken.

That is why the chapter uses the image of a safety valve. Letting the other person air the grievance can release some of the heat that makes the exchange combative. The point is not surrender. It is de-escalation before correction.

The mistake is to treat every complaint as a debate that must be answered in its first seconds. Early defense can sound like refusal to hear, and refusal to hear can harden the other person's insistence. A complaint that has not been allowed to breathe often comes back with more force.

The chapter also keeps a limit in place. Hearing a complaint is not the same as granting that it is fully right. The valve matters because pressure drops enough for the conversation to become workable again, not because truth no longer matters.

That sets up the next move in the book. Once grievance pressure has room to release, cooperation becomes easier to imagine without feeling coerced.
