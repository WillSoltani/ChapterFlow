Correction can be deserved and still be delivered badly. Carnegie says humiliation poisons cooperation. If the other person is stripped of dignity, the relationship often absorbs damage that outlasts the mistake itself.

That is why the chapter argues for saving face. Letting the other person retain some dignity does not mean pretending the problem is unreal. It means refusing to make shame itself part of the punishment when correction or loss is already painful enough.

The chapter becomes weak if face-saving is confused with excuse-making. Accountability still matters. Standards, consequences, and honest acknowledgment of the problem do not disappear.

The useful version keeps the correction real while avoiding needless public diminishment. It protects the possibility of future cooperation by not turning the moment into humiliation.

That matters because people often resist, hide, or harden after disgrace. Preserve dignity and there is still room for recovery, learning, and continued work.

This leads naturally into the next chapter. Once dignity is protected after error, the book can ask how praise for small gains turns improvement into momentum.
