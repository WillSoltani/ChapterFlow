Politeness can still feel generic. You can greet someone correctly, speak kindly, and still leave the person feeling only loosely seen. Carnegie's next move is to tighten that gap. Remembering and using a person's name makes the interaction more personal because it signals recognition rather than generic handling.

That matters because names are not just labels in this chapter. They are one of the clearest signs that another person exists distinctly in your attention. A name says more than courtesy. It says the other person did not slide past you as an interchangeable figure in the room.

The chapter stays stronger when it is kept away from memory-showmanship. The point is not to impress people with recall. The point is to make them feel specifically received. That is why forgetting a name can create more distance than the lapse itself seems to deserve. What is missed is not only a word. What is missed is the feeling of being personally recognized.

A boundary keeps the idea credible. Using a name too heavily can sound mechanical, salesy, or staged. Carnegie is not recommending verbal overperformance. He is pointing toward regard that becomes audible.

That move leads naturally into the next chapter. Once another person feels personally recognized, what kind of conversation keeps that person engaged rather than merely acknowledged?
