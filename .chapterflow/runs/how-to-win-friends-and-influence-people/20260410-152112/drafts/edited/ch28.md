People often act partly in response to the name attached to them. Carnegie treats a good reputation as a lever because people may try to live up to an identity that feels both available and believable.

That is why this chapter focuses on giving someone a good name. The move is not mystical or magical. It works when the offered reputation is credible enough to feel like a standard the person can inhabit rather than a slogan pasted over reality.

The chapter becomes weak if reputation is treated like branding magic. Empty labels can feel manipulative, false, or socially performative. The useful version points toward a real trait, capacity, or direction that the person can plausibly step into.

That matters because identity can stabilize progress already underway. Once a person begins to see themselves as careful, reliable, or capable, that self-story can support better choices. The name becomes a scaffold instead of a costume.

So the chapter is not asking for flattery. It is asking for an offered reputation that invites conduct upward. The method fails if the identity is dishonest, theatrical, or detached from evidence.

This leads naturally into the next chapter. Once a better identity is available, the book can ask how to make faults feel easier to correct instead of crushing people under them.
