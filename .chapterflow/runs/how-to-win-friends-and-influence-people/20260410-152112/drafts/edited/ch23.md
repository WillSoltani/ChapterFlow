People often resist criticism not only because of the fault itself but because of what direct fault does to pride. Carnegie says criticism can sometimes work better when it is delivered indirectly.

The point is dignity, not fog. Indirect correction can reduce the sting of being singled out and give the other person more room to adjust without feeling publicly struck. That can lower resentment and keep the relationship steadier.

But the chapter draws a limit immediately. Indirectness cannot become cowardice, vagueness, or evasive hinting. If the problem disappears into politeness, the method stops helping because nothing clear remains to change.

The useful version keeps the fault legible while softening unnecessary offense. It chooses tact without deleting meaning. That balance is the whole chapter.

This matters because criticism has two jobs at once. It has to preserve enough dignity for the person to stay open, and it has to preserve enough clarity for the correction to matter.

That leads naturally into the next chapter. Once criticism is less accusatory in tone, the book can go further by having the speaker admit their own mistakes first.
