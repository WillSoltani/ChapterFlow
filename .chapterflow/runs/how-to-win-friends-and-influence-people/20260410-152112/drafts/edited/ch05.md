First impressions begin before conversation has fully started. People read the face early. They notice ease or strain, openness or distance, before they understand much of what is being said. Carnegie's fifth chapter makes that first-contact signal concrete. A good first impression often starts with visible warmth.

That matters because expression reaches another person before explanation does. Before your reasons are clear, the other person is already feeling whether the exchange seems easy to enter. A warm expression can lower strain quickly. It can make the other person feel received instead of measured.

The chapter stays useful only if it stays narrow. The point is not to paste friendliness onto the face. The point is to let real welcome show. When expression stays guarded, flat, or preoccupied, the room can feel a little colder before words have a chance to help.

That is why forced brightness usually fails. People often sense the difference between ease and effort, between a human expression and one that is being pushed into place for effect. Carnegie is not recommending a sales smile. He is pointing toward sincerity that becomes visible.

A boundary keeps the chapter credible. Visible warmth is not an order to grin constantly or to perform a personality that is not yours. The principle works only when expression grows out of genuine good will. Otherwise the first impression may look polished without becoming trustworthy.
