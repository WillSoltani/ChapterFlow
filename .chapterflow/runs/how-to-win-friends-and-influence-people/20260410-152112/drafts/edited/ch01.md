Two-Gun Crowley was armed, surrounded, and headed toward the electric chair, yet he still saw himself as justified. Carnegie opens there to destroy a comforting illusion. The illusion is that obvious fault produces obvious self-knowledge. It usually does not. Crowley kept a private story in which he was the wronged party. Al Capone did the same. Dutch Schultz did the same. Once that pattern is visible, the chapter stops being about gangsters and starts being about the ordinary human habit of hearing blame as an attack on identity before hearing it as a correction of conduct.

That is why criticism so often fails even when it is deserved. The critic feels clear, factual, even righteous. The target feels cut, exposed, and pushed toward self-defense. Pride moves faster than reflection. A person who has just been condemned usually starts gathering reasons, not lessons. Carnegie's opening move is sharper than a plea for manners. Fault-finding is weak at producing willing change because it wounds pride first and invites resentment immediately. The critic gets the relief of saying the truth out loud. The other person gets the urge to protect a self-image that now feels under attack.

Lincoln's unsent rebuke to General Meade is the chapter's strongest proof. After Gettysburg, Lincoln believed Meade had let a decisive chance escape. He wrote the condemnation. He had the grievance. He had history on his side. But he did not send it. Carnegie treats that restraint as wiser than the criticism itself. Lincoln could have satisfied his anger and still damaged the very man he needed. That is the chapter's central distinction. Being correct about failure does not automatically tell you what response will produce the best next result.

This is hard to accept because criticism feels productive from the inside. It feels decisive, honest, and morally clean. The chapter keeps showing the defect anyway. Criticism often serves the emotions of the speaker better than the future conduct of the listener. If the other person leaves replaying humiliation, the correction has already lost force. Lincoln matters here because he shows restraint as discipline rather than softness. He was not confused about the mistake. He was refusing to let his need to condemn outrun his actual objective.

Bob Hoover brings the same logic into a more modern kind of failure. A mechanic's mistake nearly killed him by putting the wrong fuel in his plane. Hoover had every reason to explode. Instead he found a man already shattered by what he had done and responded without crushing him. Carnegie uses that moment to expose what criticism misses. People often punish themselves more deeply than we can. Add humiliation to that state and you do not create care. You create fear, paralysis, or silent bitterness. Hoover's restraint did not make the error trivial. It kept the focus on future reliability instead of emotional revenge.

The domestic version is quieter and, for most readers, closer to home. Father Forgets hurts because it shows how easily correction becomes atmosphere. A parent keeps noticing the child's noise, delay, clumsiness, or bad manners. None of the complaints sounds monstrous alone. The damage comes from accumulation. A home becomes a place where one person is always being measured and found wanting. Carnegie's point lands hardest there. Criticism is not only a weapon for large failures. It can become a daily habit of diminishing someone while the critic keeps telling himself he is only being accurate.

The chapter does not say standards vanish. Some wrongs still require boundaries, consequences, or removal from trust. Carnegie's claim is narrower and more strategic than that. If your goal is influence, criticism is usually a poor opening move because it makes pride brace for impact. The first principle therefore arrives as a negative command: do not begin by criticizing, condemning, or complaining. Once that lands, the next question becomes unavoidable. If blame is such a weak lever, what positive force actually moves people?
