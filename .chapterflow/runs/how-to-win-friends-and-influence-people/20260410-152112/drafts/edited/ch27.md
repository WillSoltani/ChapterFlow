People do not need correction alone. They also need evidence that progress is real. Carnegie treats small improvements as leverage because noticed gains can change effort from something fragile into something more self-sustaining.

That is why this chapter turns to praise. The praise here is not flattery, mood management, or generic positivity. It is a way of marking real movement early enough that the other person starts to believe continued improvement is possible.

The chapter becomes weak if praise is detached from evidence. Empty encouragement can sound manipulative, unserious, or merely polite. The useful version names an actual gain, however small, and lets that gain become proof that the next gain is reachable.

That matters because people often stall when change feels invisible. Small progress, clearly noticed, can lower discouragement and create momentum. Effort stops feeling pointless when improvement is made visible.

So the chapter is not asking for sweetness. It is asking for credible reinforcement. Praise should make effort feel consequential rather than imaginary.

This leads naturally into the next chapter. Once progress is noticed, the book can ask how a good reputation gives people an identity they will try to honor.
