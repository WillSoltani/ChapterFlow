When we are wrong, our first impulse is often to defend ourselves before the other person can press harder. Carnegie argues that this instinct usually gives the conflict more fuel. A fast, honest admission can be stronger than self-protection.

That is because attack often lives on resistance. Once the fault is openly owned, the other person has less reason to keep proving it. The exchange can start moving toward repair instead of staying trapped in accusation and defense.

This is why the chapter treats quick admission as a power move rather than as weakness. Defensiveness can create extra conflict after the original mistake is already visible. Honest self-correction can shorten the struggle by removing the need for combat.

The chapter does not ask for false confession or dramatic self-abasement. It asks for truthful ownership. Admission works when it is real enough to clear the air, not theatrical enough to become a different kind of performance.
