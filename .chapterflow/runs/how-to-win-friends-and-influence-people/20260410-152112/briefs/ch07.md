Core Claim:
The chapter argues that good conversation comes less from saying impressive things than from listening in a way that makes the other person want to continue. People open when they feel heard and invited into topics that matter to them. The point is not passive silence. It is active attention that creates conversational room.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
Chapter 6 made welcome more personal through names. Chapter 7 turns that recognition into the ongoing texture of conversation. It cannot become generic small-talk advice, interview technique, or a bag of question prompts. Its distinct work is to show that listening is often the strongest conversational move.

Author Logic Chain:
First the chapter assumes that people stay engaged when conversation touches their own interest and experience.
Then it shows that listening and inviting the other person forward is often more powerful than trying to dominate the talk.
Then it keeps the chapter from thinning out by insisting that questions without real attention do not count as good conversation.

Frameworks or Terms Introduced:
- conversational room: the sense that another person has space to unfold what matters
- active listening invitation: attention that encourages the other person to continue
- question-without-attention failure: asking things without truly receiving the answer

Specific Applications:
- helping meetings, classes, and social gatherings feel less competitive and more open
- asking follow-up questions that come from what the other person just said
- noticing when you are waiting to talk rather than actually listening

Common Misreadings:
- treating the chapter as silence for silence's sake
- reducing it to a list of stock questions
- assuming good conversation means never sharing anything about yourself

Counterarguments or Limits:
Not every conversation should be one-sided, and listening can become thin if it never develops into real exchange. The prose should keep the chapter balanced: the point is not disappearance but attention strong enough to let the other person unfold.
