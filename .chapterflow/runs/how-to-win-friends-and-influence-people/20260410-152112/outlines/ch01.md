Chapter Promise:
Show why criticism so often fails even when the critic is correct, and why restraint protects influence better than blame does.

Opening Move:
Open on Two-Gun Crowley still seeing himself as justified while the outside world sees only a killer.

Anchor Allocation:
- anchor 1 used in paragraph(s): P1-P2 -> Crowley, Capone, and Schultz as self-justification cases
- anchor 2 used in paragraph(s): P3-P4 -> Lincoln and Meade as the strongest restraint example
- anchor 3 used in paragraph(s): P5-P6 -> Bob Hoover and Father Forgets as modern and domestic pressure tests

Paragraph Job Map:
- P1: startling opening case of obvious wrongdoing with zero inner guilt
- P2: widen from criminals to ordinary human pride and resentment
- P3: Lincoln wants to condemn Meade
- P4: Lincoln's restraint reveals the difference between emotional discharge and real influence
- P5: Bob Hoover example shows correction without humiliation
- P6: family-life cost through Father Forgets and the ordinary cruelty of constant fault-finding
- P7: preserve the limit that standards still matter and bridge toward appreciation as the stronger lever

Required Distinction or Mechanism:
Being right is not the same thing as being effective. Criticism often satisfies the critic's need to judge while making the other person more committed to self-defense.

Hard-Depth Tension:
If blame backfires, how do you correct dangerous or consequential behavior without drifting into softness, silence, or fake approval?

Threshold Question:
When does restraint preserve influence, and when does a situation still require direct correction or consequence?

Takeaway Count Lock:
- Easy: 3 (always)
- Medium: 5
- Hard: 5

Hard Takeaway Topics:
- self-justification survives obvious fault
- criticism wounds pride before it changes conduct
- restraint can preserve leverage better than scolding
- refusing humiliation is not the same thing as lowering standards
- correction still needs a boundary when harm is real

Scenario Lesson Map:
- Scenario 1 (decision_point, work): whether to publicly correct a teammate who already knows they failed
- Scenario 2 (postmortem, school): debate-team feedback that became personal instead of useful
- Scenario 3 (dialogue, personal): roommate conflict where blame keeps replaying the same argument
- Scenario 4 (predict_reveal, work): manager opening a performance conversation with accusation and losing the room immediately
- Scenario 5 (dilemma, school): student editor deciding how to address repeated mistakes without humiliating a writer
- Scenario 6 (before_after, personal): family correction delivered first as blame and later as steadier restraint

Scenario Vividness Targets:
For at least 4 of 6 scenarios, include a marked-up draft, a Slack thread, a dorm sink full of dishes, a debate ballot, a conference-room glass wall, or a family kitchen counter.

Preview Constraint:
The preview must create a live prediction about what positive force can move people if criticism usually fails.

Genericity Risks:
- turning the chapter into generic kindness language
- making criticism sound ineffective only because it is rude
- forgetting that the real mechanism is pride under attack

Forbidden Drift:
- no pop-psych diagnosis language
- no management-book cliches about feedback culture
- no "never criticize anyone ever" absolutism
- no brief-language leakage into reader-facing prose
