Most messages do not fail because they are false. They fail because they arrive in the exact shape people expected, which lets the mind slide past them without noticing. That is why the chapter turns to surprise so quickly. A pattern break forces attention because it tells the audience that the mental script they were running no longer fits. For a second, the world becomes unstable enough to look at again.

But the chapter does not celebrate shock for its own sake. Surprise works when it points back to the core. A strange opener that cannot explain itself after the jolt is not memorable in the useful sense. It is random. The chapter's standard is harder: the surprise should feel post-predictable. Once the point lands, the audience should feel that the opening could not have been built any other way.

That is why the buried-lead lesson matters. Nora Ephron's journalism-class story survives because it exposes a common mistake: writers often spend too long warming up instead of leading with the fact that makes the room care. The issue is not impatience alone. It is that attention decays fast when the real stake stays hidden under setup. A good lead gives the audience the reason to keep reading before inertia wins.

Curiosity carries the chapter beyond the first jolt. George Loewenstein's gap theory helps explain why attention can last after surprise interrupts it. People lean forward when they can feel the shape of what they do not yet know. The key phrase there is feel the shape. A gap works only when the audience has enough context to recognize that something important is missing. Without that foothold, confusion replaces curiosity.

The chapter's bigger examples, like Sony's pocket radio and Kennedy's moonshot, make the same point at scale. Both feel surprising because they violate what seemed realistic at the time. Yet they do not float as disconnected stunts. They point toward a clear new possibility. That is why they energize attention instead of wasting it. The surprise creates a question the audience suddenly wants answered.

So unexpectedness is really a two-part discipline. First you break the pattern. Then you justify the break by showing the hidden meaning inside it. Miss the first move and the message gets ignored. Miss the second and the message becomes a gimmick. The chapter keeps both failures in view because people often overcorrect from one into the other.

The harder test, then, is not whether your opening gets a reaction. It is whether the reaction opens a live question tied to your real point. When it does, attention stops being borrowed energy and becomes curiosity. When it does not, the surprise burns hot and dies fast. Once that question is alive, the next problem is giving the audience something concrete enough to picture and hold.
