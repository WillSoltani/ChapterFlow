Most messages do not fail because they are false. They fail because they arrive in the exact shape people expected, which lets the mind slide past them without noticing. That is why the chapter turns to surprise so quickly. A pattern break forces attention because it tells the audience that the mental script they were running no longer fits.

But the chapter does not celebrate shock for its own sake. Surprise works when it points back to the core. A strange opener that cannot explain itself after the jolt is not useful. It is random. The chapter's standard is harder: the surprise should feel post-predictable. Once the point lands, the audience should feel that the opening now makes sense.

That is why the buried-lead lesson matters. Nora Ephron's journalism-class story survives because it exposes a common mistake: writers spend too long warming up instead of leading with the fact that makes the room care. Attention decays fast when the real stake stays hidden under setup. A good lead gives the audience the reason to keep reading before inertia wins.

Curiosity carries the argument beyond the first jolt. George Loewenstein's gap theory helps explain why attention can last after surprise interrupts it. People lean forward when they can feel the shape of what they do not yet know. A gap works only when the audience has enough context to recognize that something important is missing. Without that foothold, confusion replaces curiosity.

The bigger examples, like Sony's pocket radio and Kennedy's moonshot, make the same point at scale. Both feel surprising because they violate what seemed realistic at the time. Yet neither works as a disconnected stunt. Each points toward a clear new possibility. The surprise creates a question the audience suddenly wants answered.

Unexpectedness is really a two-part discipline. First you break the pattern. Then you justify the break by showing the hidden meaning inside it. Miss the first move and the message gets ignored. Miss the second and the message becomes gimmickry.

The real test is not whether your opening gets a reaction. It is whether the reaction opens a live question tied to your point. When it does, attention turns into curiosity. When it does not, the surprise burns hot and dies fast. After that, the next problem is making the idea concrete enough to picture.
