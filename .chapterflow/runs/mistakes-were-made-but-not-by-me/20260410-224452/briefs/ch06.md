Core Claim:
This chapter argues that self-justification becomes especially dangerous inside law because authority, public commitment, and moral certainty harden error into institutional force. Police, prosecutors, and other officials can jump to conclusions, interpret evidence through guilt, and then defend the case more fiercely after commitment and conviction. The practical danger is not only one wrong guess. It is a system that experiences doubt as weakness and exonerating evidence as a threat to its own legitimacy. The chapter matters because wrongful judgments can become self-protective careers and public narratives.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
This is the institutional-stakes chapter. It takes the closed-loop pattern into interrogations, prosecutions, and convictions where the cost of self-justification becomes life-altering.

Author Logic Chain:
First the authors show how interrogation and prosecution cultures reward early certainty.
Then they show how guilt assumptions shape what evidence gets noticed, what confessions get trusted, and what alternatives get ignored.
Then they show that once a conviction is won, later evidence of innocence becomes psychologically and institutionally hard to accept.

Required Anchors:
- Jumping to convictions: friction between incomplete evidence and a legal actor's early certainty; consequence is tunnel vision.
- Reid-style interrogation / confession culture: friction between deceptive interrogation methods and a suspect's vulnerability; consequence is confession pressure and false certainty.
- Post-conviction defense of the case: friction between exonerating evidence and the need to preserve institutional legitimacy; consequence is resistance to innocence claims.

Frameworks or Terms Introduced:
- tunnel vision: early certainty narrowing what counts as relevant evidence
- confession pressure: interrogation tactics creating psychological incentives to say what the authority already believes
- post-conviction self-justification: defending a verdict because reversing it threatens identity, status, and institutional face

Specific Applications:
- noticing how a chosen suspect or favored theory reshapes later evidence review
- asking what incentives make officials resist disconfirming evidence after a public commitment
- recognizing that legal certainty can be a symptom of defended identity rather than evidentiary strength

Common Misreadings:
- wrongful convictions happen mainly because of bad intentions or obvious corruption
- confessions are inherently strong evidence because people would not admit guilt falsely

Counterarguments or Limits:
The frozen bundle supports tunnel vision, interrogation pressure, and resistance to innocence claims after conviction. It does not support a detailed catalog of individual cases, forensic science debates, or a complete theory of criminal justice reform.
