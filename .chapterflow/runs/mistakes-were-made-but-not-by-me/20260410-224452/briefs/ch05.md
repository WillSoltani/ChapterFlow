Core Claim:
This chapter argues that good intentions do not rescue bad methods. Clinical and helping professions can become self-sealing when practitioners trust intuition, confirmation, and authority more than disconfirming evidence. The practical danger is that a benevolent expert can feel more certain as their method grows less reliable. The chapter matters because self-justification now enters professions that claim to heal, protect, or diagnose, making harm look like care and error look like expertise.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
This is the methods chapter. It moves from private recollection into professional judgment and shows how weak scientific discipline lets confident helpers trap themselves in a closed loop.

Author Logic Chain:
First the authors argue that science is valuable because it exposes error rather than flattering certainty.
Then they contrast falsifiable method with intuition, observation, and self-confirming clinical judgment.
Then they show how child-interviewing and recovered-memory style mistakes become more likely when experts seek confirmation instead of testing alternatives.

Required Anchors:
- Science as arrogance control: friction between expert confidence and the discipline of being proven wrong; consequence is that method, not benevolence, protects against self-justification.
- Closed loop of clinical judgment: friction between a clinician's sincere belief in intuition and the absence of independent verification; consequence is self-confirming error.
- Suggestive interviewing / false-report risk: friction between a helper convinced abuse occurred and the need to test multiple hypotheses; consequence is contamination and false certainty.

Frameworks or Terms Introduced:
- falsifiability: a claim counts as scientific only if it could be shown false
- closed loop of clinical judgment: intuition and interpretation confirming themselves without independent checks
- confirmation bias in helping systems: searching for signs that the preferred diagnosis is right while screening out alternatives

Specific Applications:
- asking what evidence would disconfirm a confident diagnosis or interpretation
- distinguishing empathy or benevolence from reliable method
- noticing how interviews and assessments can contaminate the evidence they claim to uncover

Common Misreadings:
- caring professionals are safe from self-justification because their motives are good
- observation and intuition become reliable if the practitioner is experienced enough

Counterarguments or Limits:
The frozen bundle supports falsifiability, clinical closed loops, and child-interview confirmation-bias risks. It does not support a sweeping history of psychotherapy, a full trauma-memory debate, or a broad attack on all expertise.
