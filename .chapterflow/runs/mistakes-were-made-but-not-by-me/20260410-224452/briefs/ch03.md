Core Claim:
This chapter shows what cognitive dissonance looks like when it leaves the private mind and becomes a social lens. People experience their own judgments as fair, direct, and reality-based, which makes prejudice and partisan certainty feel less like distortion and more like simple honesty. The practical sting is that blind spots survive precisely because they do not feel like blind spots from the inside. Self-justification therefore does not only defend isolated choices. It can also defend status, stereotypes, and the belief that only other people are biased.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
This chapter turns the mechanism outward. It is the chapter where self-justification fuses with naive realism, prejudice, and the conviction that one's own side is merely seeing the world as it is.

Author Logic Chain:
First the authors move from dissonance inside the person to the blind spot in social perception.
Then they show how prejudice and conflict are stabilized by the sense that one's own view is objective while rival views are distorted.
Then they show that this asymmetric certainty makes correction harder because evidence against the self gets interpreted as evidence against the other side instead.

Required Anchors:
- Naive realism and the bias blind spot: friction between claiming neutrality and actually reasoning from a defended standpoint; consequence is that disagreement gets read as proof that others are biased.
- Prejudice and stereotype preservation: friction between a fair-minded self-image and selective judgment about another group; consequence is that the stereotype survives while the self still feels decent.
- Escalating disagreement: friction between two sides who both feel plainly right; consequence is that mutual certainty hardens conflict instead of clarifying it.

Frameworks or Terms Introduced:
- naive realism: the habit of experiencing one's own view as straightforward reality rather than as an interpretation
- blind spot: the inability to detect one's own bias while easily spotting bias in others
- prejudice as self-justifying judgment: stereotype-preserving interpretation that protects moral innocence

Specific Applications:
- catching the move where disagreement is treated as proof that the other person is irrational
- recognizing how a fair-minded self-image can coexist with selective, biased interpretation
- seeing why conflict escalates when both sides experience themselves as neutral observers

Common Misreadings:
- prejudice only belongs to obviously malicious people
- blind spots disappear once people endorse fairness in principle

Counterarguments or Limits:
The frozen support is strong enough for blind spots, naive realism, and prejudice as defended interpretation. It does not support a long catalog of modern polarization studies or a taxonomy of every bias. The chapter should stay with the book's claim that moral innocence and distortion can travel together.
