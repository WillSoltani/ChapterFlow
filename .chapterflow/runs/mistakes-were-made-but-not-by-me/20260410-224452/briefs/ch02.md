Core Claim:
This chapter names the mechanism the opening chapter only framed. Cognitive dissonance is the tension created when actions, beliefs, or identities conflict, and self-justification is the mind's way of reducing that tension without surrendering a flattering self-story. The chapter's practical force is that dissonance reduction can feel reasonable, automatic, and even honest while it is quietly distorting judgment. It matters because once a decision is made, people often stop evaluating it neutrally and begin defending it. That defensive shift helps explain why weak choices harden into commitments.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
This is the conceptual engine room. It has to take the moral discomfort of Chapter 1 and turn it into a clean, memorable mechanism with real examples of how contradiction gets reduced.

Author Logic Chain:
First the authors define cognitive dissonance as tension between inconsistent cognitions.
Then they show how people reduce that tension by changing beliefs, memory, or interpretation rather than changing the action.
Then they show that once commitment begins, confirmation bias and self-justification make reversal harder.

Required Anchors:
- Smoking inconsistency example: friction between knowing smoking is harmful and continuing to smoke; consequence is belief revision and rationalization.
- Leon Festinger's failed-prophecy group: friction between the expected apocalypse and the ordinary world continuing; consequence is a new miracle story that preserves belief.
- Everyday post-decision defense: once a choice is made, people search for support and downplay disconfirming evidence.

Frameworks or Terms Introduced:
- cognitive dissonance: tension produced by psychologically inconsistent beliefs, choices, or self-images
- dissonance reduction: the mental moves used to reduce that tension
- confirmation bias: searching for support after a choice while discounting contrary evidence

Specific Applications:
- noticing how a decision becomes harder to reverse after public commitment
- seeing why harmful habits recruit rationalization instead of surrender
- recognizing that post-choice certainty can be a symptom of tension reduction rather than stronger evidence

Common Misreadings:
- dissonance only appears in dramatic moral crises
- reducing dissonance always means conscious deception

Counterarguments or Limits:
This chapter explains a broad mechanism, so it can tempt generic prose. The frozen bundle supports the smoking example, the failed-prophecy group, and the post-decision hardening pattern. It does not support a parade of extra studies or modern neuroscience gloss.

Cross-Chapter Tensions:

Moral Complexity:
The prose should keep the mechanism ordinary and human, but it must not make the damage feel harmless. Dissonance reduction protects the ego and can also preserve bad decisions, prejudice, and harm.

Hard-Depth Minimum:
Hard depth must keep the live question of whether dissonance is reduced more by changing facts, changing memory, or changing identity cost, and why committed people often experience their certainty as proof instead of defense.

Unsupported Zones:
