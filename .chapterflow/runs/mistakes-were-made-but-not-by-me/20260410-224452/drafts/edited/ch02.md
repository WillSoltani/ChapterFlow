You know smoking can kill you, and you smoke anyway. The chapter wants that split to arrive before any definition does. One belief says the habit is dangerous. Another fact says the habit continues. The mind does not enjoy leaving those two things grinding against each other for long.

That friction is cognitive dissonance. It is the tension that appears when beliefs, actions, attitudes, or self-image do not line up cleanly. The issue is not inconsistency in the abstract. The issue is inconsistency that threatens a story you need in order to keep living as yourself. If you think of yourself as sensible and in control, continuing a dangerous habit is not just one bad fact. It is pressure against the self you think you are.

That is what makes dissonance the engine behind self-justification. Once the tension appears, the mind starts looking for relief. Quitting would reduce it. So would admitting, flatly, that you are acting against your own judgment. But those options are costly. It is often easier to attack the warning, minimize the risk, invent an exception, or promise that the real change will happen later. The behavior stays. The meaning of the behavior gets revised.

The smoking example matters because it is easy to recognize and hard to wave away. A person can say the evidence is exaggerated. They can point to an uncle who lived forever. They can insist the danger only applies to heavier users. They can promise that this pack is temporary. None of those moves removes the contradiction. They only dull it. Dissonance reduction is relief, not truth correction.

Once that pattern is clear, a stranger case makes more sense. People do not always loosen their grip on a belief when reality humiliates it. Sometimes they grip harder. Leon Festinger's failed-prophecy group expected the world to end. The world did not cooperate. On paper, that should have shattered the belief. In practice, the failure was reworked into a miracle story that let belief survive. Contradiction did not produce surrender. It produced a more urgent explanation.

That example breaks the comforting idea that bad evidence naturally creates humility. Often it creates pressure instead. And once the pressure is strong enough, a person may not ask, “What is true?” first. They may ask, more privately and more urgently, “How do I keep living with this?” If the answer requires turning failure into hidden confirmation, hidden confirmation is what the mind reaches for.

This also explains why decisions harden after they are made. Before a choice, uncertainty can still feel open. After a choice, uncertainty starts to threaten judgment itself. A person who has committed to a candidate, a plan, a purchase, a relationship, or a public position now has more to defend than the choice. They also have their own competence, discernment, and story about why they chose well. That is when support starts looking irresistible and contradiction starts looking unfair.

People call that stubbornness. The chapter makes it more precise. Post-decision certainty is often dissonance reduction at work. The person is not merely weighing evidence badly. They are trying to quiet the tension created by the possibility that they chose poorly. Evidence that supports the choice feels calming. Evidence that threatens the choice feels hostile, overblown, or somehow not quite relevant. Confirmation bias does not arrive alone. It arrives with work to do.

That gives the chapter a hard edge. More commitment does not always mean more clarity. Sometimes it means more defense. The person who sounds most certain may be the person under the most pressure to protect the choice already made. Certainty is not proof by itself. Sometimes it is the surface look of a mind trying to shut down discomfort.

The cost of that relief can be high. If a smoker keeps rewriting risk, the habit keeps going. If a group keeps rewriting failed prophecy, belief deepens instead of learning. If a person keeps defending a bad decision after commitment, the next decision gets built on the same weak foundation. The short-term reduction in tension can create a longer-term increase in distortion.

The mechanism has to stay ordinary. It does not belong only to cult members or spectacularly self-destructive people. It lives in daily life: the proposal you defend after the numbers turn against it, the purchase you suddenly love more once buyer's remorse appears, the argument you start believing more deeply after you win it in public because backing down would now cost too much. The scale changes. The structure does not.

By the end, self-justification looks less like a personality quirk and more like a pressure response. That makes it easier to understand and harder to excuse. The mind reduces tension by protecting the self faster than it protects the truth. Once that lands, the next question is unavoidable. If people defend commitments this fiercely, how often are they mistaking a defended view for simple reality? The next chapter moves into that blind spot.
