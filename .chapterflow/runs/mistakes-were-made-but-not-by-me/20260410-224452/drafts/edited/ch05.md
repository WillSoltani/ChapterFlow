It is easy to trust a helper who sounds certain. The certainty feels generous. It feels like care. A practitioner who says, with calm confidence, “I know what is happening here,” can seem safer than one who keeps testing, doubting, and reopening the case. That is the trap at the center of this chapter. Good intentions and firm conviction do not rescue a bad method.

That is why the chapter turns to science so sharply. Science matters here not because it makes people less caring, but because it places care inside procedures that can expose error. A good method does not ask only how to confirm an idea. It asks what would count against it. That is the discipline the authors frame as a kind of arrogance control. The aim is not to humiliate expertise. The aim is to keep expertise from sealing itself inside its own confidence.

Once that standard is visible, the closed loop of clinical judgment becomes easier to see. A clinician observes a person through a favored theory, interprets the behavior through that theory, and then treats the interpretation as proof that the theory was right all along. The loop closes. Intuition confirms itself. Observation looks rich and humane, but because it is not being tested against independent disconfirmation, it can become a machine for protecting the original belief.

That makes the chapter harsher than a simple warning against overconfidence. The people inside these loops are often trying to help. They may be empathic, experienced, and sincere. Sincerity is not a substitute for a method that can fail publicly. If a professional identity is built around insight, healing, or protection, then being wrong threatens more than one conclusion. It threatens the self-story of being the kind of person who sees what others miss. That is exactly when self-justification becomes most useful and most dangerous.

Observation and intuition are not worthless here. The point is narrower and harder. They are unreliable when left alone. They become especially risky when a practitioner mistakes felt depth for tested truth. A meaningful look, a halting sentence, a remembered fragment, a child's pause before answering, or a pattern that just fits can start feeling diagnostic long before it has earned that authority. Once the interpretation starts to feel morally urgent, alternative explanations become harder to tolerate.

That is where suggestive interviewing and false-report risk enter. If a helper already believes abuse or trauma must have occurred, the interview can quietly stop being a search and become a hunt. Questions begin leaning. Repetition starts pressing. Ambiguity feels intolerable. What the adult hears as careful persistence may function as contamination. The evidence being uncovered is no longer cleanly separate from the method that drew it out.

This matters because benevolence can protect bad method. A cruel practitioner is easier to distrust. A compassionate one can be harder to question because the motive feels morally clean. Good motives do not neutralize confirmation bias. In some cases they intensify it. The more the professional believes they are rescuing, protecting, or finally uncovering the truth, the more disconfirming evidence can look like obstruction, denial, or failure to care enough.

That is why falsifiability matters so much here. A method is not reliable because the expert believes in it, because it sounds humane, or because it produces emotionally compelling stories. It becomes more reliable when it can be shown wrong, checked from the outside, and disciplined by procedures that do not depend on the practitioner's self-trust alone. Without that discipline, the closed loop keeps rewarding itself. Each interpretation becomes the evidence for the next interpretation.

The damage is not abstract. A bad clinical theory can guide a bad interview. A bad interview can produce contaminated recollection. A contaminated recollection can then be treated as the proof that the theory was right. By the time the loop completes itself, the result can feel not only persuasive but righteous. Harm begins wearing the face of help.

That gives the chapter its edge. The problem is not only that experts can be wrong. The problem is that some systems of expertise make wrongness harder to detect precisely because they reward confidence, narrative fit, and emotional conviction more than disconfirming method. A practitioner can grow more certain while growing less reliable.

Seen that way, this chapter follows the memory chapter naturally. Chapter 4 showed how recollection can become self-protective inside one person. Here the same pressure enters professional roles and methods. Defended interpretation now has credentials, authority, and institutional room to operate.

That is why the move into law is so close. Once a system rewards early certainty, suggestive questioning, and resistance to disconfirmation, it does not stay confined to clinics or interviews. It appears wherever authorities interpret people under pressure and then protect the story they first chose. The next chapter enters that harsher room.
