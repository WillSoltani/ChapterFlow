Two people leave the same argument equally sure of one thing: the other person is impossible. Neither one feels hysterical. Both feel factual. Both would say they are reacting to what is plainly there. That calm certainty is part of the trap. Blind spots do not feel like blindness. They feel like clear sight.

Cognitive dissonance explained why people defend choices and identities once contradiction appears. Here the defense becomes social. A person does not only protect a past decision. They protect the sense that their own view is neutral, fair, and reality-based. If judgment feels like what any reasonable observer would see, then disagreement stops looking like interpretation and starts looking like distortion on the other side.

That habit is often called naive realism. The phrase matters because it does not mean foolishness. It means people experience their own perception as direct contact with the world rather than as one perspective shaped by loyalties, fears, interests, and prior commitments. Your own reaction feels like sight. The other person's reaction feels like bias. Once that asymmetry settles in, correction gets difficult. You do not feel as if you are defending yourself. You feel as if you are defending reality.

That is why the bias blind spot has such force. People can detect selective reasoning and self-serving interpretation in rivals quickly. They are much worse at detecting the same moves in themselves. This is not because they have no standards. Often the standards are part of the flattering story. A person can think of themselves as open-minded and still screen evidence in ways that protect that self-image. The feeling of fairness can become one of the devices that keeps unfairness in place.

Prejudice survives inside that structure more easily than people want to admit. It would be convenient if prejudice belonged only to openly malicious people, because then everyone else could escape the problem by rejecting malice. The chapter removes that convenience. A person can believe they are decent and humane while still interpreting another group through a selective lens that protects old stereotypes. The stereotype does not have to feel vicious in order to do harm. It only has to feel reasonable.

That is what makes blind spots morally dangerous while still feeling ordinary. A manager insists they are only hiring for fit. A student group insists it is only protecting standards. A neighbor insists they are only being careful. Each explanation may contain a little truth. Self-justification works in the space between that little truth and the whole story. Evidence that confirms the prior suspicion stands out. Evidence that complicates it fades. The person keeps a clean self-image and a distorted judgment at the same time.

Once the pattern becomes mutual, conflict hardens. Each side feels it is responding to facts while the other side is responding to ideology, resentment, insecurity, or prejudice. The dispute is no longer only about the original issue. It becomes a dispute about who is sane, fair, and realistic. That is why arguments grow sticky. Every new disagreement can be read as fresh proof that the other side is the biased side.

This pushes past a generic plea for empathy. The issue is not only that people dislike being wrong. The issue is that their own stance feels like the unfiltered baseline. If your position feels like common sense and the other person's position feels like distortion, then your own motives disappear from view. You start reading yourself as a witness and the other person as a defendant. Self-justification protects not only the conclusion but also the authority of the person reaching it.

That helps explain why accusing people of bias often fails. The accusation may be correct. It may also hit the exact structure being defended. A person who already experiences themselves as fair can hear the charge of unfairness not as useful friction but as more evidence that the accuser is unreasonable or manipulative. The criticism folds back into the story already in place. The challenge does not dissolve the blind spot. It can deepen it.

None of this means every disagreement is symmetrical or every claim of bias is equally weak. The argument is harder than that. Harmful judgment often survives by borrowing the emotional force of innocence. People can act selectively, unfairly, or prejudicially while sincerely feeling that they are only responding to what is plainly there. Sincerity does not rescue the judgment from distortion.

That is why this chapter belongs right after cognitive dissonance. Dissonance showed how the mind protects a choice or identity from contradiction. Blind spots show what happens when that same defense reaches perception itself. The person is no longer only editing the meaning of a bad choice. They are editing the status of their own viewpoint. What they see feels like reality. What others see feels like bias.

Once that lands, another question is hard to avoid. If people can defend the present so thoroughly that their own perspective feels objective, what happens to the past they remember living through? The next chapter moves there. It asks what becomes of memory when the self needs not only to see clearly now but also to remember cleanly later.
