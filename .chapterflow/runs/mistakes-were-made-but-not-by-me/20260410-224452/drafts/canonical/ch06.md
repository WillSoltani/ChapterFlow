An official decides, early and with relief, that the right suspect is already in hand. From that moment on, the case can start feeling cleaner than it is. The evidence still looks open from the outside. Inside the investigation, though, the story has already begun to narrow. That is the opening force of this chapter. In law, self-justification does not stay private. It gains authority.

That is why the chapter is so severe. A wrong guess in ordinary life can distort one relationship or one memory. A wrong guess inside law can begin organizing interviews, evidence review, prosecution, and public narrative. Once guilt feels chosen, later evidence stops arriving on neutral ground. It arrives inside a system that now has a suspect, a theory, and a growing investment in being right about both.

That is the logic behind jumping to convictions. Early certainty is not merely an intellectual mistake. It is the point at which tunnel vision begins doing its work. The chosen suspect starts attracting confirming details. Alternative explanations start looking thinner, less urgent, or inconveniently distracting. The more public the commitment becomes, the more reversal starts to feel like weakness rather than correction.

Interrogation pressure makes that narrowing harsher. If the authority already believes it knows what happened, questioning can turn from inquiry into extraction. Deception, pressure, insistence, and interpretive confidence begin pushing the suspect toward the answer the system already wants. A confession then feels like gold because it seems to come from the suspect's own mouth. But what made the confession possible may be the very pressure that should make it suspect.

That is part of what makes the chapter so unsettling. People like to think confession settles reality. The authors press against that comfort. In a charged room, a confession can become the fastest route out of unbearable pressure, confusion, fatigue, or hopelessness. What looks like the strongest evidence may also be the moment the system finally got the person inside it to cooperate with the story it had already chosen.

Once that story is in motion, evidence itself can start getting reweighted. Details that fit guilt look strong and coherent. Details that complicate guilt look noisy, technical, or secondary. The interpretation does not only sit on top of the evidence. It begins shaping what kind of evidence feels worth trusting. That is the legal form of the same closed loop the previous chapter described in clinical work.

The chapter becomes darker still after conviction. At that point, the case is no longer only a theory. It is a result that has been announced, defended, and often woven into professional identity. A prosecutor, officer, or institution that reopens the possibility of innocence is not just revising an inference. It is threatening its own history of certainty. Exonerating evidence can therefore feel less like clarification and more like attack.

That is why later proof of innocence becomes psychologically and institutionally hard to absorb. The system now has a verdict to preserve, careers attached to it, public confidence attached to it, and a self-image attached to it. If the conviction was wrong, then confidence was not strength but part of the damage. Self-justification enters to prevent that admission from landing cleanly.

This does not mean every legal actor is corrupt or malicious. The chapter's harder point is that many may believe they are protecting the public. That belief can make the system more dangerous, not less, because sincerely held duty gives certainty a moral charge. Doubt begins to resemble betrayal. Reversal begins to resemble disloyalty to victims, colleagues, or the role itself.

That is why legal certainty has to be treated carefully in this chapter. Confidence may reflect strong evidence. It may also reflect how much has already been invested in the chosen story. The person who sounds most settled may be the person least able to tolerate what reversal would mean. Institutional self-justification makes certainty louder, not necessarily truer.

Seen that way, the chapter extends the previous one exactly. Clinical judgment showed how a professional loop can protect its own method. Law shows what happens when the loop gets coercive power, public stakes, and the authority to declare guilt. The mechanism is the same. The costs are simply far higher.

That is why the next move into marriage is not a decrease in seriousness. It is a shift of scale. The same machinery that defends a case file can also defend a private grievance, a remembered insult, or a hardened blame story inside intimate life. The badge comes off. The self-justification stays.
