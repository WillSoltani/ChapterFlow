Two people leave the same argument convinced that one thing, at least, is now obvious: the other person is impossible. Each one feels calmer than caricature would predict. Each one feels factual. Each one would say they are reacting to what is plainly there. That calm certainty is part of the problem. Blind spots do not announce themselves as blind spots. They arrive with the feeling of clear sight.

That is the move this chapter cares about. Cognitive dissonance explained why people defend choices and identities once contradiction appears. Here the defense becomes social. A person does not only protect a past decision. They protect the sense that their own view is neutral, fair, and reality-based. If they feel that their judgment is simply what any reasonable observer would see, then disagreement no longer looks like a difference in interpretation. It looks like distortion on the other side.

That habit is often described as naive realism. The phrase matters because it does not mean stupidity. It means people experience their own perception as direct contact with the world rather than as one perspective shaped by interests, loyalties, fears, and prior commitments. Your own reaction feels like sight. The other person's reaction feels like bias. Once that asymmetry sets in, correction gets difficult. You do not feel as if you are defending yourself. You feel as if you are defending reality.

That is why the bias blind spot has such a grip. People can spot selective reasoning, self-serving excuses, and motivated interpretation in rivals quickly. They are much worse at spotting the same moves in themselves. This is not because they have no standards. Often it is because standards are part of the flattering story. A person can think of themselves as open-minded and still screen evidence in ways that protect that self-image. The conviction of fairness can become one of the tools that keeps unfairness in place.

Prejudice survives inside that structure more easily than people want to admit. It would be comforting if prejudice belonged only to openly cruel people, because then everyone else could step outside the problem by rejecting cruelty. The chapter pushes against that comfort. A person can believe they are decent, modern, and humane while still interpreting another group through a selective lens that protects old stereotypes. The stereotype does not have to feel vicious in order to do damage. It only has to feel reasonable.

That is one reason blind spots can be morally dangerous while still feeling ordinary. Suppose a manager insists they are only hiring for “fit,” or a student group insists it is only protecting standards, or a neighbor insists they are only being cautious. Each explanation may contain a little truth. But self-justification works in the gap between a little truth and the whole story. Evidence that confirms the prior suspicion stands out. Evidence that complicates it fades. The person keeps a clean self-image and a distorted judgment at the same time.

Once that pattern becomes shared, conflict hardens. Each side starts from the feeling that it is responding to the facts while the other side is responding to ideology, insecurity, resentment, or prejudice. The dispute is no longer only about the original issue. It becomes a dispute about who is sane, fair, and realistic. That is why arguments become so sticky. Every new piece of disagreement can be read as more proof that the other side is the biased side.

This is where the chapter presses past a simple warning about tolerance. The problem is not only that people dislike being wrong. The problem is that they experience their own position as the unfiltered baseline. If your stance feels like common sense and the other person's stance feels like distortion, then your own motives fade from view. You start reading yourself as a witness and the other person as a defendant. Self-justification does not merely defend the conclusion. It also defends the authority of the person reaching it.

That helps explain why accusing people of bias often fails. The accusation may be accurate. It may also strike the exact structure that is being defended. A person who already experiences themselves as fair can hear the charge of unfairness not as useful friction but as more evidence that the accuser is unreasonable or manipulative. The criticism folds back into the story they were already telling. The challenge does not dissolve the blind spot. It can deepen it.

None of this means every disagreement is symmetrical or every claim of bias is equally weak. The chapter is not asking the reader to flatten real differences into a fog of mutual misunderstanding. It is making a harder point. Harmful judgment often survives by borrowing the emotional force of innocence. People can do selective, prejudiced, or self-serving things while sincerely feeling that they are only responding to what is plainly there. Sincerity does not rescue the judgment from distortion.

That is why the chapter belongs right after the one on cognitive dissonance. Dissonance showed how the mind protects a choice or identity from contradiction. Blind spots show what happens when that same defense reaches perception itself. The person is no longer only editing the meaning of a bad choice. They are editing the status of their own viewpoint. What they see feels like reality. What others see feels like bias.

Once that lands, another question becomes hard to avoid. If people can defend the present so thoroughly that their own perspective feels objective, what happens to the past they remember living through? The next chapter moves there. It asks what becomes of memory when the self needs not only to see clearly now but also to remember cleanly later.
