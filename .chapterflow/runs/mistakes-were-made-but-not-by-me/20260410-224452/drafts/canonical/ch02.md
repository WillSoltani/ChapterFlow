You know smoking can kill you, and you smoke anyway. That is the kind of split the chapter wants you to feel before it defines anything. One belief says the habit is dangerous. Another fact says the habit continues. The mind does not enjoy leaving those two things rubbing against each other for long.

That friction is cognitive dissonance. It is the tension that appears when beliefs, actions, attitudes, or self-image do not line up cleanly. The point is not that people hate inconsistency in the abstract. The point is that inconsistency becomes painful when it threatens a story they need in order to keep living as themselves. If you think of yourself as sensible and in control, then continuing a dangerous habit is not just one bad fact. It is an insult to the self you think you are.

That is why dissonance matters so much to the book. Self-justification is not floating free. It is the relief valve. Once tension appears, the mind starts looking for a way to reduce it. Quitting would reduce it. So would admitting, plainly, that you are acting against your own judgment. But those options can be costly. It is often easier to attack the warning, minimize the risk, invent an exception, or tell yourself that you can stop any time. The behavior remains. The meaning of the behavior gets reworked.

The smoker example matters because it is easy to recognize and hard to dismiss. A person can say the evidence is exaggerated. They can point to a relative who lived forever. They can insist the damage only happens to heavier users. They can promise that this pack is temporary and the real decision will come later. None of those moves removes the contradiction. They only make it feel less sharp. Dissonance reduction is not the same thing as truth correction.

Once you see that, a second pattern becomes easier to understand. People do not always back away from a belief when reality embarrasses it. Sometimes they grip harder. Leon Festinger's failed-prophecy group expected the world to end. The world did not cooperate. On paper, that should have destroyed the belief. In practice, the group found a way to reinterpret the failure so the belief could survive. The contradiction did not produce surrender. It produced a more desperate story.

That example matters because it breaks the comforting assumption that bad evidence naturally creates humility. Often it creates pressure. And once the pressure becomes strong enough, a person may not ask, “What is true?” first. They may ask, more urgently and more privately, “How can I keep living with this?” If the answer requires turning failure into hidden confirmation, then hidden confirmation is what the mind reaches for.

This is also why decisions harden after they are made. Before a choice, uncertainty can still feel open. After a choice, uncertainty starts to feel like a threat to judgment. A person who has committed to a candidate, a plan, a purchase, a relationship, or a public position now has more to defend than the choice itself. They have their own competence, their own discernment, their own story about why they chose well. The result is predictable. They begin collecting support and discounting contradiction.

That shift often gets described as stubbornness. The chapter makes it more precise. Post-decision certainty is frequently dissonance reduction at work. The person is not merely weighing evidence badly. They are trying to quiet the tension created by the possibility that they chose poorly. Evidence that supports the choice feels calming. Evidence that threatens the choice feels unfair, hostile, overblown, or somehow not quite relevant. Confirmation bias does not arrive alone. It arrives with a job to do.

This is where the chapter gains some bite. People like to imagine that more commitment means more clarity. Sometimes it means more defense. The person who sounds most certain may be the person under the most pressure to protect the choice they already made. That does not mean all certainty is fake. It does mean certainty is not innocent evidence by itself. Sometimes it is the surface look of a mind trying to get the discomfort to shut up.

The cost of that relief can be high. If a smoker keeps rewriting risk, the habit keeps going. If a group keeps rewriting failed prophecy, belief deepens instead of learning. If a person keeps defending a bad decision after commitment, the next decision gets built on the same weak foundation. Dissonance reduction can feel stabilizing in the moment and still make the longer path more distorted.

This is why the mechanism has to stay ordinary. It is not an exotic flaw belonging to cult members or obviously self-destructive people. It sits inside daily life. The proposal you defend after the numbers turn against it. The habit you explain instead of changing. The purchase you suddenly love more after buyer's remorse appears. The argument you won in public and start believing more deeply in private because backing down would now cost too much. The scale changes. The structure does not.

By the end of the chapter, self-justification looks less like a character defect and more like a pressure response. That makes it easier to understand and harder to excuse. The mind reduces tension by protecting the self faster than it protects the truth. Once that idea lands, the next problem is almost unavoidable. If people defend commitments this fiercely, how often are they mistaking their defended view for simple reality? The next chapter moves there, into the blind spots that feel like objectivity from the inside.
