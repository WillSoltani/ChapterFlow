Pride and Prejudice . . . and Other Blind Spots

- Secondary support describes the authors' claim that people often experience their own perceptions as direct reality while treating rivals as biased.

Allowed chapter-local claims:
- People commonly experience their own perspective as objective reality rather than as one interpretation among others.
- The bias blind spot is the habit of noticing bias in other people more readily than in oneself.
- Prejudice can be sustained not only by hostility but by self-justifying interpretation that keeps the self feeling fair.
- Conflict escalates when both sides interpret their own view as neutral and the opposing view as distorted.
- Merely accusing another person of bias can intensify self-defense rather than open reflection.

Supported paraphrase anchors:
1. Naive realism:
   A person feels they are simply seeing the facts, which makes disagreement look like evidence of the other person's distortion.
2. Prejudice with moral innocence:
   A person preserves a stereotype or unfair judgment while continuing to experience themselves as decent and fair.
3. Mutual certainty:
   Two sides in conflict each experience themselves as the realistic side, which hardens the dispute.
