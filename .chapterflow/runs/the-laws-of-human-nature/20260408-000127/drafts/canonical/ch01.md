# Introduction

People explain a tense interaction in seconds and then spend hours defending the story they chose. Greene opens by challenging that reflex. Human nature is not easiest to see in what people say about themselves. It is easier to see in motive, contradiction, repetition, and the way behavior shifts under pressure. Surface presentation still matters, but it is incomplete evidence. The introduction teaches the reader to stop treating immediate interpretation as settled fact.

The most important restraint arrives early: begin with your own distortions. If you skip that step, the book becomes a catalog of accusations. Your hope edits warning signs. Your fear upgrades ambiguity into threat. Your vanity turns discomfort into insult. The reader is not asked to distrust everything. The reader is asked to stop assuming the observing mind is neutral. That is the first methodological correction.

From there the introduction establishes a practical sequence. Compare declarations with repeated conduct. Watch what changes when status is at stake, when someone is cornered, when praise appears, when pressure rises, when a pattern repeats. Greene is shifting the reader from a language-based read to a behavior-based read. That move is useful because behavior leaves a trail. A polished explanation can change in an hour. A recurrent reaction usually does not.

The opening also names its own failure mode. Pattern reading can degrade into paranoia if every inconsistency becomes evidence of secret malice. Greene's frame is narrower than that. Read motive probabilistically. Hold interpretation lightly until behavior confirms it. Include your own bias in the scene. The result is not omniscience. It is better calibration. That cleaner calibration is the opening tool the rest of the book depends on, because every later law becomes easier to misuse if the reader never learns to question the first story that flashes into mind.

Initial interpretation is frequently self-protective before it is evidentially sound.

The nervous system prefers a coherent explanation to an open question. That preference biases early interpretation toward whatever reduces ambiguity fastest.

Your own hope, fear, vanity, and resentment distort what you notice.

A method that studies everyone else but exempts the person using it will always collapse into projection.

Repeated conduct under pressure is more diagnostic than controlled self-description.

Sequence matters because it lets you compare what was said with what followed, and then compare that with what keeps recurring.

The opening problem is not simply that people hide what they want. It is that the observing mind rushes to close the case before the evidence is mature. A manager hesitates, a partner softens their tone, a classmate withdraws, and the explanation arrives instantly. Greene starts by attacking that reflex because human nature is not easiest to read in declarations. It is easier to read in motive, contradiction, repetition, compensation, and the conduct that appears when pressure narrows a person's options. Surface behavior still matters, but only as one layer in a larger sequence.

The first corrective move is methodological, not moral: treat your own interpretation as compromised from the start. Hope edits away danger. Fear overweights threat. Vanity personalizes neutral friction. Resentment supplies motive before evidence does. The introduction blocks the fantasy that the reader is standing outside the scene with clean instruments. The instruments are already fogged by appetite, insecurity, and self-protection. If that is not admitted early, the entire book turns into a language system for flattering projection.

Greene's proposed alternative is disciplined pattern reading. Ask what the person says, what they do next, what changes under pressure, and what repeats across settings. This is stronger than relying on declarations because behavior leaves a sequence, and sequences can be compared. The method is still inferential. It does not abolish uncertainty. That is why the introduction keeps narrowing its ambition. Read motive provisionally. Do not confuse a useful hypothesis with omniscience.

That limit is more important than it first appears. Overreading does not represent an excess of intelligence. It represents a collapse back into emotional need, this time the need to feel certain, superior, or protected. The paranoid reader and the naive reader both misread the room; they just fail in opposite directions. The calibrated reader leaves room for confirmation, revision, and the possibility that a behavior reveals stress rather than malice, vanity rather than strategy, habit rather than a master plan.

The opening also introduces an ethical burden. A more accurate read of people can become a basis for patience and precision, or it can become a basis for contempt. Greene blocks the contempt route by making the same discipline apply inward. You are not invited to stand above human nature. You are included inside it. That inclusion is the introduction's hardest edge, and it is what makes the next chapter necessary: before you can trust your read of anyone else, you have to understand what emotion is doing to your own judgment in the first place.
