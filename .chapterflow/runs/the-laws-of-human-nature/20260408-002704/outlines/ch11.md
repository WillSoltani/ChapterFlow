Chapter Promise:
By the end, the reader should understand envy as hidden status pain that often disguises itself as praise, coolness, criticism, or sabotage.

Opening Move:
Open on Mary Shelley and Jane Williams so the law begins with comparison, admiration, and concealed bitterness rather than abstract definition.

Anchor Allocation:
- Mary Shelley and Jane Williams used in P1 and P2
- envy and status pain used in P2 and P3
- poisonous praise, disguised hostility, and emulation used in P4 and P5

Paragraph Job Map:
- P1: hook with Mary Shelley and Jane Williams as a charged comparison pair
- P2: define envy as ego pain caused by another person's advantage
- P3: explain why envy hides and how fragile ego turns comparison into humiliation
- P4: show disguised forms such as poisonous praise, coldness, sabotage, and pleasure at decline
- P5: distinguish corrosive envy from healthy emulation
- P6: bridge to grandiosity as another response to ego fragility

Required Distinction or Mechanism:
The key distinction is between emulation and envy. Emulation says "I can learn from that." Envy says "That should not belong to them."

Hard-Depth Tension:
The reader must admit envy without pretending the answer is simply self-condemnation or fake humility.

- Takeaway 1: envy as hidden status pain

- Takeaway 3: fragile ego and humiliating comparison
- Takeaway 4: poisonous praise and disguised hostility

Scenario Lesson Map:

- Scenario 4 (predict_reveal, work): reveal hidden envy behind moral criticism of a high performer
- Scenario 5 (dilemma, personal): choose between admitting status pain or disguising it as superior taste
