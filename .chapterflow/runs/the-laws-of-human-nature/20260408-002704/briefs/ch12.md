Chapter Number: 12
Chapter Title: Know Your Limits: The Law of Grandiosity
Position In Book: eleventh law chapter, chapter 12 of 19

Core Claim:
Greene argues that success can intoxicate the ego and turn confidence into grandiosity. Once people start treating their victories as proof of extraordinary judgment rather than a mix of skill, timing, and luck, they stop respecting limits, ignore feedback, and overreach.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
This is the success-inflation chapter. It follows envy by showing a different response to fragility: instead of shrinking under comparison, the ego can enlarge itself past reality and begin acting as if ordinary constraints no longer apply.

Author Logic Chain:
First the author uses Michael Eisner to show a leader whose early success fed an inflated sense of special insight and control.
Then the author defines grandiosity as success delusion: forgetting luck, rejecting limits, and identifying too completely with an enlarged self-image.
Then the author concludes that the reader must build reality-testing habits, preserve dissent, and remember dependence on others before confidence hardens into overreach.

- Michael Eisner as the central success-to-overreach anchor.

- overreach and feedback collapse as the practical warning signs.

Common Misreadings:
- treating grandiosity as the same thing as healthy confidence
- reading the chapter as anti-ambition or anti-scale
- assuming only celebrities or CEOs suffer from success delusion

Counterarguments or Limits:
Confidence and vision are necessary, and success does provide evidence of ability. The problem is not ambition but inflated self-reading that forgets luck, stops listening, and treats temporary advantage as permanent superiority.

Previous Chapter Bridge:
Chapter 11 showed how fragile ego can suffer under comparison and hide in envy. Chapter 12 studies the opposite compensation: the ego swells itself upward so it no longer has to feel small.
Next Chapter Bridge:
Once the reader sees how the self can inflate into a caricature of power, the next chapter studies another rigid caricature: exaggerated identification with the masculine or feminine side of the psyche.

Cross-Chapter Tensions:
- The chapter must separate grandiosity from real confidence and earned ambition.
- It must keep Michael Eisner concrete instead of drifting into generic anti-leader rhetoric.

Moral Complexity:
High. The reader is asked to admit that success can corrupt self-knowledge, even when the success was partly deserved.
