Chapter Title: Make Them Want to Follow You: The Law of Fickleness
Position In Book: fifteenth law chapter, chapter 16 of 19

Core Claim:
Greene argues that people are unstable in their loyalties to leaders. They want direction, but they also resent being controlled, tire of familiarity, and quickly reprice whoever seems weak, needy, or too available. Leadership therefore requires steadiness, authority, and measured distance rather than a constant bid for affection.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
This is the leadership-volatility chapter. It follows conformity by showing that the same crowd capable of absorbing people is also emotionally unstable. The problem is no longer only group pressure but how authority survives inside shifting followership.

Author Logic Chain:
First the author uses Queen Elizabeth I, with William Cecil and Mary Queen of Scots in the frame, to show how authority can be preserved when a ruler understands the volatility of public feeling.
Then the author defines fickleness as unstable followership: the tendency of people to admire, depend on, test, resent, and quickly downgrade leaders depending on mood, access, and appearance.
Then the author concludes that the reader must lead with steadiness, controlled distance, and symbolic authority, avoiding overfamiliarity, favoritism, and visible need for approval.

Counterarguments or Limits:
Authority without competence, fairness, or steadiness does not last. Greene's point is narrower: because followership is emotionally unstable, leaders weaken themselves when they make authority too cheap, too familiar, or too dependent on affection.

Previous Chapter Bridge:

Next Chapter Bridge:
