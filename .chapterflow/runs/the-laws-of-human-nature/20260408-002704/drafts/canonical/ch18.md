Greene opens with Georges-Jacques Danton and Robespierre because one revolution can contain more than one emotional climate. Danton fit an earlier phase of the French Revolution, when forceful energy and broad movement mattered. Robespierre fit a later phase, when purity, suspicion, and harder discipline gained force. The point is not trivia about two men. It is timing.

That is the law of generational myopia. Every generation is shaped by a particular zeitgeist during its formative years. What feels exciting, honorable, rebellious, realistic, or outdated gets stamped into people early, and later they mistake those assumptions for permanent reality.

This is why Greene treats generation as an emotional imprint rather than as a mere birth label. A cohort grows up reacting against the values of its elders, forms a style of ambition and rebellion around that reaction, and then risks becoming blind in its own turn. What once looked daring hardens into habit. What once felt current starts to lag the moment.

Danton and Robespierre matter because they show how quickly the mood can move even inside the same upheaval. One style of leadership matches an opening burst of change. Another style matches a darker, more suspicious phase. The reader is meant to see that success is not just about character or will. It is also about whether your instincts fit the historical climate now rather than five or ten years ago.

The practical signs of generational myopia are familiar. A leader keeps using language that once inspired people but now sounds stale. An institution keeps defending rituals that no longer carry emotional force. A person mocks younger tastes instead of asking what frustration or aspiration those tastes express. The mistake is not age. The mistake is treating one's own formative lens as the whole map.

Greene's response is not to worship novelty or stereotype people by cohort. It is to study shifts in mood, status, aspiration, and style more carefully. Readers have to notice when their instincts are lagging, look at what younger energies are reacting against, and adapt their message or ambition to the world that is actually taking shape.

That placement after aggression matters. After reading hidden hostility inside people, Greene widens the frame to the larger moods shaping what kinds of behavior rise or fail. And that is why the final chapter turns to death denial: once timing and historical mood are visible, the last question becomes how mortality restores proportion, seriousness, and purpose.
