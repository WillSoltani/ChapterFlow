The South Sea Bubble is Greene's warning that people can become intelligent about details while becoming foolish about time. In the heat of a crowd, everyone sees the rising price, the immediate excitement, and the fear of missing out. Very few people keep seeing the longer arc, the fragility of the story, or the cost that waits beyond the current surge.

That is shortsightedness. It is not simply bad planning. It is what happens when emotion compresses the horizon. Desire, fear, anger, and urgency all make the near-term moment feel larger than the future that will eventually have to absorb the choice.

Greene pairs that warning with Maria Mitchell. Her value is not drama but disciplined observation. She represents the mind that can step back, keep watching, and let a wider frame reveal what excitement hides. Distance is not passivity here. It is a way of seeing more.

The practical lesson is to widen time. Delay the email. Revisit the offer tomorrow. Ask what this move looks like in a month, a year, or after the current mood cools. Ask what second-order effects arrive after the short-term relief. Those questions weaken the spell of the present tense.

Most daily mistakes are not dramatic bubbles, but they run on the same mechanism. A manager reacts to one bad quarter as if the whole strategy is dead. A partner speaks from one angry evening as if the relationship has always been that hour. A student chases the prestigious option without asking what the long game will actually require.

That is why chapter 7 matters after covetousness. Desire heats the system. Shortsightedness narrows the lens. If the reader cannot create distance, the thing they want most now will keep overruling what they will need later.
