Richard Nixon matters to Greene because Nixon represents a person who built a public image around discipline, control, and strategic realism while darker insecurity, hostility, and suspicion kept leaking through the surface. Nixon is the warning that what is pushed down does not disappear.

That is the chapter's central law of repression. Greene argues that people disown traits they cannot fit into their preferred self-image. Carl Jung gives him the language for this hidden side: the shadow. Repression is not removal. It is denial combined with concealment, and the denied material keeps staying active.

This is why Greene focuses on projection and leakage. A person who cannot admit ambition, envy, cruelty, vanity, or need in themselves often starts seeing those traits everywhere else. They moralize, overreact, gossip, scapegoat, or lash out in ways that reveal more than they intend.

The answer is not indulgence. Greene does not tell the reader to obey every impulse because it feels authentic. He asks the reader to notice recurring triggers, fantasies, disproportionate judgments, and moments of unexplained heat. Those are clues that the shadow is active and asking to be recognized rather than disguised.

The law matters in ordinary life. A manager who condemns selfish ambition may be defending against their own hunger for status. A partner who keeps accusing the other person of manipulation may be hiding their own need to control. A student group that isolates one member may be exporting insecurity it does not want to admit collectively.

That is why chapter 10 follows self-sabotage. The repeating attitude from chapter 9 often has buried material underneath it. And once the reader sees how unwanted traits hide and leak, the next chapter can turn to envy, one of the least admitted shadow feelings in human nature.
