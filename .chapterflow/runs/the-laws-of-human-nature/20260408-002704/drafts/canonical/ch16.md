Greene uses Queen Elizabeth I because fickleness becomes easiest to understand when a ruler has to manage admiration, resentment, dependency, and rivalry all at once. William Cecil helps keep that authority intact, while Mary Queen of Scots shows the danger of making power too entangled with personal exposure and favoritism.

That is the law of fickleness. People want leadership, but they do not stay emotionally fixed toward leaders for long. They admire authority, test it, resent it, and quickly downgrade it when it looks needy, predictable, or too available. Followership is therefore unstable even when it sounds loyal.

This is why Greene keeps distance and appearances inside the law. Elizabeth did not try to make herself emotionally cheap. She preserved symbolic weight, controlled access, and let authority feel larger than the daily moods of the court. Cecil matters because he helped stabilize that form rather than flatten it into intimacy.

Mary Queen of Scots serves as the contrast. Greene uses her to show how authority weakens when leaders become too exposed, visibly dependent on favorites, or too easy for rivals to read and test. Once familiarity outruns form, affection and contempt start changing places quickly.

The practical lesson is not to become cold or theatrical for its own sake. It is to stop bargaining for authority through constant explanation, reassurance, and visible bids for approval. Leaders hold steadier ground when they make fewer emotional demands, avoid obvious favorites, and let consistency carry more of the burden than charm.

That is why this chapter follows conformity. The crowd that absorbs people is also volatile in how it treats those above it. And that is why aggression comes next: unstable followership often stores resentment behind compliance, politeness, and praise.
