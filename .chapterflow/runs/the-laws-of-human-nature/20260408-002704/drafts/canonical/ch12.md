Greene uses Michael Eisner because grandiosity is easiest to see when success stops acting like evidence and starts acting like permission. Early victories can make a person feel as if their instincts have become unusually trustworthy, almost exempt from ordinary limits.

That is the law of grandiosity. Greene argues that success can intoxicate the ego. Instead of reading achievement as a mix of effort, timing, help, and luck, people start reading it as proof that they possess special judgment. Confidence swells into success delusion.

This is why the chapter stays close to luck and limits. Grandiosity begins when the self edits those things out of the story. Praise narrows feedback. Admiration makes dissent harder to hear. The person starts believing that earlier wins authorize broader bets, larger risks, and weaker correction.

The practical signs are impatience with advice, contempt for constraints, and overreach. A leader expands too fast. A partner becomes hard to question. A student who has done well begins treating every objection as proof that others do not understand their vision. The outer style may still look bold or charismatic, but the inner problem is loss of proportion.

Greene is not arguing against ambition. The key distinction is between confidence and grandiosity. Confidence can tolerate reality, limits, and feedback. Grandiosity wants the self-image protected from friction, so it starts rewriting luck as destiny and criticism as blindness.

That is why grandiosity follows envy. Envy is one way fragile ego reacts to another person's height. Grandiosity is another way it escapes pain: by inflating the self. And once the ego hardens into an enlarged image of what it is, the next chapter can study another form of rigidity, the masculine or feminine mask people become trapped inside.
