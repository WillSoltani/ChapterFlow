Greene uses Caterina Sforza because gender rigidity is easiest to see when a person refuses the script others try to confine them to. Her force, command, and strategic toughness make the chapter begin with range rather than with stereotype.

That is the law of gender rigidity. Greene argues that many people overidentify with a narrow masculine or feminine image and then disown the complementary traits that would make them more whole. The result is not maturity but caricature.

This is why the chapter brings in anima and animus. The point is that the psyche contains capacities people may reject because they do not fit the role they are trying to perform. What is rejected often returns through projection. People become irritated, suspicious, or contemptuous toward others who display traits they secretly need themselves.

The practical signs are brittleness and overperformance. A person turns authority into hardness because tenderness feels dangerous. Another turns receptivity into passivity because forcefulness feels improper. The surface style may look principled, but the deeper issue is one-sided identity.

Greene is not arguing for sameness. The key distinction is between integration and caricature. Integration broadens range. It allows toughness without deadness, receptivity without helplessness, and a less defensive read of other people. Rigidity keeps the self narrow and reactive.

That is why this chapter follows grandiosity. An ego that hardens around an inflated image can also harden around a gendered mask. And once the reader loosens that mask, the next question becomes what fuller human range should be used for. That leads naturally into purpose and the law of aimlessness.
