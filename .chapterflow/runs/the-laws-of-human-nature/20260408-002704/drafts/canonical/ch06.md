Coco Chanel did not build desire by making herself endlessly available. Greene uses her to show a harsher truth about human wanting: people often value what seems partly withheld, independent, stylishly self-contained, or difficult to possess. Wanting grows when imagination has room to work.

This is the law of covetousness. The chapter argues that desire is not only about utility or goodness. It is shaped by scarcity, status, fantasy, and the forbidden edge around what looks not fully attainable. The same object can become more valuable in the mind once access tightens and certainty drops.

That is why oversupply is dangerous. If you explain everything, reveal everything, and remain instantly available at all times, you may reduce the imaginative charge around you or your work. Greene is not defending dishonesty. He is showing that mystery and selective absence give desire something to build on. The mind starts filling in what it cannot fully own.

Transgression also matters. What feels slightly improper, exclusive, or socially elevated often draws more attention than what feels fully safe and settled. People do not merely want the thing. They want the feeling of crossing into a higher or more charged space through it. Covetousness therefore mixes status hunger with emotional projection.

The chapter's hardest distinction is between self-possession and cheap withholding. Real allure comes from having a life, taste, standards, and independence that do not bend immediately toward approval. Empty game-playing eventually collapses because scarcity without substance becomes transparent. The point is not to act cold. It is to become less needy and less overavailable.

That also makes the chapter diagnostic. If you suddenly want something far more because it feels less available, you may be inside the covetousness loop yourself. Desire can inflate value and narrow judgment at the same time. That is why this law bridges into the next one: once wanting is activated, perspective often starts shrinking.
