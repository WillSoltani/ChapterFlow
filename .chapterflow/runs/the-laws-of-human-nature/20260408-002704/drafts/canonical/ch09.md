Anton Chekhov matters to Greene because Chekhov represents a person who refused to turn hardship into a permanent identity. His circumstances were real, but he kept enlarging his life by changing how he interpreted them and how he chose to act inside them.

That is the chapter's core move. Self-sabotage often begins in attitude. A resentful, fatalistic, or grievance-driven lens changes what the reader notices, what they attempt, and how they respond to setbacks. Over time the attitude helps recreate the very conditions it claims only to describe.

This is why Greene treats resentment as dangerous. It can feel like realism, but it often narrows freedom. Once a person keeps seeing insult, limitation, or proof of defeat everywhere, their next actions become smaller, harsher, or more withdrawn. The world then starts feeding the attitude back.

The practical answer is not denial. Greene does not ask the reader to pretend suffering is imaginary. He asks the reader to examine the interpretive lens itself and to ask what different action becomes possible once that lens loosens.

The law matters in daily life. A worker who reads one setback as destiny stops acting creatively. A partner who stores grievances keeps speaking from the old injury. A student who decides they are "just this kind of person" quietly reinforces the same limits semester after semester.

That is why chapter 9 follows defensiveness. What a person defended in conversation can become the attitude they defend in private. And once that inner stance hardens, the next question is what deeper material has been pushed down, which is why the next chapter moves into repression.
