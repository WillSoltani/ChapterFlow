Lyndon B. Johnson matters to Greene because Johnson understood that most people do not experience correction as information first. They experience it as a threat to how they see themselves. He read vanity, pride, insecurity, and the need to feel respected, then worked through those pressures instead of crashing straight into them.

That is defensiveness. People carry a self-opinion in which they are intelligent enough, decent enough, independent enough, or competent enough. When a conversation threatens that image, they often stop listening to the substance and start protecting the self.

This is why blunt honesty so often fails. A direct attack may contain the truth, but if it humiliates, corners, or morally shrinks the other person, resistance hardens. The argument becomes secondary. The ego alarm becomes primary.

Greene's practical move is not fake praise. It is to begin with what the person wants to feel about themselves, confirm some honest part of that picture, and let the next step preserve dignity. Ask questions. Offer room. Let them arrive at the correction with as much autonomy as possible.

The law matters in ordinary life. A manager who publicly exposes a mistake usually gets excuses instead of change. A partner who speaks from superiority gets counterattack instead of reflection. A student who corrects a classmate by making them look foolish gets silence or resentment instead of cooperation.

That is why chapter 8 follows shortsightedness. The reader first needs distance from immediate emotion. Then the reader can see that other people's resistance is rarely about logic alone. And once self-opinion hardens enough, it becomes attitude, which is why the next chapter turns to self-sabotage.
