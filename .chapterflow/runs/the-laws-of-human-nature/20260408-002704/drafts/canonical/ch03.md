Stalin understood something rotten about social life: if he controlled the emotional weather around him, he could control people who were supposedly serving ideas. Greene uses him to show what deep narcissism looks like at scale. The issue is not mere vanity. It is the inability to let other people exist as full minds with needs and perceptions of their own. They become mirrors, tools, or threats depending on whether they support the self-image.

That is why the chapter begins by broadening the term. Greene does not treat narcissism as a rare disorder that belongs only to monsters. Everyone begins with strong self-love. We all track slights, hunger for recognition, and interpret scenes through the pressure of our own importance. The danger appears when this baseline hardens into a wound that needs constant repair through admiration, control, or fantasy.

Tolstoy and Sonya show how this damage can live inside intimacy, not only politics. Two intelligent people can keep converting each disagreement into an injury to the self. Listening narrows, empathy collapses, and each person starts reading the other mainly as a source of pain or validation. The relationship becomes crowded with interpretation and starved of reciprocity.

Greene's corrective is empathy, but not in the soft or sentimental sense. Empathy means shifting attention outward long enough to register another person's motives, fears, and pressures without immediately translating them into your own drama. That move weakens narcissistic distortion because it loosens the assumption that every event is secretly about you.

Shackleton supplies the healthier counter-model. He had self-belief, ambition, and presence, but he kept taking in the morale of the crew around him. He did not need every scene to confirm his greatness. Because he could read others and absorb their condition, his authority steadied rather than consumed the group. Greene uses him to show that strong self-regard is not the enemy. Unchecked self-centrality is.

The chapter closes by making the practice uncomfortable. Empathy does not mean rescuing manipulative people, agreeing with everyone, or shrinking your own aims. It means refusing to let self-reference dominate the read. Once you can do that, another difficulty appears: people are still performing versions of themselves. The next law asks how to read those masks without sliding into suspicion and theatrical overinterpretation.
