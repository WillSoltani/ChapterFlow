Greene uses John D. Rockefeller because aggression is easier to miss when it does not need shouting to become effective. Rockefeller could pressure, control, and dominate while still appearing measured, disciplined, and outwardly civilized.

That is the law of aggression. Human beings carry aggressive impulses, but modern social life often forces those impulses into indirect channels. Instead of open attack, people punish through delay, guilt, sabotage, baiting, moral language, and passive aggression.

This is why Greene treats friendliness with more caution than naive people do. A pleasant surface can hide control. A calm tone can hide the wish to block, humiliate, or wear someone down. The point is not to assume malice everywhere. It is to stop assuming civility means harmlessness.

Rockefeller matters because he anchors aggression as disciplined power rather than emotional eruption. Greene wants the reader to see that some of the strongest aggressors are not the loudest. They are the ones who can keep hostility under control long enough to make it effective.

The practical warning signs are repeated obstruction, strategic confusion, guilt tactics, and polite contempt. A person smiles while undermining you. Another avoids direct conflict but creates friction everywhere. The pattern matters more than the single incident.

That is why this chapter follows fickleness. Resentment often hides before it strikes. And that is why the next chapter widens the frame: once the reader can see hidden hostility in people, Greene turns to the larger historical mood shaping whole generations.
