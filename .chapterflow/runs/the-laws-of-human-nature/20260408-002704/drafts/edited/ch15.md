Greene uses Gao Jianhua, Gao Yuan, and the Cultural Revolution because conformity becomes clearest when collective heat makes ordinary people absorb a group line they would not have created alone. The chapter begins where belonging, fear, and faction start rewriting judgment.

That is the law of conformity. Greene argues that groups exert a downward pull on the individual. Under pressure to belong, people imitate moods, outsource moral certainty, and start borrowing conviction from the crowd instead of testing things for themselves.

This is why the chapter focuses on group mind and faction. The danger is not cooperation by itself. The danger is emotional contagion strong enough to make dissent feel like betrayal and collective feeling feel like truth.

The practical signs are imitation, fear of isolation, and moral outsourcing. A person starts saying what the room says before thinking it through. Another joins a pile-on because standing apart feels too socially expensive. The group becomes a shortcut for judgment.

Gao Jianhua and Gao Yuan matter because they give the chapter a concrete anchor for how far conformity can go when group emotion intensifies and reality gets filtered through faction. Greene uses them to warn that ordinary people are more absorbable than they assume.

That is why this chapter follows aimlessness. A person without inner direction is easier for the group to steer. And that is why the next chapter turns to fickleness: once the reader sees how unstable collective feeling can be, the next question is how leaders work with that volatility without becoming captive to it.
