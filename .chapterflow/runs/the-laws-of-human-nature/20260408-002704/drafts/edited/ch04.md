Milton Erickson became a powerful observer because he had to. During recovery from polio, he could not rely on movement or speed, so he began studying the gap between what people said and what their faces, posture, pacing, and tone quietly revealed. Greene uses him to open the law of role-playing because social life runs on managed surfaces. People do not show everything they feel, and their words almost never tell the whole story.

The chapter becomes useful when it stops treating masks as rare evidence of corruption. People learn early that open fear, envy, irritation, and uncertainty can be punished or ignored. So they build social faces. They act calm, agreeable, diligent, or confident. That adaptation is normal. The point is not to despise it. The point is to understand that every polished interaction contains more than the declared message.

What leaks through are not magical tells but contradictions and clusters. A person says yes while their body freezes. They flatter in public and go cold once attention moves away. Their baseline changes sharply under pressure. Greene wants the reader to watch sequences, repetition, and context rather than grab one signal and pretend certainty. Observation is about probability, not theater.

That boundary becomes essential in the Othello warning. Projection can contaminate the read so badly that the observer starts inventing proof for the emotion already inside them. Jealousy, resentment, or hunger can make neutral behavior look loaded. The skill therefore requires two directions of attention at once: outward toward other people's leakage and inward toward your own interpretive distortion.

Greene also insists that role-playing is not only something to decode in others. Since people judge by surfaces, you have to manage your own. Clear presence, strong first impressions, and conscious social adaptation are not inherently fake. They become false only when the surface is used to substitute for substance rather than to carry it effectively.

The hard edge is that signal-reading is still not character-reading. A mixed signal may tell you how someone feels now, not who they are in a durable sense. You need repetition, adversity, and time before you can call it character. That is why this law has to hand off to the next one. Once you can see through the mask, you still need a better method for judging what lies underneath it over the long run.
