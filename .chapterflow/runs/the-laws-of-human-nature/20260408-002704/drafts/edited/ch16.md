Greene uses Queen Elizabeth I because the problem of fickleness shows up most clearly where authority has to survive inside unstable public feeling. Elizabeth had to govern a court full of admiration, rivalry, dependency, and resentment. William Cecil helps the chapter because he understood that authority needed form, distance, and steadiness to survive those emotional swings. Mary Queen of Scots gives Greene the contrast case: leadership made too exposed, too personal, and too easy for others to test.

That is the law of fickleness. People say they want strong leadership, but their feelings toward leaders do not stay fixed for long. They admire authority when it seems self-possessed, then resent it when it feels restrictive, then test it when it looks needy or too familiar. Followership is unstable because people want direction without wanting to feel overruled, and they quickly change their valuation of whoever leads them.

This is why Greene keeps distance and appearances inside the law. Elizabeth did not make herself emotionally cheap. She controlled access, protected symbolic weight, and avoided letting courtiers feel they fully possessed her. Authority remained partly larger than the daily mood of the room. Cecil matters because he understood that a ruler weakened herself when she made power too ordinary or too easy to consume.

Mary Queen of Scots shows the opposite pressure. Greene uses her to show how authority suffers when the leader becomes visibly entangled with favorites, romance, overexposure, or reactive emotion. Once people can read too much need, dependence, or inconsistency, admiration starts sliding into judgment. Familiarity does not simply humanize authority. It can also invite contempt, gossip, faction, and tests of power.

The practical warning signs are overexplaining, approval-chasing, favoritism, and too much emotional availability. A leader who keeps asking to be liked trains followers to judge leadership through mood. A leader who keeps one obvious favorite invites rivalry and instability. A leader who answers every challenge with more exposure often makes authority look defensive rather than settled.

Greene's point is not that leadership should become cold theater. The point is steadiness. People follow more readily when the leader's position feels consistent, bounded, and not hungry for constant affection. Measured distance, fewer emotional bids, and symbolic consistency help authority survive public volatility better than charm alone.

That is why the chapter follows conformity. The crowd that can absorb individuals is also unstable in how it prices those above it. And that is why aggression comes next: once loyalty turns uncertain, resentment often remains hidden for a while behind agreeable language and compliant faces.
