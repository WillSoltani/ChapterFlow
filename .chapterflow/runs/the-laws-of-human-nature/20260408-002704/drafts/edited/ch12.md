Greene uses Michael Eisner because grandiosity is easiest to understand when success stops acting like evidence and starts acting like permission. Early victories can make a person feel as if their instincts have become unusually trustworthy, almost exempt from ordinary limits.

That is the law of grandiosity. Greene argues that success can intoxicate the ego. Instead of reading achievement as a mix of effort, timing, help, and luck, people start reading it as proof of special judgment. Confidence swells into success delusion.

This is why the chapter keeps returning to luck and limits. Grandiosity begins when the self edits them out of the story. Praise narrows feedback. Admiration makes dissent harder to hear. The person starts believing that earlier wins authorize broader bets, larger risks, and weaker correction.

The practical signs are impatience with advice, contempt for constraints, and overreach. A leader expands too fast. A partner becomes hard to question. A student who has done well begins treating every objection as proof that others simply cannot see the bigger picture. The outer style may still look bold or charismatic, but the inner problem is loss of proportion.

Greene is not arguing against ambition. The key distinction is between confidence and grandiosity. Confidence can tolerate reality, limits, and correction. Grandiosity wants the self-image protected from friction, so it rewrites luck as destiny and criticism as blindness.

That is why grandiosity follows envy. Envy is one response to ego fragility under comparison. Grandiosity is another: the ego expands itself so it no longer has to feel ordinary. Once that inflated image hardens, the next chapter can study another kind of rigidity, the trap of living inside an exaggerated masculine or feminine mask.
