Anton Chekhov matters to Greene because Chekhov represents someone who refused to let hardship become a permanent inner posture. His circumstances were real, but he kept enlarging his life by changing how he interpreted them and by choosing different actions inside them.

That is the law of self-sabotage. It often begins in attitude. A resentful, fatalistic, or grievance-driven lens changes what the reader notices, what they attempt, and how they answer setbacks. Over time the attitude helps recreate the very conditions it claims merely to describe.

This is why Greene treats resentment as dangerous. It can feel like realism, but it often narrows freedom. Once a person keeps seeing insult, limitation, or proof of defeat everywhere, their next moves become smaller, harsher, or more withdrawn. The world then starts feeding the attitude back.

The practical answer is not denial. Greene does not ask the reader to pretend suffering is imaginary. He asks the reader to inspect the lens itself and to ask what different action becomes possible once that lens loosens.

The law matters in ordinary life. A worker who reads one setback as destiny stops acting creatively. A partner who stores grievances keeps speaking from the old injury. A student who decides they are simply "this kind of person" quietly reinforces the same limit semester after semester.

That is why chapter 9 follows defensiveness. What a person defended in conversation can become the attitude they defend in private. And once that inner stance hardens, the next question is what deeper material has been pushed down, which is why the next chapter moves into repression.
