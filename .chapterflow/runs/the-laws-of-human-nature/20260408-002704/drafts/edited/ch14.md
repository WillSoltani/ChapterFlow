Greene uses Martin Luther King Jr. because aimlessness becomes easiest to see when placed beside a life that gathered itself around mission. King had gifts, ambition, and urgency before he became a national figure, but the chapter treats purpose as the force that organized those qualities instead of letting them remain scattered.

That is the law of aimlessness. Many people move constantly, react quickly, and even stay productive, yet still drift because no deeper direction arranges their choices. Without purpose, energy leaks into distraction, borrowed goals, prestige chasing, and moods imported from other people.

This is why Greene separates real purpose from false purpose. False purpose is the admired script: the path that looks important, sounds impressive, or wins applause. Real purpose is steadier. It grows from recurring inclination, from the problems a person feels pulled toward, and from willingness to organize life around that pull.

The practical signs of aimlessness are fragmentation and restlessness. A person starts many things and abandons them. Another becomes very busy but changes direction with every reward signal. The result is boredom, envy of other people's certainty, and a life too easy to distract.

Martin Luther King Jr. matters because he gives the chapter a concrete counterexample to that drift. Greene uses him to show how purpose channels emotion, sharpens sacrifice, and makes discipline sustainable. Purpose does not eliminate fear or doubt, but it prevents the self from being scattered by every new lure.

That is why this chapter follows gender rigidity. Once the reader loosens a cramped identity, the next task is not endless openness. It is direction. And that is why the next chapter turns to conformity: without purpose, the self is much easier for group pressure to capture.
