Richard Nixon matters to Greene because Nixon represents someone who built a public image around discipline, control, and strategic realism while darker insecurity, hostility, and suspicion kept leaking through the surface. Nixon is the warning that what is pushed down does not disappear just because it is hidden.

That is the law of repression. Greene argues that people disown traits they cannot fit into the image they want to keep of themselves. Carl Jung gives him the key term for that denied side: the shadow. Repression is not removal. It is denial plus concealment, and the denied material remains active underneath the mask.

This is why Greene focuses on projection and leakage. A person who cannot admit ambition, envy, cruelty, vanity, or need in themselves often starts seeing those traits everywhere else. They moralize, overreact, gossip, scapegoat, or erupt in ways that reveal more than they intend.

The practical answer is not indulgence. Greene does not tell the reader to obey every impulse because it feels authentic. He asks the reader to notice recurring triggers, fantasies, disproportionate judgments, and moments of unexplained heat. Those are clues that the shadow is active and asking to be recognized rather than disguised.

The law matters in ordinary life. A manager who condemns selfish ambition may be defending against their own hunger for status. A partner who keeps accusing the other person of manipulation may be hiding their own need to control. A student group that isolates one member may be exporting insecurity it does not want to admit collectively.

That is why chapter 10 follows self-sabotage. The repeated attitude from chapter 9 often has buried material underneath it. And once the reader sees how unwanted traits hide and leak, the next chapter can turn to envy, one of the least admitted shadow feelings in human nature.
