Greene uses John D. Rockefeller because aggression becomes easier to understand when it is detached from theatrical rage. Rockefeller could pressure, dominate, and control without needing constant visible outbursts. The chapter uses him to show that some of the most effective aggression arrives in disciplined, outwardly civilized form.

That is the law of aggression. Human beings carry aggressive drives, but open attack is costly in most modern settings. People therefore push hostility into indirect channels. Instead of blunt assault, they punish through delay, guilt, sabotage, baiting, passive aggression, or smiling forms of control that leave them plausible deniability.

This is why Greene asks the reader to stop reading friendliness as innocence by default. A pleasant tone can hide resentment. A helpful gesture can hide control. Strategic silence, confusion, moral superiority, or chronic delay can all serve aggressive aims when direct conflict feels too risky.

Rockefeller matters because he anchors aggression as controlled power rather than raw temper. Greene wants the reader to see that the strongest aggressors are often not the loudest people in the room. They are the people who can hold hostility in disciplined form and apply it where it creates leverage.

The practical warning signs are repeated obstruction, covert punishment, guilt tactics, and polite contempt. A coworker smiles while slowing every decision. A partner says nothing directly hostile but keeps creating friction, withdrawal, or confusion when they feel injured. A student leader speaks in polished language while using procedure to exclude rivals. The single moment can be ambiguous. The pattern is what turns pressure into readable aggression.

Greene's point is not to turn the reader paranoid. It is to reduce naivete. Not every awkward interaction hides malice, but repeated concealed pressure should not be explained away forever as personality style. The right response is distance, pattern recognition, and clearer boundaries rather than argument inside the aggressor's frame.

That is why this chapter follows fickleness. Unstable loyalty often stores resentment before it becomes visible. And that is why the next chapter widens the lens to generational myopia: after reading hidden hostility in individuals, Greene turns to the larger moods and timing errors that shape whole historical periods.
