Greene uses Mary Shelley and Jane Williams because they give envy a human face. Another person's ease, beauty, talent, or closeness can feel less like neutral information and more like a painful subtraction from the self.

That is the law of envy. Greene argues that fragile egos turn comparison into status pain. Another person's advantage can feel like evidence that the reader is smaller, less loved, less gifted, or less secure than they want to believe.

This is why envy usually hides. It feels humiliating to admit. Instead of saying, "I want what they have" or "their success hurts me," people disguise the emotion. It appears as poisonous praise, coolness, moral criticism, quiet sabotage, or secret pleasure when the other person falters.

The practical distinction is between envy and emulation. Emulation says that another person's excellence can teach or inspire. Envy wants to pull the other person downward so the comparison stops hurting. Greene wants the reader to catch that turn before admiration curdles into hostility.

The law matters in ordinary life. A worker congratulates a colleague but adds a cutting edge to the praise. A friend becomes distant after another friend's good news. A student group says it is only defending standards while quietly reacting to rank anxiety and comparison.

That is why envy follows repression. Envy is one of the feelings people most want to hide from themselves. And once Greene has shown how fragile ego suffers under comparison, the next chapter can study another compensation for ego weakness: grandiosity.
