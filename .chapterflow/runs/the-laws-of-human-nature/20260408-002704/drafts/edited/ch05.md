Howard Hughes looked like the kind of man people call strong before they have paid the bill for trusting him. Greene uses Hughes to show why talent, glamour, and daring are not the same thing as character. A person can be magnetic, inventive, and high-performing while still being ruled by compulsion, rigidity, vanity, or fear. The surface can impress long before the deeper pattern becomes expensive.

That deeper pattern is what Greene means by character. Character is not the mood of a given day and it is not the story a person tells about themselves. It is what keeps recurring when pressure rises, admiration fades, plans stall, or criticism lands. The reader is asked to move from impression to pattern because impressions are cheap and patterns keep submitting evidence.

Compulsive behavior is the chapter's method clue. People tend to return to familiar emotional grooves: blame, avoidance, self-pity, overcontrol, attention hunger, passivity, or panic. These grooves surface most clearly when life becomes inconvenient. A missed deadline, a correction from a boss, a boring phase of work, a practical sacrifice in a relationship. Greene wants the reader to notice who adapts, who learns, who finishes, who collaborates, and who keeps making others absorb the cost of the same weakness.

This is why strong character usually looks quieter than charm. It shows up in resilience, work discipline, honesty about fault, and the ability to stay functional under setback. Weak character often borrows prestige from talent or intensity, but the pattern leaks through in excuses, inconsistency, hidden entitlement, and repeated inability to face reality cleanly. The danger is not that weak-character people do nothing well. The danger is that the cost of relying on them keeps compounding.

The chapter also attacks a favorite form of self-deception: treating apology as evidence of change. Explanations can be true and still be strategically useless. A person may genuinely regret what they did and still repeat it because the underlying pattern has not moved. Greene is not asking the reader to become cold. He is asking for disciplined mercy, which means compassion without surrendering the evidence.

The inward turn matters just as much. If you want stronger character, you build it through repeated acts of reality contact: finishing difficult work, tolerating frustration without dramatizing it, correcting yourself earlier, and making your promises cheaper to say and harder to break. Chapter 4 sharpened the immediate read. Chapter 5 deepens the file. It teaches the reader to judge trust by what a person keeps becoming.
