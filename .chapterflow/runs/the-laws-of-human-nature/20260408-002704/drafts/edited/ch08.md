Lyndon B. Johnson matters to Greene because Johnson understood that correction rarely lands as pure information. It lands as a threat to how a person sees themselves. He read pride, insecurity, vanity, and the need to feel respected, then worked with those pressures instead of charging straight through them.

That is defensiveness. People carry a self-opinion in which they are intelligent enough, decent enough, autonomous enough, or competent enough. When a conversation threatens that inner picture, they often stop listening to the content and start protecting the self.

This is why blunt honesty fails more often than people admit. A direct attack may contain the truth, but if it humiliates, corners, or morally shrinks the other person, resistance hardens. The argument becomes secondary. The ego alarm becomes primary.

Greene's practical move is not fake praise. It is to start with what the person wants to feel about themselves, confirm some honest part of that picture, and then guide the conversation in a way that lets dignity survive. Ask questions. Offer room. Let the other person reach the correction with as much agency as possible.

The law matters everywhere. A manager who exposes a mistake in public usually gets excuses instead of change. A partner who speaks from superiority gets counterattack instead of reflection. A student who corrects a peer by making them look foolish gets resentment instead of cooperation.

That is why chapter 8 matters after shortsightedness. The reader first needs distance from immediate emotion. Then the reader can see that resistance is rarely about logic alone. Once self-opinion hardens enough, it becomes attitude, and attitude starts shaping circumstances, which is why the next chapter turns to self-sabotage.
