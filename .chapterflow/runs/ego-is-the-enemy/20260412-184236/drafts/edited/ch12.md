Success gives ego more to explain. Results, praise, and stronger standing do not only change what happens around a person. They also make it easier to build a flattering account of what those events supposedly prove. This chapter warns against that move. The danger is not only public image. It is the private story that turns success into proof of specialness or permanent correctness.

That story feels useful because it creates emotional order. After uncertainty and effort, a neat explanation can feel like control. The person starts arranging events around an identity: the win means I was right all along, the praise means my judgment is cleaner than other people's, the setback must only be misunderstanding. Ego likes that arrangement because it protects the self from ambiguity.

The chapter treats that comfort as distortion. Once a person starts telling a story to himself, facts get filtered through identity. Criticism becomes easier to dismiss. Warning signs become easier to minimize. The story does not need to be dramatic to be dangerous. It only needs to make reality answer to the self instead of the self answering to reality.

So the chapter pushes the reader back toward plainness. Do not explain success in the most flattering way by default. Do not turn a result into a myth. Staying a student helps here because studenthood resists the urge to finalize meaning around the self.

That sequence matters. Studenthood keeps learning open, which makes self-story harder to stabilize. If learning closes, narrative comfort rushes in. The next chapter can then ask what actually matters once ego stops trying to write the script alone.
