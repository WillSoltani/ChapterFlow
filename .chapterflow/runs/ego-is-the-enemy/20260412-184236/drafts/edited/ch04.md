Passion feels like seriousness because it is vivid. It speaks loudly, moves quickly, and gives a person the impression that strong emotion must mean strong commitment. The chapter pushes against that impression. Ego loves passion for the same reason it loves any dramatic surface: passion can make you feel intense and important before the harder question is answered. Can you keep working when the feeling changes?

That is the problem with ego-heavy intensity. It is often mood-dependent. A person surges into the work, speaks in absolute terms, frames the effort like destiny, and mistakes emotional heat for durable strength. The performance can look convincing. It can even persuade the person inside it. But if the work relies on that temperature, then the work has been tied to something unstable.

Discipline is the chapter's answer because discipline survives mood. It is steadier, less flattering, and less theatrical. That makes it easy for ego to underrate. Yet discipline is what keeps ambition alive once excitement fades, once obstacles become repetitive, and once the work stops offering dramatic proof that you are special for attempting it.

This is why the chapter is not anti-caring. It is anti-self-dramatization. Passion becomes dangerous when a person begins loving the feeling of intensity more than the standard the work requires. Then the emotional experience starts acting like evidence. The person sounds committed, feels committed, and may even be admired for commitment while the actual pace of the work remains too dependent on mood.

The attraction matters. Passion looks noble. It looks pure. It can also make a person socially impressive because intensity is visible in a way steadiness is not. The chapter keeps that temptation in view so the correction stays honest. Ego prefers dramatic force because dramatic force is easier to display than controlled repetition.

So the chapter chooses the less glamorous loyalty. Work governed by discipline may look cooler from the outside, but it is more reliable when the task gets boring, humiliating, or slow. That reliability is what studenthood needed all along. Once ambition is teachable, it still has to keep moving after the emotional surge dies. The next chapter can therefore turn toward tactical humility and indirect leverage.
