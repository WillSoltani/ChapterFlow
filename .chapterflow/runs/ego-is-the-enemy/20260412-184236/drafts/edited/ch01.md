Ambition can leak away before the work begins. Not only through laziness or failure, but through speech that lets ego collect the reward too early. A person starts describing the project, the future role, the discipline they are about to embody. The words sound serious. Other people nod. The speaker feels a little larger. Nothing has been built yet, but the appetite for recognition has already been fed.

The Isocrates-style warning matters because eloquence can look like momentum. Someone sounds committed, informed, destined, and the room begins responding to that image. The danger is not just that other people are impressed. The deeper danger is that the speaker is impressed. Once the future self has been narrated publicly, some of the internal pressure eases. Talk starts doing emotional work that should have belonged to labor.

Sherman supplies the cleaner corrective. The point is not silence as a saintly pose. It is privacy as discipline. When effort stays private, ambition stays hungry. It has not been cushioned by applause, advice, or fascinated attention. The work still has to carry the whole burden. That makes labor feel harsher at the start, but it also makes it cleaner. You are no longer dividing energy between building something and being seen as the kind of person who builds things.

That division is the chapter's real mechanism. Ego does not only want credit for finished excellence. It also wants to be recognized for intention, taste, seriousness, and potential. It wants identity before contribution. That is why talking can become a trap. A person begins mistaking the management of impressions for progress itself. Meetings feel productive because the ambition has been narrated well. Planning notes feel substantial because they are easy to share. The visible surface grows while the actual work stays thin.

The chapter would flatten into cliche if it simply said never talk. Some speech is necessary for coordination, help, and alignment. The harder distinction is whether the words make the work more likely to exist or whether they are giving ego a stage before reality has tested anything. Speech that serves execution is one thing. Speech that manufactures identity is another. Both can sound confident, but only one leaves a trail of completed effort.

That is why the opener is stricter than ordinary anti-bragging advice. Restraint keeps reality in charge. If the work is still weak, silence prevents the weakness from hiding inside a persuasive self-description. If the work becomes strong, it will eventually show itself. Ambition is safer when it is busy proving something instead of declaring something. From there the next pressure becomes unavoidable: if you are not trying to be seen as important, what should your ambition actually be trying to do?
