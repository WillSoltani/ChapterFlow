Success can look more stable once a person learns restraint, yet ego still has one more place to hide. It can make the self the reference point for everything. That is the warning in "Beware the disease of me."

The danger is not ordinary self-concern. People have needs, limits, and responsibilities. The problem starts when every event gets sorted by what it says about me, does to me, or means for my importance. Success then stops being a field of work and turns into a mirror.

When that shift happens, judgment narrows. Relationships distort because disagreement feels personal, delay feels insulting, and neutral facts begin to look charged with private meaning. The world has not actually become centered on one person, but ego keeps reading it that way.

Proportion breaks down too. Small setbacks swell because they strike the self-image. Other people shrink into supporting roles in a private drama. The person may still look active and confident, yet the scale of things is wrong because everything keeps bending back toward the self.

So the chapter asks for decentering. The aim is not self-neglect. It is recovering relation, scale, and perspective. Success becomes less corrupting when the self stops being the measure of every conversation, conflict, and outcome.

That turn prepares the next step in the book. Once the self loses its oversized place, a wider perspective becomes possible.
