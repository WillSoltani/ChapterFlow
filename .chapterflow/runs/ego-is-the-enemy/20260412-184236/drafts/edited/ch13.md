Success can create a new kind of confusion. More options, invitations, and rewards start appearing, and a person can become busy around what looks important without staying close to what actually matters. This chapter cuts through that drift by asking a clarifying question: what is important to you?

The question matters because ego likes substitutes. Recognition can feel like importance. Praise can feel like priority. A crowded calendar can feel like relevance. The chapter treats those signals as unreliable when they are not tied back to real values. Success can multiply activity while weakening judgment about what deserves commitment.

So the chapter is not asking for a dramatic mission statement or a spike of feeling. It is asking for value clarity. What matters enough to guide choices when ego offers image, prestige, and noise as easier replacements? Without that clarity, a person starts organizing life around ego rewards and calling it direction.

That is how drift begins. Once real importance gets blurred, success is easier to spend on what flatters the self but does not serve anything durable. A person can say yes to the wrong opportunities, defend the wrong priorities, and mistake visibility for substance. The chapter answers that risk by returning the reader to a stricter filter.

That filter is importance itself. Not what looks impressive. Not what sounds urgent. Not what flatters the current story. What actually matters? The chapter needs that question because the next danger in success is more defensive. When values weaken, entitlement, control, and paranoia find more room to grow.
