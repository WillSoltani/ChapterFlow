Ego dislikes delay. When a feeling arrives, ego wants to spend it immediately. It wants the reaction sent, the opinion voiced, the frustration released, the intensity displayed. That urge can feel honest because it is vivid. The chapter challenges that assumption. Immediate expression is not automatically strength. It often means the self would rather discharge pressure than protect the work.

Restraint is the chapter's answer. Not silence for its own sake, and not fear dressed up as wisdom. Restraint is control over timing. It asks whether the impulse serves the situation, the task, and the longer objective before it becomes speech or action. The chapter treats that control as power because power is partly the ability to keep energy in reserve until it can be used well.

Ego resists this because expression feels like identity. If you do not say everything the moment it arrives, part of you can feel erased. If you do not react instantly, you may worry that the room will not know what you think or how strongly you care. The chapter keeps that tension visible. Restraint can feel like a loss in the short term because the self has grown used to treating release as proof of aliveness.

But uncontrolled release can damage the very thing ambition claims to want. A premature email can harden conflict. An unnecessary opinion can crowd out better timing. A loud reaction can make the moment more about the person than about the work. The chapter's point is not that expression is bad. Expression becomes weak when it outruns judgment.

That is why restraint belongs with ambition rather than against it. Controlled ambition is not smaller ambition. It is ambition that can choose when to appear, when to wait, and when to keep its force from leaking out in useless directions. The person who can hold an impulse without spending it immediately often has more room to act effectively a little later.

So the chapter shifts the measure of strength. Do not ask only whether you felt strongly enough to react. Ask whether your timing protected the work, preserved options, and kept your effort from collapsing into self-display. Once that question becomes normal, the next problem becomes easier to see. Ego is not only loud in public. It is also loud inside the mind, constantly circling back to itself.
