You can waste ambition before the work even starts. Not by failing, not by being lazy, but by talking in a way that lets ego collect the reward ahead of time. A person begins describing the project, the role, the future life, the discipline they are about to embody. The words sound serious. Other people nod. The speaker feels a little larger, a little more defined. But nothing has been built yet. The chapter's opening suspicion is that this feeling is not innocent. It is ego feeding on attention that should have been earned later, if at all.

The Isocrates-style warning matters because it points at a familiar confusion: eloquence can look like momentum. Someone sounds committed, sounds informed, sounds destined, and everyone around them begins responding to that image. The danger is not only that others are fooled. The deeper danger is that the speaker is fooled too. Once the future self has been narrated publicly, the pressure to become it can weaken instead of sharpen. Part of the psychic payment has already arrived. Talk has started doing the emotional job that work was supposed to do.

Sherman functions as the harder corrective. The point is not romantic silence for its own sake. It is privacy as discipline. When effort stays private, the ambition remains hungry. It has not been padded with applause, advice, or fascinated attention. The work still has to carry the whole burden. That makes labor harsher in one sense because there is less social reward on the front end. It also makes labor cleaner because the person is not constantly splitting energy between building something and being seen as the kind of person who builds things.

That split is the mechanism the chapter keeps pressing. Ego does not always ask to be praised for finished excellence. Often it asks to be recognized for intention, taste, seriousness, or potential. It wants the identity before the contribution. That is why talking can be so dangerous at the start. A person begins mistaking the management of impressions for the making of progress. They become a curator of future success. Meetings feel productive because the ambition has been narrated crisply. Planning notes feel substantial because they are easy to share. The visible surface grows while the actual work remains thin.

The chapter would become shallow if it ended with a crude command to say nothing. Some words are necessary. You have to coordinate, ask for help, and clarify what is being built. The real line is narrower and more demanding. Are the words increasing the work's chances of existing, or are they giving ego a performance stage before reality has tested anything? Speech that serves execution is one thing. Speech that manufactures identity is another. The difficulty is that both can sound equally confident at first.

That is why the opening chapter is harsher than simple anti-bragging advice. It treats restraint as a way to keep reality in charge. If the work is still weak, silence prevents the person from hiding that weakness inside a persuasive self-description. If the work becomes strong, it will eventually make itself legible. Either way, ambition is safer when it is busy proving something instead of busy declaring something. Once that pressure is accepted, the next question appears immediately: if you are not trying to be seen as important, what exactly should your ambition be trying to do?
