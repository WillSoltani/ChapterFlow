Success can look more stable once a person learns restraint, but ego still has one more place to hide. It can make the self the reference point for everything. That is the warning in "Beware the disease of me."

The danger is not ordinary self-concern. People have needs, limits, and responsibilities. The problem begins when every event gets sorted by what it says about me, does to me, or means for my importance. Success then stops being a field of work and becomes a mirror.

When that happens, judgment narrows. Relationships become distorted because disagreement feels personal, delay feels insulting, and neutral facts start looking loaded with private meaning. The world has not actually become centered on one person, but ego keeps reading it that way.

This also ruins proportion. Small setbacks feel huge because they strike the self-image. Other people shrink into supporting roles in a private drama. The person is still active and may even look confident, yet the scale of things is off because everything keeps circling back to the self.

So the chapter asks for decentering. The aim is not self-neglect. It is recovering relation, scale, and perspective. Success becomes less corrupting when the self stops being the measure of every conversation, conflict, and outcome.

That move sets up the next step in the book. Once the self loses its oversized place, a wider perspective becomes possible.
