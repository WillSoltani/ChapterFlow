Success can become defensive long before it openly collapses. A person starts feeling more owed, more responsible for controlling everything, and more suspicious of ordinary disagreement or uncertainty. This chapter names that cluster directly: entitlement, control, and paranoia.

The three pressures belong together because they feed one another. Entitlement makes the self feel that success should now come with more deference, smoother conditions, or tighter obedience from other people. Once that feeling grows, control starts looking reasonable. The person begins managing too much, holding too tightly, and treating every variable as if it must be secured.

Paranoia follows naturally from that posture. If success is being treated as possession, then other people, friction, or unpredictability begin to look more threatening than they are. The person grows suspicious not because the world became wholly hostile, but because ego is trying to protect what it cannot make permanently safe.

That is why the chapter is not merely about strong standards or responsible stewardship. Real responsibility can delegate, trust, and remain proportionate. Ego-driven control cannot. It reads disagreement as insult, uncertainty as danger, and shared success as a threat to private ownership. The chapter wants the reader to see how defensive this version of success has become.

The cluster matters because it corrodes judgment. Entitlement narrows perspective. Overcontrol weakens trust. Suspicion makes ordinary reality look adversarial. The next move in the book is therefore exact: before this posture hardens further, the person has to learn how to manage the self instead of trying to possess everything around the self.
