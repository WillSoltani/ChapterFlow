Success creates a new kind of confusion. Once more options, invitations, and rewards appear, a person can become busy around what looks important without staying close to what actually matters. This chapter cuts through that drift. It asks a clarifying question: what is important to you?

That question matters because ego loves substitutes. Recognition can feel like importance. Praise can feel like priority. A crowded calendar can feel like relevance. But the chapter treats those signals as unreliable if they are not tied back to real values. Success can multiply activity while weakening judgment about what deserves commitment.

So the chapter is not asking for a burst of feeling or a dramatic mission statement. It is asking for value clarity. What matters enough to guide choices when ego is offering image, noise, and prestige as easier replacements? Without that clarity, a person starts organizing life around ego rewards and calling it direction.

The danger is drift. Once real importance is blurred, success becomes easier to waste on what flatters the self but does not serve anything durable. A person can say yes to the wrong opportunities, defend the wrong priorities, and mistake visibility for substance. The chapter tries to stop that by returning the reader to a stricter filter.

That filter is importance itself. Not what looks impressive. Not what sounds urgent. Not what flatters the current story. What actually matters? The chapter needs that question because the next danger in success is even more defensive. When values weaken, entitlement, control, and paranoia have more room to grow.
