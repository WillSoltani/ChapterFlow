Success becomes unstable when a person keeps trying to manage the world around the self before managing the self inside it. This chapter turns the correction inward. Its claim is simple: the real management problem is often you.

That does not mean external action stops mattering. It means action gets cleaner only when the self is governable first. If entitlement, control, and paranoia are still driving behavior, then even competent action will carry defensive distortion with it. The chapter therefore shifts the focus from dominating externals to governing reactions, conduct, and ego pressure.

This is harder than it sounds because managing the room can feel more powerful than managing the self. It is easier to tighten control around other people, systems, or plans than to steady your own conduct under frustration, praise, or uncertainty. The chapter says that shortcut fails. Outer domination cannot solve inner disorder.

So self-management here means inward governance. It means keeping the self from turning success into reactivity, instability, or overreach. A person who can govern the self acts with more proportion because behavior is no longer being outsourced to emotion, ego, or defensive urgency.

That is why this chapter belongs here. After the loop of entitlement, control, and paranoia is exposed, the next step is not better force. It is better self-governance. If that does not happen, success can still curdle inward and become centered on the self in another way: the disease of me.
