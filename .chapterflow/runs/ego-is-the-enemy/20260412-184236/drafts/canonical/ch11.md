Success creates a subtle temptation: to think that proof has changed your relationship to learning. Praise, wins, and stronger standing can make a person feel less like an apprentice and more like someone who has finally earned the right to stop being corrected. This chapter resists that shift. It says success does not cancel studenthood. It makes studenthood more necessary.

That pressure is easy to understand. During aspiration, being a student may feel temporary. A person tells himself that once the work starts paying off, the uncertain phase will end. Success gives that hope better material. Recognition can make teachability look optional because the world begins to confirm the identity the self already wants to protect.

The problem is that ego loves a finished self. It wants wins to become evidence that correction is now beneath you or only for other people. The chapter pushes against that false finish. If success turns learning into something occasional or decorative, then the person becomes harder to help and slower to notice drift.

So studenthood in this section is not beginner weakness. It is discipline under flattering conditions. It means treating praise as a harder test of humility, not as graduation from it. The person who stays a student after success keeps room for revision, instruction, and discomfort even when certainty would be easier to perform.

That is why this chapter belongs at the opening of Success. The same enemy crossed the threshold in the interstitial, and the first answer is not celebration but continued teachability. Once that posture is active, the next threat becomes easier to see: the self-story that starts explaining success back to the self in flattering ways.
