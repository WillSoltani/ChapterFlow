Success gives ego more material to interpret. Results, praise, and stronger standing do not just change what happens around a person. They change the story the person can start telling about what those events mean. This chapter warns against that move. It says the danger is not only public image. It is the private narrative that starts turning success into proof of specialness, destiny, or permanent correctness.

That story is tempting because it feels stabilizing. After uncertainty and effort, a neat explanation can feel like control. The person begins to arrange events around a flattering identity: the win means I was right all along, the praise means my judgment is cleaner than other people's, the setback must only be misunderstanding. Ego prefers that arrangement because it protects the self from ambiguity.

The chapter treats that comfort as distortion. Once a person starts telling a story to himself, facts get filtered through identity. Criticism sounds less informative. Warning signs look less important. The narrative does not need to be dramatic to be dangerous. It only needs to make reality answer to the self instead of the self answering to reality.

So the chapter pushes the reader back toward plainness. Do not rush to explain your success in the most flattering way. Do not build a myth where there may only be a result, a pattern, or a temporary advantage. Staying a student helps here because studenthood resists the need to finalize meaning around the self.

That is why this chapter follows naturally after studenthood. If learning stays open, stories have a harder time hardening into identity. If learning closes, narrative comfort quickly takes over. The next section of Success will keep testing that realism by asking what actually matters once ego stops writing the script.
