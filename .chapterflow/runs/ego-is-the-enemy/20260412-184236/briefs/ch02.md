Core Claim:
The chapter asks ambition to choose between two incompatible aims: being seen as important and doing important work. It uses John Boyd as the anchor for refusing status bait when status would weaken contribution. The chapter therefore turns ego from an emotional issue into a directional one. What matters is where ambition points itself.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
This chapter is not merely another warning against talking. It sharpens the opener into a fork: do you want rank, image, and external confirmation, or do you want useful work even when it costs you those things? That makes the chapter a decision rule, not just a restraint rule.

Author Logic Chain:
First the chapter presents the be-versus-do fork as a permanent pressure inside ambition.
Then John Boyd shows what it looks like to choose contribution over prestige and flattering recognition.
Then the chapter concludes that ego keeps steering people toward status identities because status feels faster and more visible than the harder path of useful work.

Required Anchors:
- John Boyd as the central example of choosing contribution over rank and social reward; the friction is status was available, the consequence is he kept his work and standards primary.
- The general pattern of ambitious people choosing appearance, promotion, or image because those signals are easier to display; the friction is the status path is socially reinforced, the consequence is real contribution gets diluted.
- The explicit be-or-do question itself as the chapter's governing mechanism; the friction is both seem compatible at first, the consequence is the chapter insists they diverge under pressure.

Frameworks or Terms Introduced:
- be-or-do fork: the choice between status identity and useful contribution
- status bait: prestige, recognition, or flattering self-concepts that weaken the work
- contribution discipline: preferring the task, the standard, and the result over recognition

Specific Applications:
- choosing the unglamorous assignment that improves ability over the visible one that improves image
- declining praise-heavy roles when they would pull energy away from the work
- evaluating ambition by output, standards, and consequences rather than by title

Common Misreadings:
- the chapter says titles and promotions never matter
- Boyd is used as a purity legend rather than as a directional example
- choosing to do means becoming passive, invisible, or indifferent to influence

Counterarguments or Limits:
- the frozen bundle does not support a total rejection of leadership, visibility, or recognition
- the chapter should avoid pretending status and contribution are always perfectly separable
- keep the tension on what happens when they diverge, not on a naive fantasy that institutions are easy to ignore
