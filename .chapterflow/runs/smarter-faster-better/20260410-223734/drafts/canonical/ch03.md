# Chapter 3: Focus

Pressure changes attention before most people notice it. The frozen chapter frame uses Air France Flight 447 to make that visible. Duhigg is not treating focus as a soft preference or a generic productivity tip. He is showing that attention can narrow so aggressively under stress that the mind starts protecting one signal while dropping the wider picture that would make the signal intelligible. A person can feel intensely focused and still be seeing less of reality.

That is why the subtitle-level term cognitive tunneling matters so much. Tunneling is not distraction. It is a more dangerous distortion because it often feels like discipline from the inside. Someone locks onto the loudest alarm, the most recent detail, or the most frightening interpretation and begins treating that narrow slice as the whole situation. The problem is not lack of effort. The problem is that effort has collapsed into fixation.

The chapter therefore forces a distinction between strong attention and useful attention. Strong attention can be intense, fast, and confident while still failing to scan for what else matters. Useful attention keeps asking what might be outside the beam. In a crisis, that difference becomes expensive. The mind wants to reduce complexity quickly. But complexity reduced too early can become blindness wearing the costume of clarity.

The corrective idea is mental models. In this chapter, mental models are not abstract theory for its own sake. They are internal frames that help a person interpret what they are seeing and keep scanning when stress keeps trying to close the frame. A good model gives attention somewhere to move besides the loudest cue. It creates structure without letting the structure turn into panic or guesswork.

That matters well beyond aviation. A team can fixate on one red metric and stop noticing the other conditions that explain it. A student can obsess over one sentence in a prompt and misread the assignment around it. A parent in a tense conversation can hear one phrase, decide what it means, and ignore everything else happening in the room. In each case the person is not exactly unfocused. They are focused too narrowly to understand the situation they are inside.

The chapter's harder edge is that pressure does not automatically produce sharper perception. Sometimes it does the opposite. It compresses attention until scanning feels wasteful and uncertainty feels intolerable. That is why mental models matter. They do not remove pressure. They give the mind a way to organize pressure without surrendering to the first explanation that arrives.

So the chapter turns focus into a design problem. The question is not just whether you can concentrate. The question is whether your attention can stay wide enough, long enough, to keep reality from shrinking into one mistaken picture. The bridge into goal setting follows naturally. Once attention is more accurate, the next problem is where that attention should be aimed, and how goals can guide action without becoming the next source of tunnel vision.
