# Chapter 8: Absorbing Data

Absorbing data gets harder when information keeps arriving faster than meaning. Duhigg frames this chapter around turning information into knowledge in Cincinnati's public schools. That subtitle-level combination points to a narrow claim: raw inputs help only when people can organize them into a shared picture that guides action instead of drowning attention.

Information matters because scattered facts can still be useless. A dashboard can be full, a classroom wall can be covered, and a team can still fail to know what is important. The chapter treats overload as a judgment problem, not just a storage problem.

Knowledge is what appears when information is interpreted. A pattern becomes usable when people can explain what it means, why it matters, and what should happen next. That shift from raw input to structured understanding is the chapter's center.

Cincinnati's public schools give the chapter an institutional anchor. The point is not district detail. The point is that large systems need ways to turn many signals into a shared understanding that teachers, leaders, or teams can actually use. More data is not progress if nobody can translate it into a better next move.

The limit matters. Absorbing data is not memorizing everything, and more information does not guarantee better judgment. Without interpretation, discussion, and selection, added input can make people less clear rather than more informed.

That closing move consolidates the book's wider logic. Motivation, teams, focus, goals, management, decisions, innovation, and data absorption all depend on turning complexity into usable action rather than mistaking noise for progress.
