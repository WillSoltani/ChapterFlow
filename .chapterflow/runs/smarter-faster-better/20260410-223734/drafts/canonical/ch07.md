# Chapter 7: Innovation

Innovation often looks mysterious only when people ignore how ideas move. Duhigg frames this chapter around idea brokers, creative desperation, and Disney's Frozen. That subtitle-level combination points to a narrow claim: new solutions often emerge when people carry patterns across boundaries and keep revising under pressure instead of waiting for one isolated flash of originality.

Idea brokers matter because useful ideas do not always begin where they are finally used. A person who notices a pattern in one setting and brings it into another can widen what a team thinks is possible. Innovation, in that sense, is often less about inventing from nothing and more about recombining what different people already know.

Creative desperation adds the chapter's pressure. Deadlines and weak material can force a room to stop protecting early drafts and start changing them. Pressure alone does not create insight, but it can strip away vanity and make revision unavoidable when the work is still not good enough.

Frozen gives the chapter a visible anchor for that process. The point is not movie trivia. The point is that innovation can require many rounds of borrowing, reshaping, and hard editing before the final version becomes coherent. The chapter treats creativity as a process of transfer plus revision rather than as solo magic.

The limit matters. Borrowing is not the same as copying without thought, and pressure is not a miracle cure. Innovation improves when people can still compare ideas, move fragments across contexts, and revise honestly while the clock is loud.

That sets up the final chapter naturally. Once a room is generating more possibilities, it still needs a way to absorb information and decide what those possibilities actually mean.
