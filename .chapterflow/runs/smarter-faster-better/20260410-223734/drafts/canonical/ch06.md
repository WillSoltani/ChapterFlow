# Chapter 6: Decision Making

Decision making gets harder when the future stays partly hidden. Duhigg frames this chapter around forecasting the future, poker, and Bayesian psychology. That subtitle-level combination points to a narrow problem: people still have to act when certainty is unavailable, so judgment has to work with odds, incomplete evidence, and revisable beliefs rather than with perfect proof.

Forecasting matters because good decisions do not begin with total confidence. They begin with a best estimate that stays open to revision. A forecast is useful not because it predicts everything exactly, but because it gives people a working picture of what is likely, what is uncertain, and what would force an update. The chapter pushes against the habit of treating commitment as a refusal to change your mind.

Poker sharpens that lesson. Hidden information means a player can never see the whole field at once. The useful question is not whether the next move is guaranteed. It is whether the move makes sense given the odds now and whether new evidence should change the estimate later. That makes poker a practical model for decision making under uncertainty rather than a metaphor for bluffing.

Bayesian psychology gives the chapter its conceptual center. People make better choices when they update beliefs as evidence changes. Revision is not weakness in this frame. Revision is the skill that keeps judgment connected to reality. Confidence can help action, but confidence becomes dangerous when it starts pretending accuracy.

The limit matters too. Probabilistic judgment is not the same as indecision, and Bayesian language should not turn into math fog. The point is simpler and harder: act on the best available estimate, keep track of uncertainty, and change course when the evidence changes enough to justify it.

That is what carries the book forward. Once people can decide with probabilities instead of certainty theater, the next question is how new ideas travel and combine under pressure. Better judgment sets up the conditions for smarter innovation because people can test possibilities without worshiping their first guess.
