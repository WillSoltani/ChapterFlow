# Chapter 6: Decision Making

Decision making gets harder when the future stays partly hidden. Duhigg frames this chapter around forecasting the future, poker, and Bayesian psychology. That combination points to a narrow problem: people still have to act without certainty, so judgment has to work with odds, incomplete evidence, and revisable beliefs instead of perfect proof.

Forecasting matters because good decisions begin with an estimate that can still change. A forecast is useful when it marks what seems likely, what remains uncertain, and what kind of evidence would force an update. The chapter pushes against the habit of confusing commitment with refusing to change your mind.

Poker sharpens the lesson. Hidden information means no one sees the whole field at once. The useful question is not whether a move is guaranteed. It is whether the move makes sense given the odds now and whether new evidence should change the estimate later.

Bayesian psychology gives the chapter its center. People decide better when they update beliefs as evidence changes. Revision is not weakness here. It is the skill that keeps judgment connected to reality. Confidence can help action, but confidence becomes dangerous when it starts pretending accuracy.

The limit is important. Probabilistic judgment is not indecision, and Bayesian language should not become math fog. Act on the best available estimate, track uncertainty, and revise when the evidence shifts.

The bridge forward is clear. Once people can decide with probabilities instead of certainty theater, the next question is how new ideas combine into innovation under pressure.
