Robert Philippe had spent half a century behaving like a man who could always make the next move. He built a small gas station into an auto-parts empire in Louisiana, pulled businessmen into rough bars to close deals, and treated work as the ordinary rhythm of being alive. Then he came home from South America and seemed to go dim. He stopped reaching for the office. He stared at the television. He let whole days pass without moving toward anything that had once defined him.

Duhigg opens here because the loss is so hard to explain away. Robert is not presented as a man who never had drive. He is presented as a man whose life was organized around it. After the trip, he can still talk about his business, remember his past, and describe what needs doing. What has gone missing is not knowledge. What has gone missing is launch.

At the clinic in New Orleans, Dr. Richard Strub cannot find the obvious answer. Robert's vital signs are normal. His intelligence testing does not show collapse. He can understand a newspaper, interpret a story, and explain how his company works. He does not sound like a man sinking into ordinary sadness. He tells Strub, "I don't seem to have as much get-up-and-go as I used to." The line is simple, but it carries the chapter's first real distinction: wanting, knowing, and acting do not always move together.

The MRI gives the puzzle a shape. Deep in Robert's brain, Strub sees evidence of a small injury in the striatum. The chapter does not need a fake-clinical speech about brain chemistry. Its point is tighter than that. A person can retain memory, reasoning, and outward competence while losing part of the mechanism that turns intention into motion. Later medical cases in the excerpt keep pressing the same point. The failure is not ignorance. It is a break between recognition and initiation.

That is what makes Duhigg's version of motivation more useful than pep talk. In ordinary speech, motivation sounds like enthusiasm, grit, or emotional brightness. This chapter starts from a harsher possibility. Action can stall even when understanding stays intact. Motivation is bound up with whether the next move feels available. Robert knows his employees are worried. He knows his business needs him. None of that becomes forward motion. Information is present. Agency is weak.

From there the chapter widens from pathology to ordinary life. The subtitle points toward boot camp, nursing home rebellions, and the locus of control. The official framing adds a crucial clue: a Marine Corps general reimagines boot camp and finds that a bias toward action can help directionless recruits become self-motivating. Even without adding unfrozen detail, the contrast is clear. Motivation grows when people experience themselves as agents inside a situation, not as cargo being dragged through it.

That sounds smaller than most productivity advice, but it is more exact. Weak advice treats motivation like a speech problem. Say the right words to yourself, demand more discipline, raise the stakes, and action should follow. Duhigg pushes somewhere else. If motion depends partly on control, the useful question is not only, "How do I care more?" It is, "Where is the point of choice?" A recruit taught to decide, a resident given a meaningful say, or a worker who can claim one next move is standing in a different motivational environment than someone being managed harder from above.

That also explains why control can backfire. People often assume stalled action calls for tighter supervision. But a system that strips away ownership can make motion less likely, not more. You can tell someone exactly what matters and still leave them passive if every meaningful choice has already been made for them. Knowledge without agency stays thin. Pressure without ownership often turns into compliance on the surface and inertia underneath.

The chapter should also be read with one limit in view. Agency is not the same thing as unlimited freedom. A bias toward action is not chaos, and locus of control is not a magic trick. People still need constraints, training, and reality. The sharper claim is that motivation becomes sturdier when someone can see how their decision changes what happens next. The next step must feel choosable, not merely admirable.

That makes Robert Philippe's case more than a memorable opening. It gives the book a clean diagnostic tool. A person can have facts, obligations, memory, and even a strong identity on their side and still fail to move. The missing ingredient is often not meaning. It is agency. Once that becomes visible, motivation stops looking like a mysterious inner flame and starts looking like a relationship between choice and action.

The opening chapter does not pretend this solves everything. It does something better. It gives you a better place to look when motion disappears. Instead of asking only whether someone cares enough, ask whether they can feel a hand on the next decision. If they cannot, more information may change very little. If they can, even a hard situation begins to change shape. The next pressure point is what happens when that ability to act is no longer personal but shared across a group.
