# Chapter 4: Goal Setting

Goals matter because attention needs somewhere to go. After the focus chapter shows how pressure can narrow perception, Duhigg turns to the question of aim. The chapter frame uses SMART goals, stretch goals, and the Yom Kippur War to keep that issue concrete. A goal is not just a wish with cleaner language. It is a structure that directs attention, effort, and judgment.

SMART goals represent the chapter's concrete side. Vague ambition can feel energizing for a moment and still leave people with no usable next move. A sharper goal reduces that fog. It gives effort enough definition to guide action instead of merely describing hope.

Stretch goals add the second half of the argument. A target can be so modest that it protects the current baseline instead of challenging it. Stretch matters because ambition changes behavior too. Bigger aims can call out more effort, more invention, and more reach than a safe plan usually asks for.

The tension is that both strengths can become distortions. Specificity can collapse into narrow measurement. Stretch can collapse into empty escalation. Then the goal stops guiding action and starts hijacking it. People begin serving the target instead of using the target to serve the larger mission.

The Yom Kippur War anchor keeps the chapter from sliding into office cliché. Plans and targets shape what people prepare for, what they assume matters, and what they miss. A tidy goal can still push effort in the wrong direction if the goal is built on a bad frame.

Goal setting becomes a design problem. A good goal is concrete enough to guide behavior and ambitious enough to widen effort, while still flexible enough to remain connected to reality. The next chapter naturally turns to leadership, because once goals exist, someone has to help other people pursue them without crushing trust or judgment.
