# Chapter 3: Focus

Pressure changes attention before most people notice it. The chapter uses Air France Flight 447 to make that visible. Focus is not presented here as a soft preference or a generic productivity tip. It is an operational question about what the mind notices when stress starts shrinking the frame. A person can feel intensely focused and still be seeing less of reality.

That is why cognitive tunneling matters. Tunneling is not distraction. It is more dangerous because it feels disciplined from the inside. Someone locks onto the loudest alarm, the most recent detail, or the most frightening interpretation and starts treating that slice as the whole situation. The failure is not weak effort. The failure is effort collapsing into fixation.

The chapter therefore separates strong attention from useful attention. Strong attention can be fast, intense, and confident while still missing what else matters. Useful attention keeps scanning. In a crisis that difference becomes costly. The mind wants to simplify quickly, but simplification that arrives too early can become blindness disguised as clarity.

The corrective idea is mental models. In this chapter, mental models are not decorative theory. They are internal frames that help a person interpret signals and keep searching when pressure keeps trying to close the field. A good model gives attention somewhere to move besides the loudest cue. It creates structure without turning structure into panic.

That logic reaches beyond the headline case. A team can fixate on one red metric and stop noticing the surrounding conditions that explain it. A student can obsess over one sentence in an assignment and miss the larger task. A tense conversation can narrow around one phrase while everything else in the room gets ignored. None of those people are unfocused in the ordinary sense. They are too narrowly focused to understand what is happening.

The chapter's harder edge is that pressure does not automatically sharpen perception. Sometimes it compresses attention until scanning feels inefficient and uncertainty feels unbearable. Mental models matter because they help the mind organize pressure without surrendering to the first explanation that feels available.

Focus becomes a design problem. The question is not only whether you can concentrate. It is whether your attention can stay wide enough, long enough, to keep reality from shrinking into one mistaken picture. The next chapter naturally asks where that attention should be aimed and how goals can guide action without becoming the next source of distortion.
