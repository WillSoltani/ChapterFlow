`You'll Change` makes a quiet but destabilizing claim: the person you are planning for in the future may not want exactly what the current you is trying so confidently to lock in.

Housel is not arguing against planning itself. He is arguing against overconfidence about identity. People often assume their present goals, tastes, and priorities will stay mostly intact, so they make long commitments as if the future self were already fully known. The chapter says that assumption is much weaker than it feels.

That is the risk of identity drift. The future self may inherit obligations, career paths, lifestyles, or tradeoffs that once felt meaningful but no longer fit. In that case, the problem is not only that the world changed. It is that the person carrying the plan changed too.

The practical lesson is to leave some room for revision. Not every decision has to be maximally reversible, but plans can often be built with more flexibility, less humiliation around changing course, and less dependence on one rigid forecast of who you will become.

The chapter also protects itself from an easy misuse. Flexibility is not drift, and changing your mind is not automatically wisdom. Some commitments are worth honoring despite changing preferences. The point is not to celebrate inconsistency. It is to avoid needless rigidity where false certainty about the future self creates avoidable pain.

That is what makes the chapter humane. It treats revision as a skill rather than a failure when the original plan depended too heavily on a version of the self that no longer exists. Good planning leaves room for that possibility without letting every impulse take over.

That naturally leads into `Nothing's Free`. A plan may be flexible enough to fit a changing person and still demand that person pay the emotional fee attached to uncertainty and return.
