`Room for Error` makes a practical argument: margin is not wasted space in a financial plan. It is part of what keeps the plan alive.

Housel's reasoning starts with fragility. When assumptions are tight, a normal mistake, a small delay, or an ordinary swing can trigger outsized pain. The problem is not always the size of the shock. The problem is often that the plan had no room to absorb it before bad options became forced options.

That is why the chapter treats margin as anti-ruin design. Cash, lower leverage, conservative timelines, and less aggressive assumptions all act as shock absorbers. They do not guarantee success, but they reduce the chance that one error or one rough stretch turns into irreversible damage.

This can feel inefficient during calm periods. Slack often looks like unused capacity when nothing dramatic is happening. That is exactly why people underinvest in it. They judge the buffer by what it appears to cost in quiet times rather than by what it prevents in hard times.

The chapter is not asking for maximum caution in every situation. Too much margin can become stagnation or underuse. But the book's concern is that people more often underestimate how easily tight plans can be broken by uncertainty and by their own imperfect judgment.

The practical lesson is to build room before you need it. Leave space in budgets, return expectations, leverage, and timelines while flexibility is still cheap. Once pressure arrives, it is much harder to create breathing room without paying for it through bad timing or forced choices.

That naturally leads into `You'll Change`. A plan may survive a shock from the world and still face another source of uncertainty: the person making the plan will not remain exactly the same.
