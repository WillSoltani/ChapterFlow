`Surprise!` makes a humbling claim: history is shaped by unexpected events often enough that planning as if surprise were rare is its own forecasting error.

Housel is not saying nothing can be known. He is pointing to a pattern in how people think about the future. They overweight what is recent, visible, and emotionally fresh, then treat that short window as if it captures the real range of what can happen next. Quiet periods start to feel like proof of stability. Recent shocks start to feel like the only shocks worth fearing.

That is the mechanism the chapter wants the reader to notice. Recency bias does not only distort opinions. It quietly narrows forecasts. People build plans and narratives that assume tomorrow will mostly stay inside the range they have just experienced, even though history keeps being redirected by events that were barely present in ordinary expectations.

The practical lesson is not to stop planning. It is to plan with more humility and wider ranges. A good plan admits that important events may arrive from outside the recent script. Instead of treating the absence of recent shocks as reassurance, it remembers that unobserved risk is still risk.

That changes how planning works. Forecasts become less about precision theater and more about range management, exposure control, and resilience. The right question is often not only what is most likely, but what happens if the forecast is wrong in a meaningful way.

The chapter also keeps itself from sliding into fatalism. Respecting surprise does not mean all probabilities are equal or all preparation is useless. It means confidence should be softer, stories should be looser, and plans should leave room to absorb what they failed to see.

That naturally leads into `Room for Error`. Once surprise is treated as normal rather than exotic, margin stops looking wasteful and starts looking like design.
