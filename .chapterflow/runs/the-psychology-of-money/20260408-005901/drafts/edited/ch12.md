`Reasonable > Rational` makes a practical claim: the best money strategy is often the one you can keep living with, not the one that wins a spreadsheet beauty contest.

Housel's distinction is between theoretical efficiency and behavioral fit. A plan can be technically superior and still fail if the person using it cannot sleep through the volatility, stick with the drawdowns, or tolerate the uncertainty required to hold it. In that case, the elegant plan is not actually superior in practice.

That is why the chapter brings temperament into the center of financial judgment. Risk tolerance, patience, attention, and emotional stability are not side issues. They shape whether a person can stay with a strategy long enough for its logic to matter.

The practical lesson is to favor durability over brilliance. A good-enough plan that survives stress can outperform a perfect-looking plan that gets abandoned under pressure. Consistency changes results because compounding only helps what stays in motion.

The chapter also draws a limit around its own message. `Reasonable` does not mean making excuses, hiding from math, or calling every comforting habit wise. A reasonable plan still has to respect reality, tradeoffs, and honest self-knowledge. Otherwise it becomes fiction with a soothing tone.

That balance is what gives the chapter force. Rational analysis remains necessary, but it is incomplete if it ignores the human being who has to carry the plan. The aim is not to choose feelings over arithmetic. It is to choose a strategy where arithmetic and human behavior can hold together.

That naturally leads into `Surprise!`. If even durable plans are designed for imperfect people, they also need to be designed for a world that keeps producing outcomes no model fully predicts.
