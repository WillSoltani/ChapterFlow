# Chapter 22 Canonical Draft

The postscript widens the book's lens. Housel argues that many American consumer attitudes are not timeless human defaults. They were shaped by a particular historical period in which growth, rising incomes, expanding access, and improving convenience felt normal. That environment changed what people came to expect from money and from life.

The chapter points to the unusual force of the postwar experience. A generation that lived through depression and war then encountered a world of rising opportunity, mass production, suburban expansion, household technology, easier access to goods, and a broad sense that tomorrow should be more comfortable than today. That pattern trained expectations upward.

This matters because expectations do not feel historical from the inside. They feel normal. A person raised inside rising abundance can come to see bigger homes, more consumption, easier credit, and constant improvement as the baseline rather than as one product of one era. That helps explain why spending, debt, and aspiration can feel emotionally reasonable even when they create strain.

The postscript therefore adds context to the whole book. Money psychology is not only about individual temperament. It is also about the environment that taught people what to desire, what to expect, and what counts as ordinary. History becomes part of behavior.

That is a useful closing move. The book ends by reminding the reader that financial behavior is shaped both by the mind and by the world that trained the mind. Understanding that broader setting makes the earlier chapters feel even more human and more historically grounded.
