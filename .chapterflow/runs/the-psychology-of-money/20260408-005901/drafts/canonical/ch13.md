`Surprise!` argues that people make a major forecasting mistake when they plan as if surprise were rare.

Housel's point is not that the future is pure chaos. It is that history is repeatedly shaped by events that arrive from outside what people were recently treating as normal. After a calm stretch, people quietly assume the future will stay calm. After a certain kind of shock, they assume that kind of shock is the main thing to fear. In both cases, recent experience narrows the imagination.

That narrowing is what gives recency bias its power. People treat the recent visible range as if it were the true range of possibilities. Then they build forecasts, budgets, and stories that assume tomorrow will mostly resemble what they already know.

The chapter's lesson is not to give up on planning. It is to plan with humility. A good plan does not pretend to know every coming event. It leaves room for events that are hard to name in advance and remembers that absence of recent shocks is not proof that shocks are unlikely.

That changes the job of a forecast. Instead of acting like a certainty machine, planning becomes a way to manage ranges, exposure, and resilience. The question shifts from what exactly will happen to how much damage being wrong would cause.

The chapter also keeps a limit in view. Respecting surprise does not mean probabilities stop mattering or obvious fragilities can be ignored. It means certainty should soften and ranges should widen.

That leads directly to the next chapter. If surprise is common enough to humble the forecast, then good design has to include room for error on purpose.
