`Reasonable > Rational` argues that the best money plan is often the one you can actually follow without being broken by it.

Housel draws a line between a plan that looks ideal in theory and a plan a human being can live with over time. In finance, a strategy can be mathematically impressive and still fail if fear, impatience, or exhaustion make the person abandon it at the worst moment.

That is why the chapter treats temperament as part of the plan instead of a distraction from it. Sleep, attention, risk tolerance, and the ability to hold steady under pressure all matter because money decisions are made by people, not by detached equations.

The chapter's practical rule is to prefer durable commitments over brittle optimization. A slightly less efficient plan that you can sustain may beat a brilliant plan that collapses the first time volatility rises or life gets noisy.

But the chapter is careful not to turn `reasonable` into a loophole. A plan is not reasonable just because it feels comforting. It still has to face arithmetic, real tradeoffs, and honest self-knowledge. Otherwise it becomes an excuse for avoidance.

This keeps the chapter balanced. Rational analysis matters, but it must be filtered through what a person can truly stick with. The goal is not to defeat math. The goal is to build a plan where math and human behavior can survive together.

That sets up the next chapter. If even good plans must be built for real humans rather than perfect models, then they must also be built for a future that will keep surprising them.
