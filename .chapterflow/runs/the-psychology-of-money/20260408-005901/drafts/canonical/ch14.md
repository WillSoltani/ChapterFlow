`Room for Error` argues that margin is not wasted space in a money plan. It is the price of surviving uncertainty.

Housel's point is that people often build plans as if ordinary volatility, small mistakes, or timing problems will stay manageable. But when assumptions are tight, even a modest error can force terrible choices at the worst possible moment. That is why slack matters.

The chapter treats room for error as anti-ruin design. Cash reserves, lower leverage, conservative assumptions, and flexible timelines all create space for a plan to absorb friction without collapsing. They do not remove uncertainty. They keep uncertainty from becoming fatal.

This is why the chapter resists calling margin waste. During calm periods, slack can feel inefficient because nothing dramatic seems to be happening. But that feeling is exactly what tempts people to trim buffers right before they need them most.

The chapter also keeps a limit in view. Margin is not an instruction to become timid or to eliminate all ambition. Too much slack can become stagnation. But too little slack can destroy compounding before it has time to work.

The practical lesson is to build room into leverage, budgets, timelines, and expectations before pressure arrives. Flexibility is cheapest before it is needed and hardest to create after a mistake has already narrowed the options.

That leads into the next chapter. Even robust plans are made by people who will not stay exactly the same over time, which means survival depends on adapting to change in the world and in yourself.
