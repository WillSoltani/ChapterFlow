Chapter Title: No One's Crazy
Position In Book: 2 of 22

Core Claim:
What looks irrational in money often makes perfect sense once you understand the person's lived experience. Financial beliefs are formed by tiny slices of history seen up close, so disagreement about risk, saving, debt, and investing is often biography before it is logic.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
This chapter shifts the book from the general claim that money is behavioral to the specific reason behavior diverges. It frames money judgment through personal history, generational experience, and the recency of modern financial tools, then uses that frame to replace contempt with humility.

Author Logic Chain:
First the author argues that each person's direct money experience is a tiny fraction of history but a huge share of what that person thinks is normal.
Then the author shows that inflation, recessions, bull markets, and labor-market conditions shape what risk feels like.
Then the author explains that many modern investing norms are historically young, which means people are often improvising rather than inheriting timeless money wisdom.
Then the author concludes that other people's money behavior usually becomes more legible once their history is visible.
Then the author bridges to luck and risk by showing that outcomes are shaped by forces outside any one person's control.

Required Anchors:

- different generations seeing inflation, jobs, and markets differently
- the recency of common retirement and investment structures
- decisions that make sense from the inside even when they look odd from the outside

Specific Applications:
- ask what history shaped a person's money decision before judging it
- build a plan that fits your own risk tolerance rather than someone else's story
- notice when you are copying advice from people playing a different money game

Common Misreadings:
- the chapter says every money decision is equally good
- the chapter says evidence no longer matters
- the chapter excuses permanent avoidance rather than explaining where it comes from

Counterarguments or Limits:
Experience can explain a decision without making it optimal. The chapter asks for understanding first, then better judgment, not endless relativism.
