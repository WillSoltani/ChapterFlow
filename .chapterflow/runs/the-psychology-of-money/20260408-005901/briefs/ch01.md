Core Claim:
Money success is a behavioral skill before it is a technical skill. The opening chapter argues that intelligence, credentials, and formulas matter less than the ability to manage fear, ego, envy, patience, and incentives over long stretches of ordinary life.

What Makes This Chapter Distinct:
The chapter is the book's frame setter. It contrasts quiet long-run discipline with elite financial sophistication that breaks under pressure, then broadens into the claim that money happens in kitchens, careers, marriages, and status games, not just in spreadsheets.

Author Logic Chain:
First the author opens with two men from the same town who end up with radically different financial outcomes.
Then the author shows that the difference is not raw intelligence or access to formulas, but behavior under real-life emotional pressure.
Then the author generalizes the lesson: money decisions are social and psychological, so stories teach the subject better than pure equations.
Then the author adds the limit: math still matters, but only if behavior allows the math to stay in place long enough to work.
Then the author bridges to the next chapter by asking why so many reasonable people see money so differently.

Common Misreadings:

- The chapter romanticizes ignorance
- The chapter treats wealthy outcomes as pure character rather than a mix of behavior and circumstance

Counterarguments or Limits:
The chapter does not erase the role of taxes, fees, asset allocation, or time horizon. It says those technical factors only help if behavior keeps the plan alive.
