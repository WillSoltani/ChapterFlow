# Naval’s Recommended Reading

The appendix opens with a line that almost sounds anti-bookish: Naval says he does not read for self-improvement. He reads out of curiosity and interest. That matters because it immediately breaks the dutiful, performative frame many people bring to reading. The best book, he says, is the one you will devour. Not the one that looks smartest on the desk. Not the one that makes you feel responsible for buying it. The one that captures enough of your actual attention to be metabolized instead of merely possessed. Curiosity is not being treated as indulgence here. It is being treated as the engine that makes deep reading possible.

That engine changes taste over time. Naval says that if you read enough, you become a connoisseur, and once that happens you naturally drift toward theory, concepts, and nonfiction. The chapter does not mean that fiction becomes useless or that every serious reader ends in the same place. It means appetite matures. As taste sharpens, you start wanting denser signal, larger frameworks, and the kinds of books that keep changing how you interpret other things. That is why so many of his nonfiction picks are recommended for mental models, history, physics, or game theory rather than for easy self-improvement. A reading list is not a personality badge here. It is an input stream.

The category spread shows what that input stream is trying to feed. In nonfiction, Naval gravitates toward books that compress reality into stronger models: history, evolution, risk, cooperation, physics, and macro-level explanations. In philosophy and spirituality, he looks for uncompromising self-observation, old wisdom, meditative practice, and texts that return him to truth rather than performance. In science fiction, he values writers who smuggle philosophy and thermodynamics through story rather than through lectures. The point is not that every category does the same job. The point is that judgment gets sharper when different kinds of books stretch different parts of the mind.

Then the appendix widens the medium list even further. Blogs, essays, Twitter threads, graphic novels, television, and stand-alone pieces all count if the signal is strong enough. A Scott Adams blog post becomes Naval's background template for writing. A Hamming essay on great work matters far beyond scientific research. Stratechery, Farnam Street, Kevin Simler, and other blog or account recommendations show that live analysis can matter as much as books. Even Rick and Morty and graphic novels get included because medium is clearly not the deciding standard. Signal, surprise, and conceptual yield are. The appendix keeps saying, without quite using the phrase, that genius is unevenly distributed and good inputs show up in strange containers.

That is why Naval includes rereading, gifting, reference use, and even embarrassment in the same reading philosophy. He gives certain books to his kids. He rereads science, philosophy, and selected spiritual texts. He keeps a writing post open while he works. He says he skips naming some of what he reads because other people would find it trivial, silly, junky, or even reprehensible. That line matters. A status-driven reader curates appearances. Naval is describing something closer to nutritional honesty. If a text bends the mind, clarifies a problem, or sharpens taste, it belongs in the diet regardless of how respectable it sounds in conversation. That is also why he says books are an investment, not an expense. The return is upstream.

Read that way, this final chapter stops being a miscellaneous appendix and becomes the book's source map. Chapter 2 explained why reading strengthens judgment. Chapter 5 showed a worldview built from tested ideas rather than borrowed certainty. Chapter 6 shows where the raw material often comes from: appetite strong enough to devour, taste strong enough to discriminate, and humility strong enough to learn from whatever medium actually carries signal. The point is not to copy the list title for title. The point is to copy the reading posture. Read widely enough to become choosy, deeply enough to be changed, and honestly enough that you will follow signal even when it arrives in an unfashionable package.
