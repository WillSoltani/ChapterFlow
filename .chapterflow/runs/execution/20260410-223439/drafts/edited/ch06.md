A team can speak confidently about talent until one key role wobbles and reveals how thin the bench really is. The name of a successor exists, but nobody trusts the name. The development language sounds healthy, but nobody can say what business need the development is serving. A weak performer is still being discussed as a future question instead of a present cost. That is the pressure Chapter 6 takes on. Bossidy and Charan argue that execution needs a real people process, not a ceremonial talent conversation. The process matters because people decisions have to be linked to strategy and operations instead of floating beside them.

That linkage is the chapter's first hard demand. The people process does not exist to produce abstract profiles or store opinions about potential. It exists to make sure the organization has the talent depth, role clarity, and decision quality required to carry the business. That is why the frozen bundle keeps linking people to strategy and operations. A process that cannot answer what the business needs, who can do it now, who might do it next, and where the risks are remains a filing system with better language.

The chapter then moves into the leadership pipeline. Pipeline talk can sound harmless until you ask whether it would survive a sudden departure or a stretch of growth. Bossidy and Charan want a process that evaluates people accurately and in depth, not one that flatters future possibility while avoiding present weakness. Leadership assessment, continuous improvement, and pipeline depth all matter because execution gets weaker when the company keeps discovering too late that its next layer is thin, vague, or untested.

Succession depth and retention risk sharpen that point. It is not enough to name one possible replacement and call the problem solved. A serious people process asks how many credible options exist behind key roles and how fragile the system becomes if one person leaves. That question is uncomfortable because it exposes whether the organization has really been developing talent or simply hoping stability continues. In that sense, succession depth is not a ceremonial planning exercise. It is a realism test on the future capacity of the business.

The chapter also refuses to let the people process become optimistic paperwork. Dealing with nonperformers has to be inside the process, not outside it as an awkward exception. Linking HR to business results means the process has to influence actual performance, actual deployment, and actual correction. If weak performers remain endlessly described but never seriously addressed, then the process is documenting drift instead of changing it. Bossidy and Charan are clear that talent systems become dishonest when they can celebrate development while refusing to face underperformance.

That is why candid dialogue becomes the live ammo of the chapter. A people process stays alive only when the talent conversation is truthful enough to affect decisions. Leaders have to speak clearly about strengths, limits, bench depth, risk, and nonperformance without converting the review into politeness theater. But the point is not harshness. The point is to keep the process connected to reality strongly enough that it changes who is developed, who is moved, who is trusted, and what the business can actually execute. That is the bridge to Chapter 7. Once the people process is real, strategy can no longer pretend it is being built in a vacuum away from talent depth, succession strength, and the truth about who can carry the plan.
