A strategy can sound persuasive right up to the moment someone asks how it is supposed to work. The growth target is there. The market language is there. The confidence is there. But the route between intention and result is thin, missing, or hidden behind broad slogans. Bossidy and Charan use Chapter 7 to press on that weakness. Strategy is not an isolated planning document in their frame. It is a process linked to people and operations, and it becomes serious only when it can explain the hows instead of only celebrating the desired end state.

That pressure on the hows is the chapter's first demand. A strategy that announces direction without showing how the business will move, compete, and execute is not giving the organization a working plan. It is giving the organization a larger hope. That is why the frozen support keeps linking strategy back to people and operations. The strategy process has to stay attached to the people who must carry it and the operating reality that must sustain it. If those links disappear, then the plan becomes easiest to admire precisely when it is hardest to execute.

The chapter then shifts into the building blocks of a strategy and the construction of the strategic plan itself. That move matters because strategy is not being treated as inspiration floating above the business. It is being treated as a built object that has to answer practical questions. Who builds the plan matters because ownership affects realism. If the people responsible for carrying the strategy are distant from building it, the finished document can become cleaner than the truth it is supposed to organize. Bossidy and Charan push toward a strategy process specific enough to be carried by real people rather than applauded in abstraction.

That is why the chapter's question set matters so much. The plan has to answer what is happening in the external environment, what existing customers and markets are saying, who the competition is, and what the best path to profitable growth actually looks like. None of those questions are decorative. They force the strategy to stop pretending it lives alone inside the company. A plan that ignores customer reality or competitive pressure may still sound bold, but it is building confidence on missing information. The chapter forces the business to face whether the chosen direction has contact with the field where it will actually win or lose.

The questions then turn inward without getting gentler. Can the business execute the strategy with the people and capability it has or can build? What important milestones would show the plan is moving instead of merely being praised? How should short-term and long-term balance be handled so that urgency does not kill the future and distant aspiration does not excuse present vagueness? Those questions make the strategy process harder because they expose whether the organization has confused desire with capacity. A strong strategy is not only a claim about opportunity. It is also a judgment about capability, sequence, and proof.

The chapter closes by tightening around critical issues and sustainable profit logic. Strategy cannot be treated as finished when the headline sounds attractive. It has to keep answering what could block the business, what economics make the plan durable, and whether the business can make money on a sustainable basis rather than through temporary momentum or selective optimism. That is the chapter's realism test. A strategy is only as serious as its answers to the how, the field, the capability, the milestones, and the economics. That is the bridge to Chapter 8. Once strategy has to answer those questions, the next problem is how leaders review it without letting the review become presentation theater.
