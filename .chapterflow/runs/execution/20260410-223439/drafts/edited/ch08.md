A strategy review can look disciplined while doing almost nothing to test the strategy itself. The slide order is clean. The language is sharp. The room is attentive. Then one real question lands, and it becomes obvious that everyone has been describing the plan more than pressuring it. Bossidy and Charan use Chapter 8 to attack that failure. A strategy review is not a ceremonial presentation in their frame. It is a live management practice whose job is to test whether the strategy can survive contact with reality.

That is why the chapter starts with the questions leaders have to raise. The review matters because it forces the plan into a conversation strong enough to expose weak knowledge, soft assumptions, and blurred accountability. If the meeting is mostly a stage for explaining the plan again, then the strategy is being protected rather than examined. The chapter's standard is harsher and more useful. The review has to make the plan answer back under pressure, not simply receive polite attention.

The first pressure point is competition and capability. How well does the business unit team actually know the competition, and how strong is the organization's capability to execute the strategy it is defending? Those questions belong together because a strategy can fail from either side. The company may misunderstand the field, or it may understand the field and still lack the operating strength to respond. The chapter does not let leaders hide inside market language or inside internal optimism. It wants both external realism and internal realism in the same room at the same time.

The next pressure point is focus. Is the plan scattered or sharply focused, and are the leaders choosing the right ideas? That pair matters because a strategy can sound energetic while still being spread across too many priorities, too many themes, or too many attractive possibilities. Focus is not a style preference here. It is a test of seriousness. Choosing the right ideas also means refusing to confuse interesting ideas with executable ideas. A review that cannot narrow and prioritize is still serving the plan's self-image more than the business.

The chapter then forces the review to make the linkages with people and operations clear. That move protects against one of the easiest forms of strategy theater: talking as if the strategy exists in one discussion while staffing, capability, sequencing, and operational consequence get handled somewhere else later. Bossidy and Charan keep pressing the opposite standard. The review should expose whether the people and operations needed to carry the plan are actually aligned with it. If those links remain hazy, then the strategy is still being admired from a distance instead of prepared for execution.

The closing pressure is follow-through. A strategy review does not prove itself by sounding rigorous in the meeting. It proves itself by sharpening what happens afterward. That is why follow-through belongs inside the chapter's logic instead of after it. If no harder questions survive into clearer action, stronger accountability, or a narrower plan, then the review was mostly performance. The real test is whether the conversation changed the strategy's chances of being carried. That is the bridge to Chapter 9. Once the review has exposed what the strategy really requires, the next task is to synchronize the plan through the operations process.
