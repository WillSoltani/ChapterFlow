Before most conversations have a chance to prove anything, people have already started deciding what you are likely to cost them. Safe or risky. Warm or cold. Easy to approach or easier to avoid. That is the uncomfortable starting point of Schafer's second chapter. First impressions arrive early, and they do not wait for your best sentence.

His argument is not that words do not matter. It is that words often arrive after the first filter has already formed. The person across from you has already read your posture, your face, your pace, and the general feeling you bring into the space. If those cues suggest tension, arrogance, threat, or indifference, you begin the interaction from a worse position than you realize. If they suggest warmth and safety, the interaction gets more room to work.

That is where Schafer introduces what he calls friend signals. These are small nonverbal cues that communicate low threat and approachability before a full conversation takes shape. The chapter keeps them simple on purpose. An eyebrow flash works as a quick sign of recognition and nonhostility. A head tilt softens the impression of guardedness and can read as trust rather than challenge. A real smile does similar work by lowering the emotional temperature of the exchange. Open posture reinforces the same message. None of these cues create closeness on their own. Their job is narrower. They help another person decide that continuing the interaction is unlikely to go badly.

That narrowness matters because it keeps the chapter honest. A lot of body-language advice collapses into performance. It promises magnetism, dominance, or near-magical control over the room. Schafer's version is less dramatic and more useful. He is not saying one smile makes people love you. He is saying first impressions often turn on whether you seem safe enough to engage. That is a smaller promise, but it is also the one that holds up better in ordinary life.

The chapter gains force by tying those cues to the cost of a negative first impression. Once someone has read you in a negative way, they are less inclined to offer the extra contact that could revise that judgment. A bad opening shrinks your chance to recover because the other person may avoid a second look entirely. That is why first impressions matter so much here. They do not settle the whole relationship, but they often determine whether the relationship gets enough daylight to grow.

This also clarifies what friend signals are not. They are not substitutes for warmth, listening, or substance. They are not the whole of trust. They do not excuse later coldness, self-absorption, or fake charm. In fact, overplaying them probably creates the opposite problem. If the signal feels too managed, too eager, or too polished for the moment, it can stop reading as safety and start reading as performance. The chapter's real lesson is not to memorize a trick. It is to understand what another person is scanning for before they invest attention in you.

That makes Chapter 2 a bridge chapter in the best sense. Chapter 1 explained how relationships grow once contact starts accumulating through proximity, frequency, duration, and intensity. Chapter 2 focuses on the opening seconds that make that accumulation possible. Before any formula can work, someone has to decide that staying in contact with you is a better bet than stepping away. Friend signals lower that first barrier.

From there the book can move into a deeper rule. Once the door opens, approachability is no longer enough. You still have to make the interaction feel good for the other person. That is why Chapter 3 turns toward the golden rule of friendship. The opening signal gets you invited in. What you do next determines whether the invitation lasts.
