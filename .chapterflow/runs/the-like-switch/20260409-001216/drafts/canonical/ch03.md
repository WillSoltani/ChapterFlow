Most people assume being liked depends on how impressive they seem. They try to sound smart, interesting, accomplished, or unusually charming. Schafer's golden rule starts from a different question. When someone leaves an interaction with you, how do they feel about themselves?

His answer is blunt enough to remember because it is useful: if you want people to like you, make them feel good about themselves. That line can sound obvious until you notice how rarely people really follow it. In most conversations, attention keeps snapping back toward the self. What should I say next? Did I sound good enough? How do I compare? Do they like me yet? Schafer's point is that this self-monitoring quietly ruins the very effect people want. The more trapped you are in your own ego, the less able you are to create a good emotional experience for the other person.

That is why the chapter treats ego override as a real skill. You do not make people feel good about themselves by reciting compliments on command. You do it by turning your attention outward long enough to register who they are, what matters to them, and what kind of response actually makes them feel more comfortable, seen, or understood. The rule is not empty praise. It is focused responsiveness.

This distinction matters because flattery is a cheap imitation of the same move. Mechanical praise can sound like attention, but it usually feels like an attempt to force a result. Real attention works differently. It leaves the other person feeling more present in the conversation and more at ease inside themselves. That good feeling then starts to attach to you, not because you hypnotized them, but because you became part of the interaction that produced it.

That also explains why the chapter works beyond first meetings. In Chapter 2, friend signals lowered the barrier to contact by making you seem safe to approach. Here the question changes. Once the door opens, what makes someone want more of the interaction rather than less? Schafer's answer is that people return to those who make them feel better about themselves in a sincere, responsive way. That is what turns approachability into liking.

The rule is simple, but it is not soft. It asks for discipline. You may want to prove yourself, win the room, tell the better story, or show how much you know. Schafer argues that these impulses usually work against the deeper goal. People are less drawn to the person who seems most eager to shine than to the person who reliably leaves them feeling more capable, more interesting, more understood, or more comfortable. The emotional residue matters more than the self-display.

Still, the chapter needs a limit, and it knows it. Making people feel good about themselves is not the same as agreeing with everything they say, flattering them recklessly, or disappearing as a person. If the attention feels fake, overdone, or strategically timed, the rule breaks. The point is not to become a mirror with no judgment. The point is to stop making the interaction unnecessarily about your own ego.

That tension is what keeps the golden rule from turning into a cheesy social trick. It works because the attention is real enough to improve the other person's experience of themselves, not because you performed appreciation like a script. Once that pattern is in place, attraction can deepen more broadly. That is where the next chapter goes. After the good feeling, what larger forces make attraction and attachment compound over time?
