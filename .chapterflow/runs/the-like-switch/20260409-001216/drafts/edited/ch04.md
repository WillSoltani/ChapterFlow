Attraction is often talked about as if it were a mysterious force that either appears or does not. Schafer moves in a more practical direction. After Chapter 3 explained how to make another person feel good about themselves in the moment, Chapter 4 widens the frame and asks what makes that good feeling deepen over time. His answer is not a single trick. Attraction grows when contact repeats, when interest is reciprocated, when self-disclosure is paced well, and when the relationship becomes a place of real support.

The first force is repeated contact. People often become closer to those they cross paths with regularly because recurring contact keeps the relationship alive long enough to gather momentum. Neighbors, coworkers, classmates, and fellow group members get more chances to build familiarity. That alone does not guarantee attraction, but it increases the odds that attraction can start. Without repeated contact, many promising interactions never get enough room to develop.

Still, exposure is not the whole mechanism. A person can see the same classmate every week and never become meaningfully closer. Schafer pushes on reciprocity because attraction deepens when interest moves in both directions. The relationship has to stop feeling one-sided. Each person has to show some willingness to respond, invest, and return the contact. Repeated meetings without reciprocity create familiarity, but they do not reliably create closeness.

That leads into self-disclosure. As people move from acquaintanceship toward friendship, they begin sharing more of themselves. Schafer's point is not that disclosure should become immediate or dramatic. The chapter works only when disclosure is calibrated to the level of trust already present. Too little disclosure can keep the relationship shallow, but too much too soon can create pressure instead of intimacy. Attraction deepens when openness arrives at a pace the relationship can actually hold.

Support is the next force, and it matters because attraction is partly about who you get to be around another person. People are drawn toward relationships where they feel understood, backed up, and more fully themselves. That is why supportive responsiveness matters more than abstract compatibility talk. If repeated contact and reciprocal disclosure are present, but the relationship does not feel supportive, attraction can stall. The bond lasts better when the other person confirms a valued version of who you are.

This is also where the chapter becomes more demanding than a simple "be around people more" rule. Frequency helps, but quality still decides. A relationship weakens when support is one-sided, when one person keeps disclosing while the other stays closed, or when openness becomes so intense that it feels unstable. Attraction needs rhythm and proportion. The same behavior that deepens a bond in one stage can strain it in another.

That makes Chapter 4 a natural extension of Chapter 3. First you create a good emotional experience in the interaction. Then the relationship has to keep earning itself through repeated rewarding contact, reciprocity, and paced openness. Attraction is not only about first impressions or chemistry language. It is about whether the structure of the relationship keeps making closeness easier rather than harder.

The preview into Chapter 5 follows directly from that point. If attraction grows through repeated contact, reciprocity, disclosure, and support, then the next question is behavioral. What do people actually say and do that keeps those forces moving in a healthy direction? The next chapter can narrow from broad attraction laws into the conversation habits that make them work in real life.
