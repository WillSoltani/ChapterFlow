Chapter Promise:
Show how people decide whether you feel safe, warm, and worth engaging before the conversation even gets going.

Opening Move:
Open on the discomfort of being judged before you have really spoken, then reveal that Schafer treats those first seconds as readable nonverbal signaling.

Anchor Allocation:
- anchor 1 used in paragraph(s): P1, P2
- anchor 2 used in paragraph(s): P2, P3
- anchor 3 used in paragraph(s): P3, P4

Paragraph Job Map:
- P1: Show that first impressions form quickly and stick.
- P2: Introduce friend signals as low-threat nonverbal cues.
- P3: Explain eyebrow flash, head tilt, smile, and open posture as approachability signals.
- P4: Show the cost of a negative first impression and why it is hard to reverse.
- P5: Draw the limit: cues open the door but do not create deep trust.
- P6: Bridge into Chapter 3 by showing that once contact begins, the deeper rule becomes making others feel good about themselves.

Required Distinction or Mechanism:
The key distinction is between attracting approach and earning closeness. Friend signals reduce threat and invite interaction; they do not complete the relationship.

Hard-Depth Tension:
Hard mode must keep both truths in view: first impressions are powerful and sticky, but overplaying cues or treating them as a mask creates shallow, brittle rapport.

Threshold Question:
How much can you shape a first impression before the effort turns from respectful signaling into performance that other people eventually feel?

Takeaway Count Lock:
- Easy: 3 (always)
- Medium: 5
- Hard: 5

Hard Takeaway Topics:
- Takeaway 1: first impressions form quickly and are sticky
- Takeaway 2: friend signals reduce perceived threat
- Takeaway 3: eyebrow flash, head tilt, and smile each do a distinct job
- Takeaway 4: negative first impressions are costly because they reduce second chances
- Takeaway 5: cues can open a door but cannot replace substance

- Scenario 5 (dilemma, school): decide how to recover after landing badly in a first meeting
- Scenario 6 (before_after, personal): show how the same words land differently with different nonverbal cues
